98. To N. A. Nekrasov. 1855. April 30. Sevastopol.

Most Gracious Sir Nikolai Alexeyevich,

You must already have received my article “Sevastopol in December”[^1] and the promise of Stolypin's article. Here it is.[^2] Despite the wild orthography of this manuscript, which you yourself will arrange to have corrected, if it is printed without censorship cuts, which the author tried with all his might to avoid, you will agree, I hope, that we have either very few such military articles or none at all printed, unfortunately. Perhaps with this same courier Sacken's article will be sent,[^3] about which I say nothing and which, I hope, you will not print. The corrections in Stolypin's article were made in black ink by Khrulev,[^4] with his left hand, because his right is wounded. Stolypin asks that they be placed in footnotes. Please, if possible, put both mine and Stolypin's in the July issue. Now we are all assembled, and the literary society of the fallen journal is beginning to organize itself, and, as I wrote you, every month you will receive from me two, 3, or 4 articles of contemporary military content. The two best contributors, Bakunin[^5] and Rostovtsev,[^6] have not yet managed to finish their articles. Be so kind as to answer me and in general to write with this courier, Gorchakov's adjutant, and with those to come, who are constantly shuttling from you to here. Then, with perfect respect, I have the honor to be your obedient servant.

Count L. Tolstoy.

Sevastopol, 1855, Apr. 30.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the IRLI. First published in: I. I. Panaev, *Literary Memoirs with an Appendix of Letters from Various Persons*. St. Petersburg, 1888, pp. 414–415.

## Notes

[^1]: Work on “Sevastopol in December” proceeded from 20 March to 25 April. The latter date stands at the end of the printed text. Judging by the opening words of this letter, the story was sent to Nekrasov between 25 and 30 April.
[^2]: A. D. Stolypin's article “A Night Sortie in Sevastopol. The Account of a Participant” was printed in no. 7 of *The Contemporary* for 1855 with an editorial note: “We owe the communication of this article to Mr. L. N. T.” On Arkady Dmitrievich Stolypin see note 8 to letter no. 90.
[^3]: Baron (from 10 August 1855, Count) Dmitry Erofeyevich Osten-Sacken (1789–1881) entered service as a cadet in 1804 and took part in every campaign from that of 1805–1807 through the Eastern War of 1853–1856. As chief of the Sevastopol garrison, Osten-Sacken took an active part in the defense. In the soldiers' song about the battle of 4 August composed by Tolstoy, D. E. Osten-Sacken is described: “And there General Sacken kept reading akathists to the Mother of God.” Count D. E. Osten-Sacken wrote a number of articles on military affairs and memoir sketches. On him see the *Russian Biographical Dictionary* and M. Nechkina's article “On Pushkin, the Decembrists, and Their Common Friends,” *Katorga i ssylka*, 1930, book 4 (65).
[^4]: Stepan Alexandrovich Khrulev (1807–1870), a participant in the Polish campaign of 1830–1831, the Hungarian campaign of 1849, and the Ak-Mechet expedition of 1853. The honor of capturing the fortress of Ak-Mechet (“Fort Perovsky”) belongs to Khrulev. At the beginning of 1854 Khrulev arrived on the Danube and was placed at the disposal of the chief engineer Schilder. Here, and later in Sevastopol, where from December 1854 he served under the commander-in-chief, Khrulev proved himself an outstanding military commander. The troops were very fond of him and called him “the brave soldiers' general.” Assigned to the most dangerous places, Khrulev, who never lost his presence of mind, inspired the soldiers by personal example to the most desperate undertakings. Wounded on the day Sevastopol was surrendered, 27 August 1855, he was released to Petersburg for treatment in November; two months later he was appointed to the disposal of the commander-in-chief of the Separate Caucasian Corps, and from 1862 until his death he was listed with the reserve troops.
[^5]: Alexander Alexandrovich Bakunin (born 10 November 1821, died 1908), brother of the famous revolutionary, the fifth son of Alexander Mikhailovich Bakunin (176...–1854) and Varvara Alexandrovna Muravyova (1791–1864). He graduated from Moscow University. In March 1854 he entered the Tobolsk Regiment as a cadet, with which he went through the Eastern campaign, first in Romania and then in Sevastopol, where he became acquainted with Tolstoy. On this acquaintance see the account by A. A. Bakunin's widow, Elizaveta Alexandrovna Bakunina, in *Golos minuvshego*, 1916, no. 11, pp. 201–203. Bakunin later became a prominent zemstvo figure and a participant in the movement for the liberation of Italy; “to the end of his life he retained physical vigor and spiritual cheerfulness, a thinker who worked out his own original philosophical system.” (N. P. Chulkov, “The Genealogy of the Decembrist Muravyovs,” *Russian Eugenics Journal*, vol. V, issue I, Moscow, 1927, p. 15.)
[^6]: Nikolai Yakovlevich Rostovtsev. On him see note 10 to letter no. 90. I. I. Panaev wrote in reply on 31 May. We reproduce his letter in full.
