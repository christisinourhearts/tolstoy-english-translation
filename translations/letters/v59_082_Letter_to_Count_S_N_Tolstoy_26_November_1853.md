\* 82. To Count S. N. Tolstoy. 1853. November 26. Starogladkovskaya Stanitsa.

Seryozha!

Someone absolutely must attend to my papers in the Heraldry Department, but since I have lost sight of my Petersburg acquaintances, you write to someone to find out why no action has been taken on the communication of the Inspectorate Department of the War Ministry to the Department of Heraldry, dated 24 July 1852, No. 8190, with which the papers of Count Lev Tolstoy were enclosed for examination, and try to hasten this examination.[^1] —

<!-- vol. 59, p. 251 --> I have no time to write because I am drinking tea. But in any case I am alive and well, which I wish for you too.

L. Tolstoy.

Out of modesty I must add that I arrived at composing letters of this sort not through my own reflection but by following the example of my elder brothers.

26 November 1853.

Please write about my papers as soon as possible. It is necessary. — When shall I come? God alone knows, because it will soon be a year that I have been thinking of nothing but how to sheathe my sword, and I cannot. — But since I am compelled to fight somewhere or other, I find it more pleasant to fight in Turkey than here, about which I asked Prince Sergei Dmitrievich, who wrote me that he had already written to his brother, but did not know what would come of it.[^2] —

In any case by the New Year I expect a change in my way of life, which, I confess, has become unbearably tiresome to me. — Stupid officers, stupid conversations, stupid officers, stupid conversations, nothing else. If only there were one person with whom one could talk from the heart. Turgenev is right: “what irony all by oneself” — one becomes perceptibly stupid oneself.

Despite the fact that Nikolenka, God knows why, took away the hounds[^3] (Yepishka and I often call him a swine for it), I spend whole days from morning till evening hunting alone with a pointing dog. And this alone is both pleasure and not pleasure, but rather a means of stupefying oneself. You wear yourself out, get hungry, come home and fall asleep as if dead — and the day is gone. If there is an opportunity, or if you yourself are in Moscow, buy me Dickens (*David Copperfield*)[^4] in English and send me also Sadler's English lexicon,[^5] which is among my books. I am very glad you got rid of the manager,[^6] I hinted at this to Valerian too, though weakly, because I saw that he was of the opposite opinion. I have asked you several times to send your portrait. If I were in your place, I would fulfill such a request at once. Try having some Caucasian thing sent to me for you. — Is it true that Cherkassky cut his throat?[^7] And if it is true, how and why? If you continue to manage Yasnaya rather than Valerian (which, of course, <!-- vol. 59, p. 252 --> depends on you yourselves), then take from him the memorandum[^8] I wrote him and, above all, take note of the foals and of regularly sending me 100 rubles every two months. — I only the other day received in Kizlyar a letter in which you write about sending me 150 rubles, which I had already received in Pyatigorsk at the beginning of October; but if those you write about are another sum, then count this money as though it were the 100 rubles for November and December, and for January and February send me only 50 rubles.[^9]

If a steam coffee-maker costs no more than 4 silver rubles, have one bought and sent. In all purchases for me take into account that I am a man on campaign and therefore an important merit in things for me is that they should not be bulky and should pack easily. Deduct the cost of the purchases from my two-month allowance.

## Notes

[^1]: This evidently concerns the papers connected with Tolstoy's examination for an officer's rank. In his diary under 15 November he wrote: “Received ... a paper saying that my documents are being held up in the Heraldry Department.”
[^2]: The idea of transferring to the active army arose in Tolstoy at the end of September 1853. Under 28 September he wrote in his diary: “Beginning to think about the Turkish campaign”; under 2 October: “I want to write a letter to Prince Andrei Ivanovich and Sergei Dmitrievich”; under 6 October: “The idea of a transfer came. Wrote letters and a memorandum.” The letters and memorandum were sent to Prince S. D. Gorchakov, who replied on 17 October: “Dear Count Lev Nikolaevich. Today I received your letter from Kizlyar and with it the memorandum about your transfer to the active army. My letter to my brother Mikhail has already been written and will go on the 19th with your memorandum — what will come of it I do not know, but it was written as to a relative. God grant that it succeeds. I do not understand one thing: why are you not a junker, but a fireworker, and only 4th class at that; as soon as I receive anything about you from my brother I shall certainly inform you, and until then I wish you health, patience, and happiness. Prince S. Gorchakov. All mine send greetings and wish to see you. —” (Letter unpublished; original in the ATB.) Mikhail — Prince Mikhail Dmitrievich Gorchakov. On him see the introductory note to letter no. 81. On Prince Sergei Dmitrievich Gorchakov see note 1 to letter no. 7.
[^3]: Having retired, Count N. N. Tolstoy left Pyatigorsk for Tula Province on 1 September 1853.
[^4]: Dickens's novel *David Copperfield* was published in 1849.
[^5]: By “Sadler's lexicon” Tolstoy evidently means: Muret-Sanders, *Enzyklopädisches Englisch-Deutsches und Deutsch-Englisches Wörterbuch*.
[^6]: Andrei Ilyich Sobolev.
[^7]: Prince Konstantin Alexandrovich Cherkassky (1819–1853), elder brother of the well-known public figure Prince Vladimir Alexandrovich Cherkassky, lost his entire fortune through gambling and unsuccessful ventures. For the last five or six years of his life he lived on an estate in Venev District, Tula Province. Count M. D. Buturlin, who knew him well, writes of his death in his memoirs: “The circumstances accompanying his death in 1855 or 1856 are covered in a certain mystery. Some said that, when preparing to go to dinner at his neighbor Norov's, while shaving he cut a pimple on his lip, which supposedly caused inflammation and gangrene with incredible speed, and he died two days later. Others maintained that, having grown tired of life, he slashed his own throat with a razor.” (*Russky Arkhiv*, 1897, III, p. 558.)
[^8]: a memorandum.
[^9]: Tolstoy went to Kizlyar on 20 November. In his diary under 22 November he wrote: “Received a short letter from Seryozha in which he writes about sending some 150 silver rubles, which, however, I have not received.” Count S. N. Tolstoy's letter is unknown.
