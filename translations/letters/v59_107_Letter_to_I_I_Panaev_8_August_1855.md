107. To I. I. Panaev. 1855. August 8. Bakhchisarai.

Most Gracious Sir

Ivan Ivanovich.

I received your letters 1 and hasten to answer, especially the last. 2 I thank you very much for your efforts to defend *A Spring Night* from the censorship; please strike things out, even soften them, but for God's sake do not add anything; that would upset me very much. L. N. T., 3 I can assure you, has not a hair's breadth of authorial vanity, but he would like always to remain faithful to one direction and point of view in literature. — I seem to have promised you Bakunin imprudently; all this time he has been too occupied with his service, and now he is wounded. Rostovtsev keeps promising and is lazy. Please tell N. A. Nekrasov that I received the money for *Sevastopol in December* 4 and his letter, to which I ask him to forgive me for not yet having had time to reply. For *Youth*, he writes, you have prepared a place in September. 5 Unfortunately, I cannot send it to you before the middle of September, but certainly, if only I am healthy and alive, I shall send it by then. Stolypin has already begun a story of the recent affair, 6 and perhaps I shall too. —

Then, with perfect respect, I have the honor to be your obedient servant,

Count L. Tolstoy.

8 August 1855.

Bakhchisarai.

Would you be so kind as to take upon yourself the trouble of several money commissions in Petersburg? You would oblige me exceedingly.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the IRLI. First published by M. A. Tsyavlovsky in *Krasnaya nov*, 1928, no. 9, p. 222.

2. The letter is a reply to I. I. Panaev's letter of 18 July, which we reproduce in full:

3. “I thank you beyond words, Lev Nikolaevich, for your story *A Spring Night in Sevastopol*. I have just received and read it. You are right: this story is incomparably better than the first, but it will be liked less, for the reason that its hero is truth, and truth pricks the eyes; naked truth is not loved, people are not accustomed to truth without adornment. I shall do everything I can to defend it from the censorship. The impression of the story is heavy (ah, how we are not <!-- vol. 59, p. 329 --> accustomed to truth!) — and it would be necessary to add something at the end, saying that after all Sevastopol and the Russian people and so on, for the censorship, although this would be rather banal; but I have softened and smoothed a few things, without spoiling the essence of the story and foreseeing a struggle with the censorship over it. This was necessary. I am writing to you through Stolypin. I am surprised that you did not receive my letter by post — in reply to the story *The Wood-Felling* that you sent — a delightful story, which will be in the September issue. What about Rostovtsev and Bakunin? Encourage them and send stories for the following issues. You cannot imagine with what eagerness all Russia reads them. Turgenev sends you his regards. He writes to me that your dedication is both pleasant and flattering to him. Write to me — I place all my hopes on you and am sure that you will send both your own stories and encourage your collaborators. Everyone waits in the journal with terrible impatience for the letters L. N. T. — this is not a compliment, but the same naked truth that is the hero of your story, although this truth needs not the slightest adornment. It is pleasant even naked. I am sending you herewith the announcement of *The Contemporary*, from which you will see that now you need not constrain yourself in your stories, since *The Contemporary* has received permission to print even military news. May this encourage you and your collaborators! I have answered and shall answer your letters punctually. It is a pity if they are lost in the post. Yours sincerely devoted, Iv. Panaev.

4. Forgive me for scribbling so. — I am in a hurry, because I must leave on business this very second.” (*Krasnaya nov*, 1928, no. 9, pp. 220–221.)

5. Truth — in Tolstoy's text, “Sevastopol in May” ends with the words: “The hero of my story, however, whom I love with all the powers of my soul, whom I have tried to reproduce in all his beauty, who always was, is, and will be beautiful, is truth.” In *The Contemporary* (the September issue for 1855), “Sevastopol in May” ends with words added by Panaev: “But it was not we who began this war, not we who provoked this terrible bloodshed. We are only defending our native hearth, our native land, and shall defend it to the last drop of blood....” Arkady Dmitrievich Stolypin was sent on 7 July by the commander of the troops, Prince M. D. Gorchakov, to Petersburg with a letter to Alexander II. Panaev's letter replying to Tolstoy's “The Wood-Felling” has not survived. On Tolstoy's request to dedicate “The Wood-Felling” to Turgenev, see letter no. 101. Nekrasov wrote about this to Turgenev at Spasskoye (in a letter of 30 June–1 July): “Tolstoy has dedicated to you the story *Junker*, which he sent to *The Contemporary*” (Pypin, *Nekrasov*, St Petersburg, 1905, p. 130), to which Ivan Sergeyevich replied to Panaev on 10 July: “I am very flattered by his wish to dedicate his new story to me” (Panaev, *Literary Recollections*, 1883, p. 401).
