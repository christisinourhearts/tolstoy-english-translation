\* 39. To T. A. Yergolskaya. 1851. March 8. Moscow.

March 8.

Dear aunt!

From your silence I think that you are angry with me; indeed it has been a long time since I wrote to you. But you know <!-- vol. 59, p. 90 --> that if I happen to be at fault toward you, it is very much against my will; you also know that the longer I remain without seeing you, the more vivid my feeling for you becomes. — I slandered myself when I said that the absent are in the wrong. —

Recently, in a work I was reading, the author said that the first signs of spring usually act upon people's moral state. — With nature coming back to life, one would like to feel oneself reborn too; one regrets the past, time badly used, repents of one's weaknesses, and the future appears to us like a luminous point before you; one becomes better, morally better.

As far as I am concerned, this is perfectly true. Ever since I began [to] live (independently), spring has always put me into good dispositions, in which I persevered for a longer or shorter time; but winter is always a stumbling block for me. — I always go astray. —

For that matter, if I sum up the winters past, this one is undoubtedly the most pleasant and the most sensible that I have spent. — I amused myself, went into society, kept pleasant memories, and at the same time I did not disorder my finances—nor put them in order, it is true. Just now I made a calculation of the debts I have, and it turns out that the figure is the same as the year before.

Goodbye, dear and good aunt, I kiss your hands a thousand times and hope soon to do so in reality.

Tell Valerian[^1] that I cannot answer him anything, since it has been a long time since I saw the Prince; he has just lost his daughter. 2 — However, today itself I am going to dine with them and I shall speak with him.

Leon.

On the envelope:

To Her High Nobility Tatyana Alexandrovna Yergolskaya. Tula. Chern.

March 8.

Dear auntie!

It seems to me from your silence that you are angry with me, and indeed I have not written to you for a long time; but you know that whenever I am at fault toward you it is always against my will; you know that the longer I do not see you, the more vivid my feeling for you becomes. — I slandered myself when I said that those who are absent are always to blame.

<!-- vol. 59, p. 91 --> I recently read in a book that the first signs of spring usually act upon the moral side of a person. — With nature coming to life one wants to be reborn oneself, one regrets the past, badly used time, repents of one's weaknesses, and the future appears bright ahead; one becomes better, morally better. —

With respect to me this is perfectly true; ever since I began to live (independently), spring has always brought me into a good state, in which I remained for a longer or shorter time, but winter has always been a stumbling block. — *I always go astray.* —

However, if I recall past winters, it must be admitted that this one was the most pleasant and most sensible of those I have spent. — I amused myself, went into society, retained many pleasant impressions, and did not upset my financial affairs. To tell the truth, I did not put them in order either. Just now I added up my debts, and it turns out that the sum is the same as last year.

Goodbye, dear and good auntie, I kiss your hands a thousand times and hope soon to do this in reality.

Tell Valerian[^2] that I cannot answer him anything because I have not seen the prince for a long time, and his daughter has just died.[^3] — However, I am dining with them today and will speak with him. Lev.
