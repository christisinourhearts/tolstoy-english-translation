\* 103. To Count S. N. Tolstoy. 1855. July 3. Position on the Belbek River.

To Lieutenant Count Tolstoy of the Rifle Regiment of the Imperial Family

From the Chief of the Mountain Artillery of the Southern Army and of the Naval and Land Forces stationed in the Crimea, Artillery Second Lieutenant and Knight Count Tolstoy

Report.

In consequence of the prolonged silence of Your Excellency and my Excellency, I have the honor most respectfully to report that this was mutually very stupid, and that for my part I intend to correct this error and henceforth not fall into it, which I most humbly request of Your Excellency as well. —

Chief of the Mountain Artillery, Second Lieutenant

Count Tolstoy.

No. 54

3 July 1855.

Position at the Belbek River.

I had intended not to write anything more, merely to find out where you are (although I know that you were in Suzdal, but were supposed to transfer to Petersburg, while you yourself wanted to go to Pirogovo), <!-- vol. 59, p. 321 --> but then I imagined how angry you would get and how several times you would call me “the most trifling fellow”; but chiefly I myself wanted to tell you and ask you a few things, although I greatly doubt whether and when this letter will reach you. Although you surely know through our family[^1] where I have been and what I have been doing, I shall repeat to you my adventures since Kishinev, all the more because perhaps the way I tell them will interest you, and thereby you will learn what phase I am in — since evidently it is my fate always to be in some phase or other. From Kishinev on 1 November I asked to go to the Crimea, partly in order to see this war, partly in order to get away from Serzhputovsky's Staff, which I disliked, and above all from patriotism, which at that time, I confess, came over me strongly. — In the Crimea I asked to go nowhere in particular, but left my fate to the authorities; I was attached to a battery in Sevastopol itself, where I spent a month very pleasantly among simple, good comrades, who are always especially good during real war and danger. In December our battery was withdrawn toward Simferopol, and there I spent 1 1/2 months in a comfortable landowner's house, went to Simferopol to dance and play pianos with young ladies, and went hunting wild goats on Chatyr-Dag[^2] with officials. In January there was another reshuffling of officers, and I was transferred to a battery stationed in the mountains 10 versts from Sevastopol on the Belbek.[^3] There I made the acquaintance of Kuzma's mother,[^4] the nastiest little circle of Poles in the battery, a commander who, though a good man, was a greasy, coarse creature, no conveniences, cold in the dugouts.[^5] Not a single book, not a single person with whom one could talk. And it was there that I received 1,500 rubles for the journal, which had already been abandoned, and there that I lost 2,500 rubles at cards, thereby proving to the whole world that I was still a trifling fellow; although the preceding circumstances can be accepted as mitigating circumstances,[^6] it is still very, very bad. In March it grew warmer, and a dear, excellent man, Bronevsky, arrived at the battery; I began to come to my senses, and on 1 April the battery went into Sevastopol during the bombardment itself, and I came completely to my senses. There until 15 May, although in serious danger, that is, on duty 4 days out of every 8 <!-- vol. 59, p. 322 --> at the battery of the 4th bastion, still the spring and weather were excellent; there was a mass of impressions and people, all the conveniences of life, and a splendid circle of decent people gathered around us, so that those 1 1/2 months will remain among my pleasantest memories. On 15 May it occurred to Gorchakov or to the Chief of Artillery to entrust me with forming and commanding a Mountain Platoon on the Belbek — 20 versts from Sevastopol — with which I have so far been extremely pleased in many respects. There is the general description for you; in the next letter I shall write more fully about the present. —

Please never trouble yourself about my address — always Headquarters of the Crimean Army and nothing more. — About yourself, besides whatever occurs to you, please write me two things. 1) Why and for what reason did you enter the service, and are you satisfied with it, and 2) What did you decide about Masha.[^7] Yesterday Fersen,[^8] who is with the prince on special assignments, spent the night with me and told me a little about you. —

[^2]: Chatyr-Dag — from the Tatar “tent-mountain.” One of the highest peaks of the Crimean mountains (height 5,003 feet), situated on the southeastern shore of the peninsula.
[^3]: On this see note 4 to letter no. 95.

[^5]: On the commander and officers of the battery Tolstoy recorded in his diary under 23 January: “Filimonov, in whose battery I am, is the greasiest creature one can imagine. A letter from his daughter survives in the ATB; in the 1890s she wrote Tolstoy that he had remained indebted to her father, as the latter had told her.
