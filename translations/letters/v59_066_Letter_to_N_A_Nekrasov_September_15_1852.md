66. To N. A. Nekrasov. 1852. September 15. Starogladkovskaya stanitsa.

Dear Sir.

I was very pleased by the favorable opinion you expressed of my novel, all the more because it was the first opinion of it that I had heard, and because that opinion was, precisely, yours. Despite this, I repeat the request I made to you in my first letter: value the manuscript, send me the money it is worth in your opinion, or tell me plainly that it is worth nothing. —

The autobiographical form I have adopted and the forced connection of the subsequent parts with the preceding one constrain me so much <!-- vol. 59, p. 203 --> that I often feel a desire to abandon them and leave the first without a continuation. —

In any event, if the continuation is finished, and as soon as it is finished, I shall send it to you. — Awaiting your reply, with true respect, I have the honor to be,

Dear Sir,

Your obedient servant,

L. N.

Address: Town of Kizlyar. To Count Nikolai Nikolaevich Tolstoy, to be handed to L. N.

15 September

1852.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the LB. First printed by N. S. Ashukin in AK, pp. 186—187.

2. Tolstoy's letter is a reply to an undated letter from Nekrasov from the first days of August 1852:

3. “Dear Sir! I have read your manuscript (*Childhood*). It contains enough interest that I shall print it. Not knowing the continuation, I cannot say anything decisively, but it seems to me that its author has talent. In any event, the author's direction, the simplicity and reality of the content constitute an inalienable merit of this work. If in the later parts (as one should expect) there is rather more liveliness and movement, it will be a good novel. I ask you to send me the continuation. Both your novel and your talent have interested me. I would also advise you not to hide behind initials but to begin publishing directly under your surname. Unless, that is, you are only a chance guest in literature. I await your reply. Accept the assurance of my true respect. N. Nekrasov.” (*Literary Supplements to Niva*, 1898, no. 2 [February], p. 340.)

4. On receiving this letter Tolstoy recorded in his diary under August 29: “... received a letter from Petersburg from the editor, which delighted me to the point of stupidity. Not a word about money. Tomorrow write letters: to Nekrasov...” But on August 30 Tolstoy did not write to Nekrasov, and only under September 5 did he record in the diary: “Wrote a letter to Nekrasov.” This is, of course, the letter dated September 15 on the letter itself. Meanwhile Nekrasov, not having received an answer from Tolstoy to his first letter, sent a second one dated September 5, 1852:

5. “Dear Sir! I wrote to you about your story; but now I consider it my duty to say a few more words to you about it. I sent it to typesetting for book IX of *Sovremennik* and, having read it attentively in proof rather than in the blindly written manuscript, found that this story is much better than it seemed to me the first time. I can say positively that the author has talent. For you, as a beginner, conviction of this, <!-- vol. 59, p. 204 --> I think, is the most important thing at present. The issue of *Sovremennik* with your story will appear in Petersburg tomorrow, while it will probably reach you (I shall send it to your address) no earlier than in about three weeks. Some things have been omitted from it (not much, however)... Nothing has been added. I shall write you more fully soon, but now I have no time. I await your reply and ask you—if you have a continuation—to send it to me. N. Nekrasov.

6. P. S. Although I have guessed, nevertheless I ask you to tell me definitely the name of the author of the story. I need to know this—also because of the rules of our censorship.” (*Literary Supplements to Niva*, 1898, no. 2, pp. 340—341.)

7. On the text of *Childhood* in book IX of *Sovremennik*, see the letters of November 18 and 27, 1852.

8. Having received this letter, Tolstoy recorded in his diary on September 30: “received a letter from Nekrasov—praise, but no money.”

9. Nekrasov did not answer Tolstoy's letter of September 15 until October 30, in a letter of the following content: “Dear Sir! I ask you to excuse me for delaying my reply to your last letter—I was very busy. As for the question of money, I kept silent about it in my previous letters for the following reason: in our best journals there has long existed a custom not to pay a beginning author for the first story with which a journal first recommends him to the public. All those who up to now began their literary career in *Sovremennik* have been subject to this custom, such as Goncharov, Druzhinin, Avdeev, and others. My own first works and Panaev's were likewise subject to this custom in their time. I offer you the same, on condition that for your further works I shall directly assign you the best payment received by our most famous (very few) writers of fiction, that is, 50 silver rubles per printed sheet. I delayed writing to you also because I could not make this offer to you earlier without testing my impression by the judgment of the public: that judgment proved as favorable to you as could be, and I am very glad that I was not mistaken in my opinion of your first work, and with pleasure now offer you the terms written above.

10. Write to me about this. In any event I can assure you that we shall come to terms in this respect; since your story was a success, it would be very pleasant for us to have your second work as soon as possible. Do us the favor of sending what you have ready. I wanted to send you no. IX of *Sovremennik*, but unfortunately forgot to arrange for extra copies to be printed, and our entire journal for this year has been distributed. However, if you need it, I can send you one or two offprints of your story alone, pieced together from defective sheets.

11. I repeat my most humble request that you send us a story or something in the nature of a story, novel, or tale, and remain awaiting your reply. At your service, N. Nekrasov.

12. P. S. We are obliged to know the name of every author whose works we print, and therefore give me definite information on this point. If you wish, no one except us will know it.” (*Literary Supplements to Niva*, 1898, no. 2, pp. 341—342.)

13. Ivan Aleksandrovich Goncharov (1814—1891) entered the literary field <!-- vol. 59, p. 205 --> with *A Common Story*, printed in *Sovremennik* in 1847, nos. III and IV; Aleksandr Vasilyevich Druzhinin (1824—1864), with the story *Polinka Saks*, printed in *Sovremennik* in 1847, no. XII. Mikhail Vasilyevich Avdeev (1821—1876) became known as the author of the novel *Tamarin*, parts of which were printed in *Sovremennik* in 1849—1851.
