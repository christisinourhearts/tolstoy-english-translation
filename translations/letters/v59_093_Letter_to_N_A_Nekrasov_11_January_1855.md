93. To N. A. Nekrasov. 1855. January 11. Eski-Orda.

Most Gracious Sir

Nikolai Alexeyevich!

In my previous letter[^1] I wrote to you in passing about the materials that had accumulated with me for the military journal that was supposed to be published with the army, and which — the materials — I offered you for publication in your journal; now I shall speak about this subject in more detail. In the failure of this Journal I regret not so much the wasted labor and materials as the idea of the journal, which is worth realizing, if only in part, since it proved impossible to realize it fully.

The fundamental idea of this journal was that if not the greater part, then certainly more than half of the reading public consists of military men, while we have no military literature except official military literature, which for some reason does not enjoy the confidence of the public and therefore can neither give direction to nor express the direction of our military society. We wanted to found a sheet accessible in price and content to all classes of military society, which, avoiding any collision with the military-official journals existing among us, would serve only as an expression of the spirit of the army. —

The journal would have been divided into an official and an unofficial section. In the first would be placed: 1) Reports of military events from all theaters of war; 2) Orders concerning rewards for merits falling outside the ordinary class of distinctions; 3) sentences of military courts for disgraceful conduct. In the unofficial section would be placed: 1) Contemporary and historical stories from military life. 2) Biographies and obituaries of important persons of all classes. — 3) Soldiers' songs. 4) Popular articles on the scientific subjects of military art (tactics, artillery, engineering). — From the War Ministry — where you can find both the detailed project and our specimen sheet — they answered us that we could print our articles in the *Invalid*. But from the spirit of the proposed journal you will understand that the articles prepared for it could sooner find a place in the *Agricultural Gazette* or in some *Arabesque* than in the *Invalid*. That is why I ask you to give some <!-- vol. 59, p. 297 --> sections — almost all the unofficial ones — a place in your journal, and not temporarily but permanently. I would undertake every month to supply from 2 to 5 or more printed sheets of articles of military content, of literary merit in no way below the articles printed in our Journal — (I say this boldly because these articles will not belong to me) — and of such a tendency that they will cause you no difficulty whatever with the censorship.

Our advantages — if you accept my proposal — will consist 1) in the fact that our idea of founding a literature serving as an expression of the military spirit will begin to be realized, and I hope in time this will assume larger dimensions; and 2) in the fact that our articles will be published in the best journal and the one enjoying the greatest confidence of the public.

Your advantages will consist 1) in acquiring educated and gifted contributors, 2) in increasing the interest of your journal, and 3) in having material for it prepared in part in advance. The advantages are on your side; therefore the conditions I shall propose to you will be heavier for you than those I shall take upon myself. —

I undertake to supply you every month with from 2 to 5 sheets of articles of military content, for which you pay me whatever you like. You, in turn, undertake to print immediately everything I send you. — I confess this condition seems too bold, and I fear you will not wish to accept it. But if you believe how highly I value the merit of the Journal of which I have the honor to be a contributor, and acknowledge in me some literary taste, I hope you will not by your refusal destroy an arrangement so mutually advantageous for us and an undertaking not devoid of general usefulness. —

If your answer is favorable, then for the first month I shall send you a letter on the Sisters of Mercy, Recollections of the Siege of Silistria, and a Soldier's Letter from Sevastopol.

Then, in impatient expectation of your answer and agreement to my proposal, I have the honor to be

your obedient servant,

Count L. Tolstoy.

11 January

1855

Eski-Orda.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the LB. First published by N. S. Ashukin in AK, pp. 190–193.

2. Nekrasov replied to this letter on 27 January: “Most Gracious Sir, Lev Nikolaevich. I have received your letter proposing military articles and hasten to inform you that I am not only ready but glad to give you full freedom in *Sovremennik* — I trust your taste and talent more than my own, and as for other considerations, at the present time a literary journal cannot but desire materials of this kind and hope to profit by them. I shall write about the monetary terms when I receive the first articles (I shall remain in Petersburg until the end of February, and perhaps longer); write to me or to Panaev, for Turgenev has vanished (he went to Moscow for three days and is still not back), and my hopes in him are beginning to waver. At this moment I have no time to write more. Accept the assurance of my perfect respect and devotion. N. Nekrasov.” (*Krug*, book six, 1927, pp. 196–197.)

3. Turgenev arrived from Moscow in Petersburg on 8 February.
