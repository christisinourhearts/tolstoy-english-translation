\* 5. To Count S. N. Tolstoy. 1856. March 25. Petersburg.

There is no need even to tell you about the peace; you already know.[^1] As for your regiment[^2] — what will happen to it — I have not yet learned anything, but I shall find out and write. — However, if you intend, as I suppose, to retire, it makes no difference to you. — How could you and Vasinka[^3] have had so little conscience as not to write me that you were not coming? I kept waiting for you; otherwise I would have come myself. — Volkonsky[^4] is not here, and I confess I do not remember how our accounts stand. I owed him 230; if you took another 200 and sent 230, then we still owe him 200, which, if you can, please pay. — Even without touching cards and while restraining myself in everything, I cannot live on less than 200 rubles a month. But the point is that publication of my military stories[^5] and *Childhood and Boyhood*,[^6] which could bring me about four thousand if I publish them myself, will give me no more than 1,000 rubles if I sell them; therefore I should now like not only to spend less <!-- vol. 60, p. 55 --> but also to borrow, if that were possible. I am asking to go abroad for 8 months; if they let me, I shall go. I wrote about this to Nikolenka[^7] and invited him to go. If all three of us could arrange to travel together, it would be excellent. If each takes 1,000 rubles, we could make an excellent trip. — Please write to me. How did you like *The Snowstorm*? I am dissatisfied with it — seriously. And now I want to write many things, but there is absolutely no time in this damned Petersburg. In any case, whether they let me go abroad or not, in April I intend to take leave and be in the country. — I am only afraid that you will not be able to extend your leave any further and will have to go to the regiment. That would be bad. — Farewell, until we meet; please write very soon and in more detail.

Your brother, Count L. Tolstoy.

25 March.

### Editorial notes

1. An excerpt from the letter was printed in B. I, 1, pp. 291–292. The year is determined from the contents.

2. Count Sergei Nikolaevich Tolstoy (1826–1904) — Lev Nikolaevich's brother. On him see vol. 59, pp. 31–32.

## Notes

[^1]: On 18/30 March the Treaty of Paris was signed, ending the Eastern War (1853–1856).
[^2]: In March 1855 S. N. Tolstoy entered the Rifle Regiment of the Imperial Family, formed on 20 July 1855. On 11 September 1855 the regiment set out from Moscow for Odessa by way of Tula–Orel–Kursk–Nikolaev. At the beginning of December 1855 the regiment arrived in Odessa. During a month and a half stationed near Odessa, more than 1,000 men of the regiment died of typhus. On 14 April 1856 the regiment set out back along the same route to Moscow, having been assigned to take part in the coronation of Alexander II, which took place on 26 August. On 19 September 1856 the regiment was disbanded. In March 1856 S. N. Tolstoy was on leave.
[^3]: Vasily Stepanovich Perfiliev (1826–1890) — a friend of the Tolstoy brothers. On him see vol. 59, p. 20.
[^4]: Prince Alexander Alexeyevich Volkonsky — Tolstoy's third cousin on his mother's side, a chamber junker. Tolstoy portrayed him in “The History of Yesterday” (see vol. I, p. 279).
[^5]: Tolstoy's *Military Stories* appeared as a separate edition at the beginning of October 1856. It included “The Raid,” “The Wood-Felling,” and the three Sevastopol stories.
[^6]: Tolstoy entrusted the printing of *Childhood and Boyhood* as a separate book to D. Ya. Kolbasin. *Childhood and Boyhood* appeared at the beginning of October 1856.
[^7]: Count Nikolai Nikolaevich Tolstoy — Tolstoy's elder brother. On him see vol. 59, pp. 121–122. Tolstoy's letter to him has not survived.
