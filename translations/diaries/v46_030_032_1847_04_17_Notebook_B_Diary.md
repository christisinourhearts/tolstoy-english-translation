17 April.

—All this time I have behaved not as I wished to behave.—The reason for this was, first, my move from the clinic home; and second, the society with which I began to have more dealings.—From this I concluded that with every change of situation one must think very thoroughly about what external circumstances will have an influence on me in the new situation and how this influence can be removed.—If my move from the clinic home could have such an influence on me, what influence will my transition from student life to the life of a landowner have on me?—A change in way of life must occur. But this change must not be the product of external circumstances, but the product [^1] of the soul.—Here there presents itself to me [^2] the question: What is the aim [^3] of a person's life? Whatever the point of departure of my reasoning, whatever I take as its source, I always come to one conclusion: the aim of a person's life is every possible assistance to the all-round development [^4] of everything that exists.—If I begin to reason while looking at nature, I see that [^5] everything in it is constantly developing, and that each constituent part [^6] of it unconsciously contributes to the development of the other parts; [^7] a person, since he is likewise a part of nature, but endowed with consciousness, [^8] must likewise, like the other parts, but consciously using his spiritual faculties, strive toward the development of everything that exists.—If I begin to reason while looking at history, I see that the whole human race has constantly striven toward attaining this aim.—If I begin to reason [^9] rationally, i.e. [^10] considering <!-- vol. 46, p. 31 --> only a person's spiritual faculties, [^11] then in [^12] every person's soul I find [^13] this unconscious striving, which constitutes a necessary need of his soul.—If I begin to reason while looking at the History of Philosophy, I find that everywhere and always people came to the conclusion that the aim of a person's life is the all-round development of humanity. If I begin to reason while looking at Theology, I find that among almost all peoples a perfect being is recognized, [^14] striving to attain which [^15] is recognized as the aim of all people. [^16] And so, it seems, without error I can take as the aim of my life [^17] a conscious striving toward the all-round development of everything that exists.—I would be the unhappiest of people if I had not found an aim for my life—an aim common [^18] and useful, useful because [^19] the immortal soul, having developed, will naturally pass into a higher being corresponding to it.—Now my whole life will be an active and constant striving toward this one aim.—Now I ask: What will be the aim of my life in the country during the course of two years?—1) To study the entire course of juridical sciences needed for the final examination at the University.—2) To study practical Medicine and part of theoretical Medicine. 3) To study the languages: French, Russian, German, English, Italian, and Latin. 4) To study Agriculture, both theoretical and practical. 5) To study History, Geography, and statistics. 6) To study Mathematics, the Gymnasium course. 7) To write a dissertation. 8) [^20] To attain a middling degree of perfection in music and painting. 9) To write rules.—10) To acquire some knowledge of the Natural sciences. 11) To compose works on all the subjects that I will study.—

<!-- vol. 46, p. 32 --> 18 April. I suddenly wrote many rules and wanted to follow them all; but my strength was too weak for this. Now I want to give myself only one rule and add another to it only when I have already become accustomed to following the first.—The first rule that I set is the following, No. 1.—Do everything that you have determined should be done.—Did not follow the rule.—
