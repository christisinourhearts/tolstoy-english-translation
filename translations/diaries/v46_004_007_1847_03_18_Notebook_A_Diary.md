<!-- vol. 46, p. 4 -->

18 March.

I read Catherine's *Instruction*, and since I have given myself the general rule, when reading any serious work, of thinking it over and writing out from it noteworthy thoughts, I write here my opinion of the first six chapters of this remarkable work.—Beginning her *Instruction*, in the [^1] instruction Catherine says: *Religio Christiana docet nos, ut alter alteri tantum boni faciamus, quantum quidem in cujusque nostrum viribus situm est.* [^2] From this idea she draws the following conclusion: *unumquemque probum et honestum virum viventem in civitatem vel teneri, vel incensum iri [desiderio], conspiciendi totam, quanta est, patriam suam in summo fastigio felicitatis, gloriae, beatitudinis et tranquillitatis.* [^3]—This strange <!-- vol. 46, p. 5 --> incongruity [^4] is completely incomprehensible to me, for [^5] glory, [^6] according to the ideas of the Christian Religion, is an object more worthy of censure [^7] than of man's desire. [^8]—2nd conclusion: [^9] the desire to see one's neighbor at peace under the protection of the laws is completely just.—Chapter I contains a proof that Russia is a European power.—Chapter II contains proofs of the necessity of autocracy, all the more convincing because it speaks of the Monarch in the abstract.—However great a woman's mind may be, [^10] you will always find in its manifestations a certain pettiness, lack of foundation, and therefore Catherine, among her proofs of the necessity of supreme power, says: *Altera haec est ratio: melius obedire legibus sub uno domino, quam obsequi pluribus.* [^11]—Or: *Monarchici regiminis scopus et finis est gloria civium imperii et imperantis.* [^12] Chapters III and IV: *de tuitione constitutionum imperii* [^13] contain the most ordinary thoughts.

Chapter V: *De statu omnium regni incolarum* [^14] begins with the philosophical thought that happy is the man whose will, under the influence of external circumstances, subdues his passions. Reading this, I thought that from this proposition she would derive the concept of law, which is [^15] an external circumstance influencing the will and making the person subject to law happy; but she then [^16] passes to the concept of possible equality in the State, i.e., the subjection of everyone to the same laws.—[^17] The concepts of freedom under <!-- vol. 46, p. 6 --> Monarchical government are the following: freedom, she says, is man's possibility of doing everything that he ought to do and not being compelled to do what he ought not to do. I should like to know what she understands by [^18] the words *ought* and *ought not*; if by [^19] the words what one ought to do she means Natural law, then it clearly follows from this that freedom can exist only in that State [^20] in whose legislation natural law differs in nothing from positive law—a completely correct thought.—Further, in confirmation of her opinion, Catherine gives [^21] an extraordinarily ingenious proof: Freedom is the right to act according to the laws. If, however, a Citizen acts unlawfully, then by this very act he gives others the right to act likewise, and by this freedom is violated. [^22] *Libertas politica in civibus est tranquilitas animi, quae oritur ex opinione unum quemque eorum priva frui securitate.* [^23]—*Ut autem possideant homines eiusmodi libertatem, leges ita oportet esse comparatas, ne civem civis timeat, sed omnes timeant vim legum* [^24]—she says in conclusion.

Chapter VI contains concepts of laws in general. At first she speaks of the content of laws. Further she presents the most elevated thought about the character of legislation.—*Ut immotae semper serventur leges, necesse est eas tam bonas esse, tam que omnibus refertas modis ad attingendum summum quod mortalibus habere licet bonum; ut quisque parens illis in ea versetur persuasione, contendendum sibi esse pro viribus suae ipsius utilitatis ergo ne quis leges has loco suo moveat* <!-- vol. 46, p. 7 --> *subruat ve.* [^25]—Further, speaking of the influence that Religion, nature, laws, principles adopted as foundations, manners, customs, and history should have upon legislation, she observes at the same time that the characters of peoples are composed of virtues and vices.—And the combination of these qualities among themselves constitutes the happiness or unhappiness of a people. Then she gives examples.—[^26] Her ideas about customs are noteworthy. [^27] She says: a law is an ordinance [^28] of the legislator, while a custom is an ordinance of the whole people; and so, if it is necessary to bring about a revolution in a people's legislation, destroy a law by a law and a custom by a custom. Then: The means for eradicating a crime is lawful punishment; the means for eradicating a custom is example. Besides this, intercourse with other peoples has an influence upon customs.—

In conclusion she says: *Verbo: omnis poena, quae imponitur non urgente necessitate, est tyrannica.* [^29]—
