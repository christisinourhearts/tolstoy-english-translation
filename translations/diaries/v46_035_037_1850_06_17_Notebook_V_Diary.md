17 June.

Got up toward 8, until 10 did nothing, from 10 to 12 read and the diary, 12 to 6 breakfast, rest, some thoughts about music and dinner, 6—8 music, 8—10 estate management.—

For the second day I am being lazy, not doing what I assigned myself. Why? I do not understand. Still, I do not despair; I will force myself. Yesterday, besides not doing what I had assigned myself, I also broke my rule.—Now I will no longer depart from the rule that at my place in the country I am to have no woman, except in certain cases which I will not seek, but will not let pass either.—When I am in an apathetic frame of mind, I have noticed that any philosophical work greatly rouses me to activity—now I am reading Montesquieu.—It seems to me that I began to be lazy because I began far too many things; therefore in future I will not pass to another occupation until I have completed what was assigned.—So that I cannot excuse myself <!-- vol. 46, p. 36 --> by saying that I did not have time to compose a system, I will enter in the diary both general rules and rules concerning music and estate management.—Of the general rules. What you have proposed to yourself to do, do not put off under the pretext of absent-mindedness or distraction; but immediately, even if only outwardly, set to work. Thoughts will come. [^1] For example, if you have proposed to write rules, take out the notebook, sit down at the table, and do not get up until you [^2] begin and finish.—

Rules concerning music.—[^3] Play every day: 1) all 24 scales, 2) all chords, arpeggios over two octaves, 3) all inversions, 4) the chromatic scale.—Learn one piece and do not go farther until there is a place where you will stop. Transpose every encountered *cadenza* [^4] into all keys and learn it. Every day play through at least 4 pages of music, and do not go on until you find the proper *doigté*. [^5] Concerning estate management. Think over every order from the point of view of its benefit and harm.—Every day personally inspect every part of the estate management. Do not hurry to give orders, scold, and punish; remember that in estate management, more than in anything else, patience is needed.—Revoke every order that has been given, even if it turns out to be harmful, only at your own discretion and in extreme necessity.—

Notes.

The winter of the third year I lived in Moscow; I lived very haphazardly, without service, without occupations, without an aim; and I lived this way not because, as many say and write, everyone lives this way in Moscow, but simply because this kind of life pleased me.—The position of a young man in Moscow society also partly disposes him to laziness.—I say: a young man combining certain conditions, namely [^6] education, a good name, and an income of ten or twenty thousand.—For a young man combining these conditions, life is most pleasant <!-- vol. 46, p. 37 --> and entirely carefree if he does not serve (i.e. seriously), but merely remains on the rolls and likes to be lazy. Every drawing room is open to him; with every prospective bride he has the right to have designs; there is not a single young man who, in the general opinion of society, stands above him. Let the same gentleman come to Petersburg, and he will be tormented [^7]: why were S. and G. Gorchakov at court and I was not; how can I get to the evenings at Baroness Z., [^8] to the reception at Countess A., and so on—and he will not get there, unless [^9] he can enter these salons leaning on some Countess. And [^10] if he did not grow up there, or if he does not know how [^11] to endure humiliations, take advantage of every opportunity, and crawl through, though with difficulty, but without [^12] honor.—
