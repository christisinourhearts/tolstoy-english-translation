<!-- vol. 41, p. 592 -->

25 August

Labor is a necessary condition of bodily life. If Robinson had not worked, he would have frozen and died of hunger. Everyone sees this. But that labor is a necessary condition of spiritual life, not everyone sees, although this is just as certain as the necessity of labor for the body.

1

> The separation of oneself from manual labor cannot take place without a loss of strength and truth, even for prophets. I do not doubt that the errors and vices of our literature and philosophy—their excessive refinement, effeminacy, melancholy—must be attributed to the enfeebled and sickly habits of the literary class. Let the book be not so good, but if only the one who makes the book were more capable and better, and were not, as we see now, a ridiculous contrast to what he writes.

> *Emerson.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 593 -->

2

> Manual work is the study of the external world.

> The benefits of wealth remain with the one who produces wealth, and not with the one who receives it for nothing.

> When I go into my garden with a spade and dig a bed, I feel such joy and such a surge of health that I always think: why have I all this time deprived myself of this happiness, letting others do for me what I could do with my own hands? But this is not only a matter of pleasure, of health, but also of education.

> I am always ashamed before my woodcutter, my plowman, my cook, because they have the ability to provide for themselves and to get through both a day and a year without my help; while I depend on them and have not earned the right of possessing my own hands and feet.

> *Emerson.*

3

> Whoever does not want to work, let him not eat either.

> *2 Thessalonians 3:10.*

4

*He who does nothing does what is bad.*

5

He who does nothing always has many helpers.

The brain of the lazy man is the devil's favorite abode.

6

> Nature knows no halt in its motion and punishes all inactivity.

> *Goethe.*

One may be ashamed not of any work, even the most unclean, but only of the most unclean of unclean moral states—bodily idleness, inevitably joined with making use of the labors of other people.
