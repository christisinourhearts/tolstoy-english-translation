## THE TRIAL OF SOCRATES AND HIS DEFENSE

*(After Plato's “Apology”)*

The accusations brought against Socrates consisted: 1) in that he does not recognize the state religion, and 2) in that he corrupts young men, teaching them unbelief in the state religion.

The same thing happened to Socrates that afterward happened to Christ and to most of the prophets and teachers of humanity. Socrates pointed out to people the reasonable path of life that had been revealed to him in his consciousness, and, pointing out this path, he could not help denying those false teachings on which the social life of that time was founded. And the majority of the Athenians, being unable to enter on the path pointed out to them, though recognizing it as true, could not bear the condemnation of everything that it considered sacred, and, in order to rid itself of the denouncer and destroyer of the established order, gave Socrates over to a trial that was bound to end in the death of the condemned man. Socrates knew this and therefore did not defend himself, but only made use of the trial to tell the Athenians why he acted as he had been acting, and why in the future too, if they left him his life, he would go on acting in the same way.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 349 --> The judges found Socrates guilty and sentenced him to death. Having calmly heard the sentence, Socrates addressed the judges with the following speech:

“People will say now,” he said, “that you, citizens of Athens, put the wise man Socrates to death without good grounds; they will say that I was a wise man, though I am not a wise man at all, only in order to reproach you; they will say that you put me to death without good grounds, because if you had waited a little, it would have happened of itself, since by my age I am already close to death as it is. And I also want to tell you—those of you who are sentencing me—that you are wrong, having sentenced me to death, to think that I do not know by what means I could save myself from death. I know it, but I do not do it, because I consider it unworthy of myself. I know that it would have been pleasant for you to listen if I had sobbed and moaned and done and said much else. But neither I nor anyone else should try to save himself from death in an unworthy way. In all dangers there are means of escaping death, if only one does not respect oneself. To escape death is not hard; it is much harder to escape evil: evil is swifter than death and overtakes sooner. Here I am, heavy and old, and death has overtaken me, while you, my accusers, you are vigorous and swift, and you have been overtaken by something swifter—evil. So that I, condemned by you, will meet death; while you, who condemned me, will meet evil and disgrace, to which truth sentences you. And I will abide by my punishment, and you by yours. All this was bound to be so, and all is for the best.

Besides this, I want also to foretell something to you, my accusers. For before death people foresee the future more clearly. And so I foretell to you, fellow citizens, that you will be punished immediately after my death—with a punishment much heavier than the one to which you have sentenced me. Namely, the opposite of what you expected will happen to you. By putting me to death, you will rouse against yourselves all those who condemn you, whom I held back, though you did not notice it, and these condemners will be unpleasant to you in that they are younger, and it will be still harder for you to bear their attacks. So that my death will not deliver you from censure for your bad life. This is what I wanted to foretell to you, my condemners. One cannot rid oneself of censure by putting people to death. The simplest and most effective means for this is one: to live better.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 350 --> Now I turn also to you, to those who at the trial did not accuse but acquitted me. Conversing with you for the last time, I want to tell you something astonishing that happened to me today, and what I draw from this extraordinary occurrence. In all my life, in the most important and the most insignificant circumstances, I have always heard a mysterious voice in my soul, which warned me and held me back from actions that might bring me misfortune. But today, as you yourselves see, although a circumstance has befallen me that is usually considered the utmost calamity—today this voice did not warn me or hold me back either in the morning, when I was leaving home, or when I was entering here—into the court, or during my speech.

What, then, does this mean? This, I think: that what has happened to me today is not only not an evil but a good. For indeed, it is one of two things: either death is complete annihilation and disappearance of consciousness, or else, according to tradition, it is only a change and migration of the soul from one place to another. If death is complete annihilation of consciousness and is like a deep sleep without dreams, then death is an undoubted good, because let each person recall a night he has spent in such a sleep without dreams, and let him compare with this night those other nights and days with all their fears, anxieties, and unsatisfied desires that he experienced both awake and in dreams, and I am sure that each will find few days and nights happier than a night without dreams. So that if death is such a sleep, then I at least consider it a good. But if death is a passage from this world into another, and if it is true, what people say, that all the wise and holy people who died before us are there, then can there be a greater good than to live there with these beings? I would wish to die not once but a hundred times, only to get to that place.

So that you too, judges, and all people, I think, ought not to fear death, and ought to remember one thing: for a good man there is no evil either in life or in death.

And therefore, although the intention of those who condemned me was to do me evil, I am not angry either with those who condemned me or with my accusers.

But it is already time to part: for me—to die, for you—to live. Which of us is better off—only God knows.”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 351 --> Soon after the trial the death sentence on Socrates was carried out in that he drank a cup of poison and died calmly among his disciples. The details of his end are described by his disciple Plato in the dialogue “Phaedo.”
