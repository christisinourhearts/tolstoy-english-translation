> LIVING RELICS

> Native land of long-suffering—

> You land of the Russian people!..

> *F. Tyutchev.*

> The next day I woke up very early. The sun had only just risen; there was not a single cloud in the sky; everything around shone with a strong double shine: the shine of the young morning rays and of yesterday's downpour. — While my cart was being harnessed, I went to wander about the small garden, once an orchard, now run wild, which closed in around the little wing on all sides with its fragrant, lush thicket. Ah, how good it was in the open air, under the clear sky, where the larks were quivering, from where the silver beads of their ringing voices came pouring down! On their wings they had surely carried away drops of dew, and their songs seemed sprinkled with dew. I even took my cap off my head and breathed joyfully—with my whole chest... On the slope of a shallow ravine, right by the wattle fence, an apiary could be seen; a narrow little path led to it, winding like a snake between solid walls of tall weeds and nettles, above which rose the pointed stalks of dark-green hemp, brought there God knows from where.

> I set off along this path; I came to the apiary. Next to it stood a little wattle shed, a so-called omshanik, where the hives are put for the winter. I looked in at the half-open door: dark, quiet, dry; it smells of mint, of balm. In the corner a platform has been fixed up, and on it, covered with a blanket, some small figure... I was about to walk away...

> — Master! Ah, master! Pyotr Petrovich! — I heard a voice, weak, slow, and hoarse, like the rustle of marsh sedge.

> I stopped.

> — Pyotr Petrovich! Come here, please! — the voice repeated. It came to me from the corner, from that platform I had noticed.

> I came closer and stood rooted to the spot with astonishment. Before me lay a living human being; but what was it?

> The head entirely dried up, all of one color, bronze—for all the world an icon of old workmanship; the nose narrow, like <!-- vol. 42, p. 120 --> the blade of a knife; the lips hardly to be seen—only the teeth and the eyes show white, and from under the kerchief thin strands of yellow hair straggle out onto the forehead. By the chin, on the fold of the blanket, two tiny hands, also bronze in color, are moving, slowly working their fingers, like little sticks. I look more closely: the face is not only not ugly, it is even beautiful—but terrible, extraordinary. And this face seems to me all the more terrible because over it, over its metallic cheeks, I see—a smile is straining... straining and cannot spread.

> — Don't you recognize me, master? — the voice whispered again; it seemed to evaporate from the barely stirring lips. — And how could you recognize me! I'm Lukerya... Remember, I used to lead the round dances at your mother's, at Spasskoye, remember... Remember, I was the lead singer too?

> — Lukerya! — I exclaimed. — Is it you? Is it possible?

> — It's me, yes, master—me. I'm Lukerya.

> I did not know what to say, and like one stunned I gazed at this dark, motionless face with its bright and deathly eyes fixed on me. Is it possible? This mummy is Lukerya, the first beauty among all our household serfs—tall, plump, white-skinned, rosy—a laugher, a dancer, a singer! Lukerya, clever Lukerya, whom all our young lads courted, for whom I myself sighed in secret, I—a sixteen-year-old boy!

> — For mercy's sake, Lukerya, — I said at last, — what has happened to you?

> — Why, such a misfortune came down on me! But don't be squeamish, master, don't shrink from my misfortune—sit down on the little tub over there—closer, or else you won't hear me... see what a loud voice I've got now!.. Well, and glad I am to have seen you! How did you come to be in Alexeyevka?

> Lukerya spoke very quietly and weakly, but without stopping.

> — Yermolai the hunter brought me here. But do tell me...

> — Tell you about my trouble? As you please, master. — It happened to me a long while ago now, six or seven years. I had just then been betrothed to Vasily Polyakov—remember, such a fine figure of a man he was, curly-haired—he served as butler at your mother's too? But you weren't in the village by then: you'd gone off to Moscow to study. Vasily and I came to love each other very much; he never went out <!-- vol. 42, p. 121 --> of my head; and it was springtime. So, one night... it wasn't far now to dawn... and I can't sleep: the nightingale in the garden is singing so wonderfully sweet!.. I couldn't bear it, I got up and went out on the porch to listen to him. He trills away, trills away... And suddenly it seemed to me: someone is calling me in Vasya's voice, softly like this: Lusha! I look aside, and, being half asleep, I suppose—I missed my footing, and went flying straight down off the landing—and smack onto the ground! And it seems I wasn't badly hurt, because—I soon got up and went back to my room. Only it was as if something inside me—in my womb—had torn... Let me catch my breath... just a minute... master...

> Lukerya fell silent, and I looked at her in amazement. What amazed me, properly speaking, was that she told her story almost cheerfully, without ohs and sighs, not complaining in the least and not angling for sympathy.

> — From that very time, — Lukerya went on, — I began to wither, to waste away; a blackness came over me; it got hard for me to walk, and after that—I was done with using my legs at all; I can neither stand nor sit; I'd just lie all the time, and I've no wish to drink or eat: worse and worse all the time. Your mother, in her kindness, both showed me to doctors and sent me to the hospital. But no relief came of it for me. And not one doctor could even say what sort of illness it is I've got. What didn't they do to me: they burned my back with red-hot iron, they sat me in crushed ice—and all for nothing. I got quite stiff in the end... So the masters decided there was no use treating me any more, and that it wasn't convenient keeping cripples in the manor house... well, and they sent me over here—because I've got relatives here. So here I live, as you see.

> Lukerya fell silent again and again strained to smile.

> — But this is terrible, this situation of yours! — I exclaimed. And, not knowing what to add, I asked. — And what about Vasily Polyakov?

> That was a very stupid question.

> Lukerya turned her eyes a little to one side.

> — What about Polyakov? He grieved and grieved—and then he married another, a girl from Glinnoye. Do you know Glinnoye? It's not far from us. Agrafena her name was. He loved me very much—but then he's a young man—he couldn't stay a bachelor, could he. And what sort of companion could I be to him now? But he found himself a good, <!-- vol. 42, p. 122 --> kind wife—and they have children. He lives here, at a neighbor's, as steward: your mother let him go on a passport, and he's doing very well, thank God.

> — And so you just lie and lie? — I asked again.

> — Just so I lie, master, the seventh year now. In the summer I lie here, in this wattle shed, and when it turns cold—they carry me into the bathhouse anteroom. There I lie.

> — But who looks after you? Does anyone see to you?

> — Why, there are kind people here too. They don't abandon me. And there's not much looking after me needed. As for eating, I hardly eat anything, you might say, and water—there it is, in the mug: it always stands ready, clean spring water. I can reach the mug myself: one arm of mine can still work. Well, and there's a little girl here, an orphan; every now and then she does look in, thanks to her. She was here just now... Didn't you meet her? Such a pretty little thing, fair. She brings me flowers; I'm very fond of them, of flowers. Garden ones we haven't got—there were some, but they died out. But then wild flowers are fine too; they smell even better than garden ones. Take the lily of the valley, say... what could be nicer!

> — And aren't you bored, aren't you frightened, my poor Lukerya?

> — But what can you do? I don't want to lie—at first it was very wearisome; but then I got used to it, learned to bear it—it's all right; for some it's even worse.

> — How so?

> — Why, some have no shelter at all! And some are blind or deaf! But I, thank God, see beautifully and hear everything, everything. A mole burrows under the ground—even that I hear. And I can sense every smell, even the very faintest there is! When the buckwheat flowers in the field, or the linden in the garden—nobody needs to tell me: I'm the first to catch it straight away. If only a breeze blows from that way. No, why anger God?—many have it worse than me. Take just this: some healthy person can sin very easily, but sin itself has gone away from me. The other day Father Alexei, the priest, came to give me communion, and he says: “There's no need to confess you, he says—can you sin, in your condition?” But I answered him: and what about sin in thought, Father? “Well, he says, and he's laughing himself, that's not a great sin.” — But I'm probably not very sinful even with that same sin in thought, — Lukerya went on, — because I've trained myself like this: not to think, and more than that—not to remember. Time passes quicker.

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 123 --> I confess I was surprised.

> — You're alone and alone all the time, Lukerya; how can you keep thoughts from coming into your head? Or do you sleep all the time?

> — Oh, no, master! I can't always sleep. Though I haven't any great pains, still it aches in me there, in my very insides, and in my bones too; it won't let me sleep properly. No... I just lie here like this, lie and lie away—and don't think; I feel that I'm alive, I breathe—and that's all of me. I look, I listen. The bees buzz and hum in the apiary; a pigeon settles on the roof and starts cooing; a little mother hen comes in with her chicks to peck at crumbs; or else a sparrow flies in, or a butterfly—and I find it very pleasant. The year before last swallows even built themselves a nest over there, in the corner, and raised their young. My, how interesting it was! — One flies in, drops down to the little nest, feeds the little ones—and out. You look—and already another one is there in her place. Sometimes she doesn't fly in, only sweeps past the open door, and the little ones at once—well, squeaking and opening their beaks... I waited for them the next year too, but they say a hunter from hereabouts shot them with his gun. And what did he want to gain by it? The whole of her, a swallow, is no bigger than a beetle... How wicked you are, you gentlemen hunters!

> — I don't shoot swallows, — I hastened to remark.

> — And then once, — Lukerya began again, — what a laugh that was! A hare ran in, truly! Dogs were chasing him, or something—only he comes tumbling straight in at the door!.. Sat down quite close—and sat a good long while, too—kept working his nose and twitching his whiskers—a regular officer! And he looked at me. Understood, that is, that I was nothing to be afraid of. At last he got up, hop-hop to the door, looked round on the threshold—and off he was! Such a funny one!

> Lukerya glanced at me... as much as to say, isn't that amusing? To please her, I laughed. She bit at her parched lips.

> — Well, in winter, of course, it's worse for me: because—it's dark; it's a pity to light a candle, and what for, anyway? Though I know my letters and was always fond of reading, what is there to read? There are no books here at all, and even if there were, how would I hold it, a book? Father Alexei brought me a calendar, for diversion, but he sees there's no use in it, so he took it and carried it off again. But though it's dark, there's always something to listen to: a cricket starts chirring, or a mouse starts scratching somewhere. — That's when it's good: not to think!

> — Or else I say prayers, — Lukerya went on, after resting a little. — Only I don't know many of them, those same prayers.

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 124 --> And anyway, why should I weary the Lord God? What can I ask him for? He knows better than I what I need. He sent me a cross—that means he loves me. That's how we're told to understand it. I say the Our Father, the Hail Mary, the akathist to All Who Sorrow—and then I lie on again without any little thought at all. And it's all right!

> Some two minutes passed. I did not break the silence and did not stir on the narrow tub that served me as a seat. The cruel, stony immobility of the living, unhappy creature lying before me had passed to me as well: I too seemed to have gone numb.

> — Listen, Lukerya, — I began at last. — Listen to the proposal I'm going to make you. Would you like me to give orders: they'll take you to a hospital, a good hospital in town? Who knows, perhaps they'll still cure you? In any case you won't be alone...

> Lukerya moved her eyebrows ever so slightly.

> — Oh, no, master, — she said in an anxious whisper, — don't move me to the hospital, don't touch me. I'll only suffer more torment there. What's the use of treating me now!.. Why, once a doctor came here like that; he wanted to examine me. I beg him: don't disturb me, for Christ's sake. Not he! He started turning me over, kneading my arms and legs, bending them straight; he says: I'm doing this for learning; that's what I'm a man in the service for, a learned man! He pulled me about and pulled me about, told me the name of my illness—such a tricky one—and with that he drove off. And afterward all my poor bones ached for a whole week. You say: I'm alone, always alone. No, not always. People come to see me. I'm quiet—I'm no trouble. Peasant girls drop in and have a chat; a pilgrim woman wanders by and starts telling about Jerusalem, about Kiev, about the holy cities. And I'm not afraid of being alone, either. It's even better, truly!.. Master, don't touch me, don't take me to the hospital... Thank you, you're kind, only don't touch me, my dear.

> — Well, as you like, as you like, Lukerya. After all, I was thinking of your own good...

> — I know, master, that it's for my good. But, master, dear, who can help another? Who can enter into his soul? Let a person help himself! You won't believe it, now—but sometimes I lie here like this, alone... And it's as if there were no one in the whole world but me. Only me—alive! And it seems to me as though something were overshadowing me... A pondering takes hold of me—it's a wonder, even!

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 125 --> — And what do you ponder about then, Lukerya?

> — That too, master, there's no way of telling: you can't explain it. And it's forgotten afterward, besides. It comes like a little cloud, pours down, and it gets so fresh, so good, but what it was—you can't make out! Only I think: if there were people around me—none of this would happen, and I wouldn't feel anything except my own misfortune.

> Lukerya sighed with difficulty. Her chest did not obey her—just like the rest of her limbs.

> — When I look at you, master, — she began again, — you're very sorry for me. But don't you be too sorry for me, truly! I'll tell you something, for instance: sometimes, even now... You remember how merry I was in my time? A madcap girl!.. so do you know what? I sing songs even now.

> — Songs?.. You?

> — Yes, songs, old songs, round-dance songs, fortune-telling songs, Christmastide songs, all sorts! I knew a lot of them, you know, and I haven't forgotten. Only I don't sing dancing songs. In my present station—it doesn't suit.

> — But how do you sing them... to yourself?

> — Both to myself and out loud. Loud I can't, but all the same—you can make it out. I told you, now—a little girl comes to see me. A little orphan, that is, quick to understand. Well, I've taught her; she's picked up four songs from me already. Don't you believe me? Wait, I'll just...

> Lukerya gathered her spirit... The thought that this half-dead creature was getting ready to sing aroused an involuntary horror in me. But before I could utter a word—a long-drawn, barely audible, but clear and true sound quivered in my ears... After it followed another, a third. Lukerya was singing “In the Meadows.” She sang without changing the expression of her petrified face, even keeping her eyes fixed. But so touchingly did that poor, straining little voice ring out, wavering like a wisp of smoke, so much did she long to pour out her whole soul... It was no longer horror I felt: an unspeakable pity gripped my heart.

> — Oh, I can't! — she said suddenly, — my poor strength isn't enough... I was just too glad to see you.

> She closed her eyes.

> I laid my hand on her tiny, cold little fingers... She glanced at me—and her dark eyelids, fringed with golden lashes like those of ancient statues, closed again. A moment later they glistened in the half-dark... A tear had moistened them.

> As before, I did not stir.

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 126 --> — What a one I am! — Lukerya said suddenly with unexpected force, and, opening her eyes wide, tried to blink the tear from them. — Aren't I ashamed? What's come over me? This hasn't happened to me for a long time... not since the very day Vasya Polyakov came to see me, last spring. While he sat with me and talked—well, it was all right; but once he'd gone—I did have a cry all on my own! Where did it come from!.. But then with us women tears don't have to be bought. — Master, — Lukerya added, — I expect you've got a little handkerchief... Don't be squeamish, wipe my eyes.

> I hastened to fulfill her wish—and left her the handkerchief. At first she refused... what did she want with such a present, she said? The handkerchief was very plain, but clean and white. Then she clutched it in her weak fingers and did not unclench them again. Having grown used to the darkness in which we both were, I could clearly make out her features, could even notice a faint flush showing through the bronze of her face, could discover in that face—so, at least, it seemed to me—traces of its former beauty.

> — Now you asked me, master, — Lukerya began again, — whether I sleep? I sleep, true enough, seldom, but every time I see dreams—good dreams! I never see myself ill: in my dreams I'm always so healthy and young... One sorrow: I wake up—I want to have a good stretch—and no, I'm all as if in fetters. Once I had such a wonderful dream! Do you want me to tell you? — Well, listen. — I see myself sitting, as it were, like this, on the high road under a willow, holding a whittled stick, a bag on my shoulders and my head wrapped in a kerchief—a pilgrim woman, just so! And I'm to go somewhere far, far away, on a pilgrimage. And pilgrims keep passing by me; they walk quietly, as if unwillingly, all in one direction; their faces are all mournful, and they all look very much like one another. And I see: among them one woman is weaving and darting about, a whole head taller than the others, and the dress on her is special, as if not ours, not Russian. And her face is special too, a lenten face, stern. And it's as if all the others shy away from her; and she suddenly turns—and straight to me. She stopped and is looking; and her eyes are like a falcon's, yellow, big, and bright, oh so bright. And I ask her: who are you? — And she says to me: “I am your death.” I ought to be frightened, but I, on the contrary, am as glad as glad can be, I cross myself! And that woman, my death, says to me: “I'm sorry for you, Lukerya, but I can't take you with me.

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 127 --> Farewell!” Lord! How sad I felt then!.. Take me, I say, mother dear, my darling, take me! — And my death turned round to me and began to say something to me... I understand that she is fixing my hour for me, but so unclearly, so indistinctly... After St. Peter's Fast, she says... With that I woke up... Such wonderful dreams I have!

> Lukerya raised her eyes upward... and fell to thinking...

> — And then I had another dream, — she began again, — or maybe it was a vision—I really don't know. It seemed to me that I was lying in this very wattle shed, and my late parents come to me—father and mother—and bow low to me, and say nothing themselves. And I ask them: why are you bowing to me, father and mother? “Why, they say, because, since you suffer so much in this world, you've eased not only your own little soul but taken a great weight off us too. And things have become much easier for us in that world. With your own sins you're finished already; now you're overcoming our sins.” And having said this, my parents bowed to me again—and they were gone from sight: only the walls could be seen. Afterward I was very doubtful what it was that had happened to me. I even told the priest at confession. Only he reckons it wasn't a vision, because visions come only to the clergy. Only here's my trouble: it happens that a whole week goes by and I don't fall asleep even once. Last year a lady was passing through, saw me, and gave me a little bottle of medicine against sleeplessness; she told me to take ten drops at a time. It helped me a great deal, and I slept; only now that little bottle has long been used up... Do you know what that medicine was, and how to get it?

> The lady passing through had evidently given Lukerya opium. I promised to get her such a little bottle and once again could not help marveling aloud at her patience.

> — Ah, master! — she objected. — What are you saying? What patience of mine? Now Simeon the Stylite had patience, truly, great patience: thirty years he stood on a pillar! And another saint had himself buried in the earth up to his very chest, and the ants ate his face...

> After a short silence, I asked Lukerya: how old was she?

> — Twenty-eight... or nine... It won't be thirty. — But what's the use of counting them, the years! Here's something else I'll tell you...

> Lukerya suddenly gave a sort of hollow cough, and groaned...

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 128 --> — You're talking a great deal, — I remarked to her, — it may do you harm.

> — True, — she whispered, barely audibly, — that's the end of our little talk; but never mind! Now, when you've gone, I'll be silent to my heart's content. At least I've eased my soul.

> I began to take leave of her, repeated my promise to send her the medicine, asked her once more to think well and tell me—was there not something she needed?

> — I need nothing; I'm content with everything, thank God, — she said with the greatest effort, but with tender feeling. — God grant everyone health! But you, master, if you could persuade your mother—the peasants here are poor—if only she'd let them off a little of their quitrent! They haven't enough land, no grazing or woodland... They'd pray to God for you... But I need nothing—I'm content with everything.

> I gave Lukerya my word to carry out her request and was already going toward the door... she called me back again.

> — Do you remember, master, — she said, and something wondrous flickered in her eyes and on her lips, — what a braid I had? Remember—right down to my knees! For a long time I couldn't make up my mind... Such hair!.. But how was I to comb it out? In my state!.. So I just cut it off... Yes... Well, forgive me, master! I can't any more...

> That same day, before setting out to hunt, I had a talk about Lukerya with the village constable of the farmstead. I learned from him that in the village she was nicknamed “Living Relics,” and that, for that matter, she gave no trouble at all: you heard no grumbling from her, no complaints. “She herself asks for nothing, but on the contrary is grateful for everything; a meek one, a real meek one, that's what you'd have to say.”

> A few weeks later I learned that Lukerya had died. Death had come for her after all... and “after St. Peter's Fast.” They said that on the very day of her death she kept hearing the ringing of bells, though from Alexeyevka to the church is reckoned a little over five versts, and the day was a weekday. Lukerya, however, said that the ringing came not from the church, but “from above.” Probably she did not dare to say: from heaven.

> *I. S. Turgenev.*
