## KORNEY VASILIEV

## I

Korney Vasiliev was fifty-four years old when he came to the village for the last time. In his thick curly hair there was not yet a single gray hair, and only in his black beard by the cheekbones was gray beginning to show. His face was smooth and ruddy, the back of his neck broad and strong, and his whole strong body had put on a layer of fat from the well-fed life of the town.

Twenty years before, he had finished his military service and come back from the service with money. At first he set up a shop, then he gave up the shop and began dealing in cattle. He went to Cherkassy for “goods” (cattle) and drove them to Moscow.

In the village of Gai, in his stone house roofed with iron, lived his old mother, his wife with two children (a girl and a boy), also an orphan nephew, a mute 15-year-old lad, and a workman. Korney had been married twice. His first wife was a weak, sickly woman and died without children, and he, already a widower no longer young, married for the second time a healthy, beautiful girl, the daughter of a poor widow from a neighboring village. The children were by the second wife.

Korney had sold his last “goods” in Moscow so profitably that he had put together about three thousand in money. Having learned from a man from his parts that not far from his village a ruined landowner was selling a grove on good terms, he took it into his head to go into timber as well. He knew this business, and even before his service he had lived in a grove as assistant to a merchant's clerk.

At the railway station from which the road turned off to Gai, Korney met a man from his parts, one-eyed Kuzma of Gai. Kuzma drove out from Gai to meet every train, looking for fares, with his pair of wretched shaggy little horses. Kuzma was poor and for that reason did not like any of the rich, and especially the rich man Korney, whom he called Kornyushka.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 206 --> Korney, in a short fur coat and a sheepskin coat, with a little suitcase in his hand, came out onto the porch of the station and, sticking out his belly, stopped, puffing and looking around. It was morning. The weather was still, overcast, with a light frost.

— Well, haven't you found any fares, Uncle Kuzma? — he said. — Will you take me, then?

— Why not, let's have a ruble. I'll take you.

— Well, seven grivnas is plenty.

— You've eaten yourself a belly, and you want to pull thirty kopecks out of a poor man.

— Well, all right, come on, then, — said Korney. And, having put the suitcase and a bundle into the little sleigh, he settled himself broadly on the back seat.

Kuzma stayed on the box.

— All right. Drive on.

They drove out of the potholes by the station onto the smooth road.

— Well, and how are things with you, not with us, but with you in the village? — asked Korney.

— Not much good.

— How's that? Is my old woman alive?

— The old woman's alive all right. Was in church the other day. Your old woman's alive. And your young missus is alive too. What should happen to her. She's taken on a new workman.

And Kuzma laughed in a somehow peculiar way, as it seemed to Korney.

— What workman? And what about Pyotr?

— Pyotr's fallen sick. She's taken on Yevstigney Bely from Kamenka, — said Kuzma, — from her own village, that is.

— Is that so? — said Korney.

Back when Korney was courting Marfa, the women around the village had been gossiping something about Yevstigney.

— That's how it is, Korney Vasilich, — said Kuzma. — The women have taken a lot of freedom nowadays.

— No question! — said Korney. — And your gray has got old, — he added, wanting to put a stop to the conversation.

— I'm not young myself. She takes after her master, — said Kuzma in reply to Korney's words, flicking at the shaggy, bandy-legged gelding.

Halfway there was a wayside inn. Korney had him stop and went into the house. Kuzma turned the horse in to an empty trough and was setting the breeching straight, not looking at Korney and expecting that he would call him.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 207 --> — Come on in, why don't you, Uncle Kuzma, — said Korney, coming out onto the porch, — you'll have a glass.

— Well, why not, — answered Kuzma, pretending he was in no hurry.

Korney ordered a bottle of vodka and served Kuzma. Kuzma, not having eaten since morning, at once got tipsy. And as soon as he got tipsy, he began, in a whisper, bending toward Korney, to tell him what was being said in the village. And what was being said was that Marfa, his wife, had taken her former lover as workman and was living with him.

— What's it to me? I'm sorry for you, — the drunken Kuzma was saying. — Only it's not good, people are laughing. She's not afraid of sin, it seems. Well, just you wait, I say. Give it time, he'll come himself. That's how it is, brother, Korney Vasilich.

Korney listened in silence to what Kuzma was saying, and his thick brows came down lower and lower over his shining eyes, black as coal.

— Well, are you going to stand me a drink? — was all he said when the bottle had been drunk, — and if not, let's be going.

He settled with the innkeeper and went out into the street.

He reached home at dusk. The first to meet him was that very Yevstigney Bely whom he had not been able to stop thinking about the whole way. Korney greeted him. Seeing the thin, towheaded face of Yevstigney, who had started bustling about, Korney only shook his head in perplexity. “He lied, the old dog,” he thought of Kuzma's words. “But who knows with them. Well, I'll find out.”

Kuzma stood by the horse and winked his one eye toward Yevstigney.

— So you live at our place, then? — asked Korney.

— Well, one has to work somewhere, — answered Yevstigney.

— Is the front room heated?

— How else? Matvevna's in there, — answered Yevstigney.

Korney went up onto the porch. Marfa, hearing voices, came out into the entryway and, seeing her husband, flushed and greeted him hurriedly and especially affectionately.

— And Mother and I had already stopped expecting you, — she said, and followed Korney into the front room.

— Well, how are you living without me?

— Living the same as ever, — she said, and, catching up in her arms the two-year-old daughter, who was pulling at her skirt and asking for milk, she went out into the entryway with big, resolute steps.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 208 --> Korney's mother, with the same black eyes as Korney's, dragging her feet in felt boots with difficulty, came into the front room.

— Thank you, you've come to see us, — she said, shaking her trembling head.

Korney told his mother on what business he had come, and, remembering Kuzma, went to take the money out to him. He had only just opened the door into the entryway when, right in front of him, by the door to the yard, he saw Marfa and Yevstigney. They were standing close to each other, and she was saying something. Seeing Korney, Yevstigney slipped out into the yard, and Marfa went over to the samovar, straightening the pipe that was humming above it.

Korney passed in silence by her bent back and, taking the bundle, called Kuzma into the big room to drink tea. Before tea Korney handed out the presents from Moscow to the household: to his mother a woolen kerchief, to Fedka a picture book, to the mute nephew a vest, and to his wife calico for a dress.

At tea Korney sat scowling and was silent. Only now and then did he smile unwillingly, looking at the mute, who amused everyone with his joy. He could not get enough of the vest. He folded it up and unfolded it, put it on and kissed his own hand, looking at Korney, and smiled.

After tea and supper Korney went at once into the front room, where he slept with Marfa and the little daughter. Marfa stayed in the big room to clear away the dishes. Korney sat alone at the table, leaning on his elbow, and waited. Anger at his wife stirred in him more and more. He took the abacus down from the wall, took his notebook out of his pocket, and, to distract his thoughts, began to count. He counted, glancing at the door and listening to the voices in the big room.

Several times he heard the door of the big room open and someone come out into the entryway, but it was never she. At last her steps were heard, the door gave a jerk, came unstuck, and she, rosy, beautiful, in a red kerchief, came in with the little girl in her arms.

— I bet you're worn out from the road, — she said, smiling, as if not noticing his sullen look.

Korney glanced at her and began counting again, though there was nothing left to count.

— It's not early now, — she said, and, putting the little girl down from her arms, went behind the partition.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 209 --> He heard her making the bed and putting the daughter to sleep.

“People are laughing,” he remembered Kuzma's words. “Just you wait...” he thought, drawing breath with difficulty, and with a slow movement he stood up, put the gnawed stub of pencil into his vest pocket, hung the abacus on its nail, took off his jacket, and went up to the door of the partition. She was standing facing the icons and praying. He stopped, waiting. For a long time she crossed herself, bowed, and whispered prayers. It seemed to him that she had long since gone through all the prayers and was purposely repeating them several times over. But now she made a bow down to the ground, straightened up, whispered some words of prayer to herself, and turned her face toward him.

— And Agashka's asleep already, — she said, pointing to the little girl, and, smiling, sat down on the bed, which creaked.

— Has Yevstigney been here long? — said Korney, coming in at the door.

With a calm movement she threw one thick braid over her shoulder onto her breast and began unplaiting it with quick fingers. She looked straight at him, and her eyes were laughing.

— Yevstigney? Who knows, two weeks or three.

— Are you living with him? — said Korney.

She let the braid drop from her hands, but at once caught hold of her coarse, thick hair again and began plaiting it again.

— What won't they think up. Me living with Yevstigney? — she said, pronouncing the word Yevstigney with particular resonance. — The things they think up! Who told you?

— Tell me: is it true or not? — said Korney, and he clenched into fists his mighty hands, thrust into his pockets.

— Enough of your empty talk. Shall I take your boots off?

— I'm asking you, — he repeated.

— A fine treasure, that. Taken a fancy to Yevstigney, have I, — she said. — And who on earth lied to you?

— What were you saying to him in the entryway?

— What was I saying. I was saying a hoop needs to be put on the barrel. What are you pestering me for?

— I'm telling you: tell the truth. I'll kill you, you filthy scum.

He seized her by the braid.

She pulled the braid out of his hand; her face twisted with pain.

— The only thing you're good for is fighting. What good have I ever seen from you? With a life like this I don't know what you might do.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 210 --> — What might you do? — he said, advancing on her.

— What did you tear out half my braid for? Look, it's coming out in tufts. What are you pestering me for. And it's true that....

She did not finish. He seized her by the arm, dragged her off the bed, and began to beat her on the head, on the sides, on the breast. The more he beat her, the more the anger flared up in him. She screamed, defended herself, tried to get away, but he would not let her go. The little girl woke up and rushed to her mother.

— Mama, — she bawled.

Korney grabbed the little girl by the arm, tore her away from her mother, and flung her into the corner like a kitten. The little girl squealed, and for a few seconds she could not be heard.

— Bandit! You've killed the child, — screamed Marfa, and she tried to get up to go to her daughter.

But he seized her again and struck her in the breast so hard that she fell over on her back and also stopped screaming. Only the little girl screamed desperately, without stopping for breath.

The old woman, without a kerchief, with her gray hair disheveled, her head trembling, came tottering into the little room and, without looking either at Korney or at Marfa, went to her granddaughter, who was weeping desperate tears, and picked her up.

Korney stood breathing heavily and looking around, as if just woken from sleep, not understanding where he was and who was there with him.

Marfa raised her head and, groaning, was wiping her bloodied face with her shift.

— You hateful villain! — she said. — Yes, I live with Yevstigney, and I have lived with him. There, beat me to death. And Agashka's not your daughter; I had her by him, — she got out quickly, and covered her face with her elbow, expecting a blow.

But Korney seemed not to understand anything, and only breathed hard and looked around.

— Look what you've done to the girl: knocked her arm out, — said the old woman, showing him the twisted, dangling little arm of the girl, who did not stop screaming. Korney turned and went out in silence into the entryway and onto the porch.

Outside it was still just as frosty and overcast. Flakes of hoarfrost fell on his burning cheeks and forehead. He sat down on the steps and ate snow by handfuls, gathering it off the railing. From behind the doors he could hear Marfa moaning and the little girl crying piteously; then the door into the entryway opened, and he heard his mother with the little girl <!-- vol. 41, p. 211 --> come out of the front room and go across the entryway into the big room. He got up and went into the front room. The lamp, turned down, was burning with a small light on the table. From behind the partition came Marfa's moans, which grew louder as soon as he came in. He dressed in silence, got the suitcase out from under the bench, packed his things into it, and tied it up with a rope.

— What did you kill me for? What for? What have I done to you? — Marfa began in a piteous voice. Korney, not answering, picked up the suitcase and carried it to the door. — Convict. Bandit! Just you wait. Or is there no court for you? — she said angrily, in quite a different voice.

Korney, not answering, pushed the door open with his foot and slammed it so hard that the walls shook.

Going into the big room, Korney woke the mute and told him to harness the horse. The mute, not waking up all at once, kept looking at his uncle in surprised inquiry and scratching his head with both hands. Understanding at last what was wanted of him, he jumped up, put on his felt boots and his torn short fur coat, took the lantern, and went out to the yard.

It was already quite light when Korney drove out through the gates with the mute in the small sledge and went back along the same road by which he had come with Kuzma the evening before.

He reached the station five minutes before the train left. The mute saw how he bought a ticket, how he took the suitcase, and how he got into the car, nodding his head to him, and how the car rolled out of sight.

Marfa, besides the bruises on her face, had two ribs broken and her head split open. But the strong, healthy, young woman got over it within half a year, so that no traces of the beating remained. The little girl, though, remained half crippled for life. Two bones in her arm had been broken, and the arm stayed crooked.

As for Korney, from the time he went away no one knew anything about him. They did not know whether he was alive or had died.

## II

Seventeen years went by. It was late autumn. The sun went low, and soon after three in the afternoon it was already getting dark. The Andreyevka herd was coming back to the village. The herdsman, having served out his term, had left before the fast, and the cattle were driven by the women and children in turn.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 212 --> The herd had just come off the oat stubble onto the big dirt road, muddy, black-earthed, mottled with the prints of cloven hoofs, and churned up with ruts, and with ceaseless lowing and bleating it was moving on toward the village. Along the road, ahead of the herd, walked a tall old man with a gray beard and curly gray hair, in a patched homespun coat darkened by the rain, in a big hat, with a leather sack on his stooping back; only his thick eyebrows were black. He walked, moving heavily through the mud in wet, broken, coarse Ukrainian boots and at every other step propping himself evenly on an oak staff. When the herd caught up with him, he leaned on his staff and stopped. The young woman driving the herd, her head covered with a piece of sacking, in a tucked-up skirt and men's boots, ran on quick feet now to one side of the road, now to the other, driving on the lagging sheep and pigs. Coming level with the old man, she stopped, looking him over.

— Hello, grandfather, — she said in a ringing, tender, young voice.

— Hello, clever girl, — said the old man.

— Well, for the night, is it?

— Looks like it. I'm worn out, — the old man said hoarsely.

— Now, granddad, don't you go to the constable, — the young woman said kindly. — Come straight to us, the third hut from the end. My mother-in-law lets wayfaring folk in just like that.

— The third hut. Zinoveyev's, then? — said the old man, moving his black eyebrows in a somehow significant way.

— Why, do you know it?

— I've been there.

— What are you gawking for, Fedyushka, the lame one's dropped right behind, — cried the young woman, pointing to a three-legged sheep hobbling along behind the herd, and, swinging a switch with her right hand and in some strange way catching hold of the sacking on her head from below with her crooked left arm, she ran back after the lagging, lame, wet, black sheep.

The old man was Korney. And the young woman was that very Agashka whose arm he had broken seventeen years before. She had been married into Andreyevka, into a rich family, four versts from Gai.

## III

Korney Vasiliev had turned from a strong, rich, proud man into what he was now: an old beggar who had nothing but the worn-out clothes on his body, his soldier's papers, and two shirts in his bag. All this change had come about so little by little that he could not have said when it began and when it was done. The one thing he knew, the thing he was firmly sure of, was that the cause of his misfortune was his wicked wife. It was strange and painful for him to remember what he had been before. And when he remembered it, he remembered with hatred her whom he considered the cause of all the bad that he had gone through in these seventeen years.

On that night when he beat his wife, he drove to the landowner who was selling the grove. He did not get to buy the grove. It had already been bought, and he went back to Moscow and took to drink there. He used to drink now and then before, too, but now he drank without sobering up for two weeks, and when he came to himself, he went down south for cattle. The purchase turned out badly, and he took a loss. He went a second time. And the second purchase did not come off either. And within a year, out of the three thousand he had twenty-five rubles left, and he had to hire himself out to masters. He had drunk before, too, but now he began drinking more and more often.

At first he lived a year as clerk to a cattle dealer, but on the road he took to drink, and the merchant paid him off. Then through acquaintances he found a place selling liquor, but here too he did not last long. He got muddled in the accounts, and they let him go. He was both ashamed to go home and gripped by anger. “They'll get by without me. Maybe the boy isn't even mine,” he thought.

Everything went worse and worse. He could not live without liquor. He began to hire on no longer as a clerk but as a cattle drover, and then they stopped taking him even for that job.

The worse things went for him, the more he blamed her, and the more his anger at her flared up.

The last time, Korney hired on as a cattle drover with an owner he did not know. The cattle fell sick. Korney was not to blame, but the owner got angry and paid off both the clerk and him. There was nowhere to hire on, and Korney decided to go wandering. He had good boots made for himself, and a bag, took tea, sugar, eight rubles in money, and set off for Kiev. He did not like it in Kiev, and he went to the Caucasus, to New Athos. Before reaching <!-- vol. 41, p. 214 --> New Athos, he was seized by fever. He suddenly grew weak. He had one ruble seventy kopecks of money left, he knew no one, and he decided to go home to his son. “Maybe she's died by now, that wicked woman of mine,” he thought. “And if she's alive, then at least before I die I'll tell her everything; so she'll know, the vile woman, what she's done to me,” he thought, and he set off toward home.

The fever shook him every other day. He grew weaker and weaker, so that he could not walk more than 10, 15 versts a day. When he was still 200 versts short of home, his money all ran out, and he went on now begging in Christ's name and spending the nights where the constable assigned him. “Rejoice, see what you've brought me to!” he thought of his wife, and from old habit his old and weak hands clenched into fists. But there was no one to beat, and there was no strength left in his fists anyway.

For two weeks he walked those two hundred versts, and quite sick and weak he made his way to that place four versts from home where he met, without recognizing her and without being recognized, that Agashka who was considered his daughter but was not, and whose arm he had broken.

## IV

He did as Agafya had told him. Coming to Zinoveyev's yard, he asked to stay the night. They let him in.

On entering the hut he, as he always did, crossed himself before the icons and greeted the people of the house.

— You're frozen, granddad! Go on, go on up on the stove, — said the wrinkled, cheerful little old woman of the house, who was tidying up by the table.

Agafya's husband, a young-looking peasant, was sitting on the bench by the table, filling the lamp.

— And wet you are, granddad! — he said, — but what can you do. Dry yourself!

Korney undressed, took off his boots, hung his leg wrappings opposite the stove, and climbed up onto the stove.

Agafya too came into the hut, with a jug. She had already driven in the herd and finished seeing to the cattle.

— Hasn't a wayfaring old man come by? — she asked. — I told him to come to us.

— There he is, — said the man of the house, pointing to the stove, where Korney sat rubbing his hairy, bony legs.

When it was time for tea, the people of the house called Korney too. He climbed down and sat on the end of the bench. They gave him a cup and a lump of sugar.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 215 --> The talk was about the weather, about the harvest. The grain won't come to hand. At the landowners' the shocks have sprouted in the field. As soon as they start carting, it rains again. The peasants have carted theirs. But at the gentry's it just rots away for nothing. And the mice in the sheaves—something awful.

Korney told how he had seen along the road a whole field full of shocks. — The young woman poured him a fifth cup of weak, barely yellow tea and handed it to him.

— It's nothing. Drink, grandfather, and good health to you, — she said when he refused.

— And how come your arm's not right? — he asked her, carefully taking the full cup from her and moving his eyebrows.

— They broke it back when she was little, — said the talkative old mother-in-law. — It's her father who wanted to kill our Agasha.

— Why's that? — asked Korney. And, as he looked at the young woman's face, Yevstigney Bely with his blue eyes suddenly came back to him, and the hand holding the cup shook so that he spilled half the tea before he got it to the table.

— Why, there was a man like that in Gai, near us, her father, Korney Vasiliev he was called. A rich man he was. He got so proud against his wife. Beat her, and crippled this one here.

Korney was silent, glancing out from under his black brows, which never stopped moving, now at the man of the house, now at Agasha.

— What for? — he asked, biting off some sugar.

— Who knows. They'll blab all sorts about us women, and then you answer for it, — the old woman was saying. — Something came up between them over the workman. The workman was a good lad from our village. He died in their house, too.

— Died? — Korney asked again, and cleared his throat.

— Died long ago... It's from them we took our young one. They lived well. Were the first in the village. While the master was alive.

— And what about him? — asked Korney.

— He's dead too, likely. He disappeared after that time. It'll be fifteen years or so.

— More than that, surely; Mama told me she had only just stopped nursing me.

— And how is it you don't hold it against him for your arm, that he... — Korney began, and suddenly started to snivel.

— He's no stranger—he's my father, after all. Here, drink some more after the cold. Shall I pour you some?

Korney did not answer and wept, sobbing.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 216 --> — What's the matter with you?

— Nothing, just so, Christ save you.

And Korney, with trembling hands, caught hold of the post and the sleeping shelf and climbed up onto the stove with his big thin legs.

— Look at that! — the old woman said to her son, winking toward the old man.

## V

The next day Korney got up before everyone. He climbed down from the stove, rubbed the dried footwraps soft, with difficulty pulled on his stiffened boots, and put on his sack.

— Well, granddad, won't you have some breakfast? — said the old woman.

— God save you. I'll be going.

— Then at least take some of yesterday's flatcakes. I'll put them in your sack for you.

Korney thanked her and said goodbye.

— Drop in when you come back this way, if we're alive...

Outside there was a heavy autumn fog that covered everything. But Korney knew the road well, knew every descent and rise, and every bush, and all the willows along the road, and the woods to the right and to the left, although in seventeen years some had been cut down and from old had become young, and others from young had become old.

The village of Gai was still the same, only at the edge new houses had been built that had not been there before. And wooden houses had turned into brick ones. His stone house was the same, only it had grown old. The roof had not been painted for a long time, and at the corner bricks had been knocked out, and the porch had gone crooked.

Just as he was coming up to his former house, out of the creaking gates came a mare with a foal, an old roan gelding, and a three-year-old. The old roan was the very image of the mare that Korney had brought from the fair a year before he went away.

“Must be that same one she had in her belly then. The same sloping rump, and the same broad chest and shaggy legs,” he thought.

A black-eyed boy in new little bast shoes was driving the horses to water. “Must be the grandson, Fedka's son; so he's black-eyed after him,” thought Korney.

The boy looked at the strange old man and ran after the yearling, which had started frisking about in the mud. Behind the boy ran a dog, just as black as Volchok had been.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 217 --> “Can it be Volchok?” he thought. And he remembered that that one would have been twenty years old.

He went up to the porch and with difficulty climbed those steps on which he had sat that time, swallowing snow from the railing, and opened the door into the entryway.

— What are you barging in for without asking, — a woman's voice called to him from the room. He knew her voice. And there she was herself, a dry, sinewy, wrinkled old woman, leaning out of the door. Korney had been expecting that young, beautiful Marfa who had insulted him. He hated her and wanted to reproach her, and suddenly instead of her there was some old woman before him. — If it's alms, then ask under the window, — she said in a piercing, creaky voice.

— I'm not after alms, — said Korney.

— Then what do you want? What else?

She suddenly stopped. And he saw by her face that she had recognized him.

— There's plenty of your sort tramping about. Go on, go on. God be with you.

Korney leaned his back against the wall and, propping himself on his staff, looked at her intently, and felt with surprise that there was no longer in his soul that anger at her which he had carried in himself for so many years, but that some tender weakness had suddenly taken hold of him.

— Marfa! We're going to die.

— Go on, go on, God be with you, — she was saying quickly and angrily.

— Won't you say anything more?

— I've nothing to say, — she said. — Go, God be with you. Go on, go on. There's a lot of you devils and spongers tramping about.

With quick steps she went back into the room and slammed the door.

— What's the cursing for, — a man's voice was heard, and in at the door, with an ax in his belt, came a darkish peasant, just as Korney had been forty years before, only somewhat smaller and leaner, but with the same black, shining eyes.

This was that very Fedka to whom, seventeen years before, he had given the picture book. It was he who had reproached his mother for not taking pity on the beggar. Together with him, also with an ax in his belt, came in the mute nephew. Now this was a grown man with a sparse little beard, wrinkled, sinewy, with a long neck and a resolute, attentive, piercing gaze. Both men had just had breakfast and were going to the woods.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 218 --> — In a moment, grandpa, — said Fyodor, and he pointed the mute first to the old man and then to the front room, and showed with his hand how bread is cut.

Fyodor went out into the street, and the mute went back into the room. Korney still stood with his head lowered, leaning against the wall and propped on his staff. He felt a great weakness and with difficulty held back his sobs. The mute came out of the room with a big, fragrant slice of fresh black bread and, crossing himself, handed it to Korney. When Korney, having taken the bread, crossed himself too, the mute turned toward the door of the room, passed both hands over his face, and began to make as if he were spitting. By this he was expressing his disapproval of his aunt. Suddenly he froze and, with his mouth open, stared at Korney, as if recognizing him. Korney could no longer hold back his tears and, wiping his eyes, his nose, and his gray beard with the skirt of his caftan, turned away from the mute and went out onto the porch. He was experiencing some special, tender, rapturous feeling of humility, of humiliation before people, before her, before his son, before all people, and this feeling both joyfully and painfully tore at his soul.

Marfa watched from the window and breathed a calm sigh only when she saw that the old man had disappeared around the corner of the house.

When Marfa had made sure that the old man had gone, she sat down at the loom and began to weave. She struck with the reed some ten times, but her hands would not go; she stopped and began to think and to remember how Korney had looked just now—she knew that it was he—the very one who had tried to kill her and before that had loved her, and she was frightened at what she had just done. What she had done was not what was needed. But how should she have dealt with him? He had not said, after all, that he was Korney and that he had come home.

And she took up the shuttle again and went on weaving right until evening.

## VI

With difficulty Korney made his way by evening to Andreyevka and again asked to spend the night at the Zinoveyevs'. They took him in.

— What's this, granddad, didn't you go on?

— I didn't. Got weak. I'll be going back, it seems. Will you let me stay the night?

— You won't wear out the place by lying on it. Go and dry yourself.

All night the fever shook Korney. Toward morning he dozed off, and when he woke up, the household had all gone off about their work, and only Agafya was left in the hut.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 219 --> He was lying on the sleeping loft, on a dry caftan which the old woman had spread under him. Agafya was taking loaves out of the stove.

— Clever girl, — he called to her in a weak voice, — come here to me.

— Right away, granddad, — she answered, taking out the loaves. — A drink, is it? Some kvass?

He did not answer.

Having taken out the last loaf, she came up to him with a dipper of kvass. He did not turn to her and did not drink, but just as he lay, face upward, so he began to speak, without turning.

— Gasha, — he said in a quiet voice, — my time is ripe. I'm about to die. So you forgive me, for Christ's sake.

— God will forgive. Why, you never did me any harm...

He was silent a while.

— And here's another thing: go, clever girl, to your mother, tell her... the wanderer, say... yesterday's wanderer, tell her...

He began to sob.

— Why, have you been to our folks?

— I have. Tell her, yesterday's wanderer... the wanderer, tell her... — again he stopped from sobbing and, at last, gathering his strength, finished: — came to say goodbye to her, — he said, and began fumbling about his chest.

— I'll tell her, granddad, I'll tell her. And what are you looking for? — said Agafya.

The old man, not answering, wrinkling up his face with the effort, took a paper out from his bosom with his thin, hairy hand and handed it to her.

— And give this here to whoever asks. My soldier's papers. Thank God, all the sins have come untied, — and his face settled into a solemn expression. His eyebrows rose, his eyes fixed on the ceiling, and he grew still.

— A candle, — he said, not moving his lips.

Agafya understood. She took a burned-down wax candle from by the icons, lit it, and gave it to him. He caught hold of it with his thumb.

Agafya went off to put his papers away in the little chest, and when she came back to him, the candle was falling out of his hand, and the fixed eyes no longer saw, and the breast was not breathing. Agafya crossed herself, blew out the candle, got out a clean towel, and covered his face.

All that night Marfa could not sleep and kept thinking about Korney. In the morning she put on her homespun coat, covered her head with a kerchief, and went to find out where yesterday's old man was. Very soon she found out that <!-- vol. 41, p. 220 --> the old man was in Andreyevka. Marfa took a stick out of the wattle fence and set off for Andreyevka. The farther she went, the more and more frightened she became. “We'll say goodbye to him, take him home, untie the sin. Let him at least die at home, with his son,” she thought.

When Marfa began to come near her daughter's yard, she saw a big crowd of people by the hut. Some stood in the entryway, others under the windows. Everyone already knew that that same famous rich man Korney Vasiliev, who twenty years before had thundered through the district, had died a poor wanderer in his daughter's house. The hut too was full of people. The women were whispering among themselves, sighing, and moaning.

When Marfa came into the hut and the people made way, letting her through, she saw under the icons the dead body, washed, laid out, and covered with linen, over which the literate Filipp Kononych, imitating the sextons, was chanting the Slavonic words of the Psalter.

Neither to forgive nor to ask forgiveness was possible any longer. And from Korney's stern, beautiful, old face it was impossible to tell whether he forgave or was still angry.
