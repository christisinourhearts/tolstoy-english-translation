<!-- vol. 41, p. 122 -->

23 February

The existing order of life corresponds neither to the demands of conscience nor to the demands of reason.

1

> Most men of business consider that the most suitable order of things in this world is, simply, the one in which a huge and disorderly crowd tears from one another all it can, and tramples children and old people into the mud, and manufactures various worthless articles by means of workers who can be lured and gathered and afterward dispersed, leaving them free to die of hunger.

> *John Ruskin.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 123 -->

2

> Imagine a flock of pigeons in a field of rye. Imagine that 99 of them, instead of pecking what they want and using only what is necessary, gather everything they can get into a big heap and, leaving nothing for themselves but the chaff, keep this heap for one, the weakest and worst of the pigeons of the flock. Picture to yourself how they, sitting around, watch as this one, having eaten his fill, throws about and wastes the goods, and how they throw themselves on one bolder and hungrier than the others and tear him to pieces only because he touched one little grain from the heap.

> If you saw all this, you would see only what is established and constantly done among people.

> *Paley.*

3

> Can I see without grief how people use their minds to quarrel with one another, to prepare traps for one another, to deceive and betray. Can I look without tears at the fact that the foundations of good and evil are abandoned or, rather, unknown.

> *Theognis.*

4

> In the soil and the sunlight, in the vegetable and animal kingdoms, in ore deposits and in the forces of nature which we are only just beginning to use, are contained inexhaustible riches from which people, guided by reason, could satisfy all their material needs. In nature there are no causes for poverty—not even for the poverty of the hunchbacked or the decrepit. For man by his nature is a social animal, and were it not for the brutalizing influence of chronic destitution, family love and social compassion would provide everything necessary for those who are not able to support themselves.

> *Henry George.*

5

> For the improvement of the common life it is necessary that more and more reason and love be put into the management of public affairs, not only on the part of certain persons, but on the part of <!-- vol. 41, p. 124 --> the whole of society. We cannot prudently leave our public affairs to statesmen. The people themselves must think, for only they can act.

> *Henry George.*

6

> However stable our civilization may seem to us, destructive forces are already developing in it. Not in deserts and forests, but in city slums and on the highroads, are being raised those barbarians who will do to our civilization the same as the Huns and Vandals did to the ancient one.

> *Henry George.*

7

> Reforms must be accomplished by the people and for the people; and as long as they, as now, are the possession and monopoly of one class, they lead only to the replacement of one evil by another and do not serve the salvation of the people.

> *Mazzini.*

*People are reasonable beings. Why, then, are they guided in social life not by reason, but by violence?*
