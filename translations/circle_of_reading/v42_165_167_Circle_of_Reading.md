<!-- vol. 42, p. 165 -->

21 October

As storms stir up the waters and destroy their transparency, so passions, anxieties, and fears, troubling the soul, prevent a person from being conscious of his essence.

1

> People with a great and beautiful soul are always calm and content; people who are petty in spirit are always discontented and sad.

> *Manchu proverb.*

2

> People are perplexed, uneasy, and agitated only when they are occupied with external matters that do not depend on them. In these cases they anxiously ask themselves: “What shall I do? What will happen? What will come of this? If only this or that does not happen.” So it is with those who are constantly concerned about what does not belong to them.

> On the contrary, a person who is occupied with what depends on himself, and who places his life in the work of self-perfection, will not trouble himself so. Even if he were to worry about whether he will succeed in holding to the truth and avoiding falsehood, I would say: be calm—what troubles you is in your own hands; only watch over your thoughts and acts and try in every way to correct yourself. So do not say: “What will happen?” Whatever happens, you will turn to your own instruction and benefit.

> — And if I die in the struggle with misfortunes?

> — Well, what of it? In that case you will die the death of an honest man, doing what you ought to do. You have to die anyway, and death must find you at some work or other. I would be content if death found me at work worthy of a man, at work that is good and <!-- vol. 42, p. 166 --> useful to all people; or if it found me at a time when I am trying to correct myself. Then I could raise my hands to God and say to him: “Lord, you know yourself how far I have made use of what you gave me for understanding your laws. Did I reproach you? Did I rebel against what happened to me? Did I shrink from fulfilling my duty? I thank you that I was born, for all your gifts. I have made use of them long enough; take them back and dispose of them as you please—for they are yours!”

> Can there be a better death? To live to such a death you do not need to give up much, although, it is true, by this very thing you will gain much. But if you want to keep what is not yours, you will certainly lose what is yours as well.

> He who wants to have success in worldly affairs does not sleep for whole nights on end, is constantly bustling and fussing, ingratiates himself with powerful people, and in general behaves like a base man. And in the end what has he achieved by all this? He has achieved that he is surrounded by certain honors, that he is feared, and that, having become a chief, he gives orders to other people. Is it possible that you will not want to take some trouble in order to free yourself from all such cares and to sleep peacefully, fearing nothing and tormented by nothing? But know that such peace of soul is not had for nothing.

> *Epictetus.*

3

> He who has placed his life in the light of understanding and serves it, for him there can be no desperate situations in life; he does not know the torments of conscience, does not fear solitude, and does not seek noisy society—such a man has the highest life, does not flee from people and does not chase after them. He is not troubled by thoughts of whether his spirit is enclosed for long in its fleshly shell; the actions of such a man will always be the same, even in view of near death. For him there is one care: to live reasonably in peaceful communion with people.

> *Marcus Aurelius.*

4

> Only when a person clearly knows his place in the world will the disposition of his soul also be determined. And when the disposition of the soul is determined, <!-- vol. 42, p. 167 --> all agitation of the soul will cease. And when agitation of the soul ceases, complete calm of the soul will set in, and the person possessing such undisturbed calm of soul will become capable of the work of thought. And the work of thought makes a person receptive to everything true.

> *Chinese wisdom.*

5

*A person's true strength is not in outbursts but in undisturbed calm.*

Constant calm is impossible, but when times of calm come, one must treasure them and try to prolong them. These are the times in which thoughts—the guides of life—arise, become clear, and grow strong.
