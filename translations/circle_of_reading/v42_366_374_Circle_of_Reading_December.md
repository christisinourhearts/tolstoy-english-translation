> THE DAMAGED ONE

> ...When I arrived at the inn, it was already very hot outside; I sat down on the balcony. Before my eyes a road scorched by the sun stretched out like a long thread; it ran right along the sea, on a narrow cutting that skirted the mountain. Mules, jingling their little bells and decorated with red tassels, were carrying kegs of wine, cautiously stepping from foot to foot; their slow procession was broken up by a traveling carriage; the postilion cracked his whip and shouted, the mules pressed against the rocky wall, the carters swore; the carriage, covered with thick layers of dust, came nearer and nearer and stopped under the balcony on which I was sitting. The postilion jumped down from his horse and began to unharness; a fat innkeeper in a National Guard cap opened the carriage doors and twice greeted those sitting in the carriage with a princely title before the servant, who had been sleeping on the box, came to himself and, stretching, got down to the ground.

> “Only Russian servants sleep like that on the box and stretch so appetizingly,” I thought, and looked intently at his face; a fair mustache that had turned light brown from the dust, a broad nose, side-whiskers run straight into the mustache halfway across the face, and the special national character of all his ways convinced me once and for all that the respectable stranger came from some Tambov, Penza, or Simbirsk anteroom. However much you philosophize and slander yourself, there is something that stirs in the heart when suddenly, unexpectedly, you meet your fellow countrymen far, far away. Meanwhile out of the carriage jumped a man of about thirty with the well-fed, healthy, and cheerful look that freedom from care, excellent digestion, and not overly developed nerves give. He set on his nose the riding spectacles that hung on a cord, looked to the right, looked to the left, and with childlike simplicity shouted to his companion in the carriage:

> — What a wonderful place, by God, lovely! Here's Italy for you, real Italy, the sky, the sky so blue, a sapphire! This is where Italy begins!

> — That's the sixth time you've said this since Avignon, — his companion remarked in a tired and nervous voice, slowly getting out of the carriage.

> This was a lean, tall man, much older than the first; he was almost all of one color: he had on a light-green overcoat and a cap of unbleached batiste, to match his fair <!-- vol. 42, p. 367 --> hair, which was covered with dust; his weak eyes were set off by light eyelashes; and, finally, his withered and sickly face was more yellowish-greenish than pale.

> The sad figure looked silently in the direction in which his companion was pointing, expressing neither surprise nor pleasure.

> — Why, it's all olives, all olives! — the young man went on.

> — Olive greenery is terribly dull and terribly monotonous, — objected the light-green companion, — our birch groves are prettier.

> The young man shook his head as if he wanted to say: incorrigible, you might as well give up! and glanced up. His face seemed familiar to me, but however hard I tried, I could not remember where I had seen him. Russians are generally hard to recognize in foreign lands; in Russia they go about German-style, without a beard, and in Europe Russian-style—growing a beard with incredible speed.

> I did not have to rack my brains for long. The young man, with the same good nature and the same carefree well-fedness in his expression with which he had rejoiced at the olives, came running to me and shouted in Russian:

> — Well, I never thought, never guessed—truly, as they say: mountain never meets mountain. But you don't seem to recognize me? Have you taken to forgetting old acquaintances?

> — Now I recognize you very well; you have changed terribly: the beard, and you've grown stouter, and better-looking, you've turned all blood and milk.

> — In corpore sano mens sana [^1], — he answered, laughing heartily and showing a row of teeth that a wolf would have envied. — And you've changed too, you've grown older—and so? life does leave its notches, eh? Anyway, we haven't seen each other for four years; much water has flowed by since then.

> — Not a little. How did you get here?

> — I'm traveling with a patient...

> He was a physician of Moscow University who had once held the post of prosector; some five years before, I had been studying anatomy and had made his acquaintance then. He was a kind, obliging fellow, unusually diligent, who pursued <!-- vol. 42, p. 368 --> science zealously à livre ouvert, that is, never racking his brains over a single question that had not been settled by others, but knowing all the settled questions perfectly.

> — Ah! so that green companion is your patient. Where have you put him?

> — He's such a specimen that you won't soon find one even in your Italy. What an oddball he is! The machine was a good one, but it got a little damaged (at this he pointed at his forehead with his finger), and I am repairing it now. He was coming here, but the devil prompted me to say that I know you; he took fright—hypochondria verging on mania. Sometimes he is silent for whole days, and sometimes he talks, talks such things—well, it simply makes your hair stand on end: he rejects everything, everything; it really goes over the top; I myself, you know, don't much believe old wives' tales, but still there is something. Anyway, he's very quiet and very kind. He didn't want to go abroad at all. His relatives talked him into it, you know, to get him off their hands, and besides, they were a bit afraid of his tongue—footmen, yard porters—they're all in the pay of the police—go and justify yourself then. He wanted to go to the country, but his estate is undivided with his sister's; she took fright—he'll go preaching communism to the peasants, and then try collecting the arrears. At last he agreed to go, only it had to be southern Italy—Magna Graecia! So off to Calabria goes your humble servant with him, as his physician-in-ordinary. Good heavens, what a place, you won't find a human being there except bandits and priests. Here, passing through Marseille, I bought myself a pistol-revolver—you know, where the four barrels turn round like this?

> — I know. Still, your post is not one of the most cheerful—to be constantly with a madman.

> — Well, he doesn't really climb the walls or bite. He even loves me in his own way, though he won't let me say a word without contradicting it. I'm perfectly content, though; I get a thousand in silver a year with everything found, I don't even buy cigars. He's very delicate where that is concerned. And it's worth something too that you get to see the world. Yes, listen, I must show you my oddball, I'll drag my patient here; well, what is it to you, really, you'll go your ways in an hour; he's the kindest of men and would be very clever...

> — If he had not gone mad.

> — That's a misfortune... to you, by God, it's all the same, but for him diversion is both necessary and useful.

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 369 --> — You are already beginning to use me for pharmaceutical purposes, — I remarked, but the physician was already flying down the corridor.

> I would not have submitted to his wish and to his Russian way of disposing of other people's will, but the light-green communist-landowner interested me, and I stayed to wait for him. He came in timidly and shyly, bowed to me somehow more than was necessary, and smiled nervously. The extremely mobile muscles of his face gave a strange and elusive wavering to his features, which changed incessantly and passed from a sad, sorrowful expression to a mocking one, and sometimes even to a simple-minded one. In his eyes, which for the most part looked nowhere, a habit of concentration and great inner work could be seen, confirmed by the wrinkles on his forehead, which were all drawn together above the eyebrows. Not for nothing and not in a single year had the brain pressed out through its bony casing such a forehead, and with such wrinkles; not for nothing had the muscles of the face become so mobile.

> — Yevgeny Nikolaevich, — the physician said to him, — allow me to introduce you—imagine what a strange chance, look where we've met—an old friend with whom I used to cut up cats and dogs.

> Yevgeny Nikolaevich smiled and muttered:

> — Very glad—a chance... so unexpected... you'll excuse me.

> — And do you remember, — the physician went on, — how we cut the pneumogastric nerve of the watchman Sychyov's little dog—how she coughed, the poor dear.

> Yevgeny Nikolaevich made a face, looked out the window, and, clearing his throat a couple of times, asked me:

> — Is it long since you were pleased to leave Russia?

> — This is the fifth year.

> — And it's all right, you're getting used to life here? — asked Yevgeny Nikolaevich, and blushed.

> — It's all right.

> — Yes, sir, but life beyond the border is very unpleasant and dull.

> — And within the border too, — added the free-and-easy physician.

> Suddenly, which I had not at all expected, my Yevgeny Nikolaevich went off into peals of laughter and at last, after long efforts, managed to calm down enough to say in a broken voice:

> — Here Filipp Danilovich is always arguing with me. Ha-ha-ha! I say that the globe is either a planet that didn't turn out or a sick one, <!-- vol. 42, p. 370 --> and he says that's nonsense. Then how, after that, are you to explain that it's dull, repellent, to live abroad and at home? — And again he burst out laughing so hard that the veins on his forehead filled with blood.

> The physician winked at me slyly with such an air of superiority that I felt terribly sorry for him.

> — And why shouldn't there be sick planets, — asked Yevgeny Nikolaevich most seriously, — if there are sick people?

> — Because, — the physician answered for me, — a planet does not feel; where there are no nerves, there is no pain either.

> — And what are you and I? And for illness nerves aren't needed anyway—grapes get sick, don't they, and potatoes? I'm just waiting for the globe either to burst or to break loose from its orbit and fly off. How strange that will be—Calabria, and Nikolai Pavlovich with the Winter Palace, and you and I, Filipp Danilovich, everything will fly off, and your pistol won't be needed.

> He burst out laughing again and the same minute went on with passionate insistence, turning to me:

> — One can't live like this. Why, it's obviously necessary that something or other should happen; better for the planet to start over again; the present development is very unsuccessful, there is some fault. In the composition, maybe, or when the moon was separating off, something didn't come out right, and ever since then everything has been going not as it should. At first the illnesses were acute: what an inner fever there was during the geological upheavals! Life gained the upper hand, but the illness left traces. The balance is lost, the planet is rushing from side to side. First it threw itself into quantitative absurdity: well, there came lizards the size of a house, ferns such that you could roof a drill hall with one leaf—well, of course, all that died off. How could such absurdities live? Now it has gone off on the qualitative side—still worse—the brain, the brain, the nerves developed and developed until the mind went past reason. History will destroy man, say what you like, but you'll see—it will destroy him!

> After this sally Yevgeny Nikolaevich fell silent.

> Breakfast was served; he ate very little, drank very little, and the whole time said nothing but “yes” and “no.” Toward the end of breakfast he asked for Bordeaux, poured a glass, tasted it, and set it down with disgust.

> — Well, — asked the physician, — bad, it seems?

> — Bad, — answered the patient, and the physician set about shaming the innkeeper, scolding the servant, marveling at the greed <!-- vol. 42, p. 371 --> of people, at their egoism, and reproached them that innkeepers take 35 percent and still cheat.

> Yevgeny Nikolaevich remarked indifferently that he did not understand why the physician was angry, that he for his part did not see why the innkeeper should not take 65 percent if he could, and that he was acting very cleverly in selling bad wine as long as people bought it. With this moral remark our breakfast ended.

> The damaged one had astonished me from our very first conversation by the independent daring of his sick mind. He was plainly “broken,” and although the physician assured me that in his whole life he had had neither a great misfortune nor great shocks, I put little faith in the psychology of my good prosector.

> We went together to Genoa and stopped at one of the palaces that had been demoted, in our philistine age, to a hotel. Yevgeny Nikolaevich showed neither particular interest in my talks nor particular aversion to them. With the doctor he argued incessantly.

> When the dark minutes of hypochondria weighed him down, he withdrew, locked himself in his room, seldom came out, was yellow-pale, shivered as if in a fever, and sometimes, it seemed, his eyes were red from weeping. The physician was rather afraid for his life, took foolish precautions, removed the razors and pistols, tormented the patient with resolvent and nerve-weakening medicines, and sat him in a warm bath with aromatic herbs. The patient obeyed with a bilious and embittered passivity, objecting to everything and carrying everything out, like a spoiled child.

> In his bright minutes he was quiet and spoke little, but suddenly his speech would pour out as from a burst dam, interrupted by spasmodic laughter and a nervous tightening of the throat, and then, mown down halfway along the road, it would stop, leaving the listener in melancholy musing. His strange, paradoxical sallies seemed to him as easy as the multiplication table. His view was indeed true to and consistent with those arbitrary principles which he took as his foundation.

> He knew a great deal, but authorities had not the slightest influence on him—this, more than anything, offended the physician, who had been a good student and who appealed to Cuvier or to Humboldt as to a final court.

> — But why should I, — objected Yevgeny Nikolaevich, — <!-- vol. 42, p. 372 --> think as Humboldt does. He is an intelligent man, he has traveled a great deal, it is interesting to know what he saw and what he thinks, but that does not oblige me to think as he does. Humboldt wears a blue tailcoat, so must I wear a blue tailcoat too? Now Moses, I daresay, you don't believe like that.

> — Do you know, — said the deeply wounded doctor, turning to me, — that Yevgeny Nikolaevich sees no difference between religion and science—what do you say?

> — There is no difference, — the other added assertively, — except that they say one and the same thing in two dialects.

> — And also that the one is based on miracles and the other on the mind, the one demands faith and the other knowledge.

> — Well, as for miracles, there are miracles here and there, it's all the same, only religion starts out from them, and science comes to them. Religion just says frankly that you won't understand with the mind, but there is, it says, another mind, a cleverer one, and that one, so to speak, said thus and so. But science deceives, imagining that it understands *how...* while in essence both the one and the other prove one thing: that man is incapable of knowing everything, but some things he does understand after all; one doesn't want to admit this, and so, out of human weakness, people believe, some Moses, others Cuvier. What verification is there here? One tells how God created the beasts and the grass, and the other—how the vital force created them. The opposition is really not between knowledge and revelation, but between doubt and acceptance on faith.

> — But why should I accept on faith some pathological truths or other, when I deduce them with my mind from the laws of the organism?

> — Of course, it would not be necessary, but then neither you nor anyone else knows these laws, so it comes down to believing and remembering.

> — You reason in such a way, — I said to him jokingly, taking him by both hands, — that I shall not be the least surprised if, after your return, Nikolai Pavlovich makes you minister of public education.

> — Don't accuse me, please, don't accuse me, — he replied with feeling, — and don't joke about my thoughts. I joked about Rousseau myself, and I know how Voltaire wrote to him that it is too late to learn to walk on all fours. By heavy and martyr-like labor I reached the point of understanding where all evil comes from; I understood, and took fright myself. I told no one, I kept silent, but when the sufferings and weeping of people grew louder and louder, and the evil more and more obvious, then I stopped hiding the truth. We are lost people, we are the victims of age-old deviations, and we are paying for <!-- vol. 42, p. 373 --> the sins of our forefathers. How can we be cured! The future generations, perhaps, will come to their senses.

> — And so, à la fin des fins [^2], the recovery of man will begin when, instead of progress, people go backward, with the aim of being enrolled in time among the orangutans, — said the physician, lighting a fresh cigar.

> — To draw nearer to the animals does no harm, after the unsuccessful attempts to become angels. All animals are calculated for the medium in which they are to live; transplantings are almost always fatal. River water is pleasanter and cleaner for us than sea water, but put some sea mollusk into it—it will die. Man is not at all so richly endowed by nature as he imagines; the morbid development of his nerves and brain draws him into a life not proper to him, a higher one; in it he perishes, wastes away, suffers torment! Where people have broken this illness, there they have calmed down, there they are content and would be happy if they were left in peace. Look at those rows of generations somewhere in India; nature gave them everything in abundance. The ulcer of state and political life passed, the morbid predominance of the mind over the other functions of the organism subsided. World history forgot them, and they lived as people live well, as it is possible for people to live, until the accursed East India Company, which spoiled everything.

> — However, — remarked the physician, — the crowd lives almost like that here at home too.

> — That would be the most important proof in my favor. What you call the crowd, that is precisely the human race. But the crowd is not allowed to live as it wants—that's where the trouble is. Enlightenment costs terribly dear. The state, religion, soldiers starve the lower strata. And to ruin them completely, they hang their riches out before their eyes; they develop unnatural tastes in them, unnecessary needs, and take away the means of satisfying even the necessary ones. What a sad, heart-rending situation! Below swarms a population crushed by work, worn out by hunger; above, another population withers and wears itself out, crushed by thought, worn out by strivings to which there is as little answer as there is little bread for the hunger of the poor. And between these two illnesses, two sufferings, between the fever from a bad life <!-- vol. 42, p. 374 --> and the consumption from mad nerves, between them is the finest flower of civilization, its spoiled children, the only people who somehow or other enjoy themselves. Who are they, then? Our middling landowners and the shopkeepers here. But nature does not let herself be wronged... She brands for betrayal no worse than any executioner, — he went on, walking about the room, and suddenly stopped in front of the mirror: — Well, look at this mug—ha-ha-ha! why, it's horrible; compare any peasant of ours with me—a new varietas [^3] that Blumenbach overlooked, the “Caucasian-urban”; to it belong officials and shopkeepers, scholars, nobles, and all these albinos and cretins who populate the educated world, a weak tribe, without muscles, rheumatic, and stupid besides, wicked, petty, ugly, clumsy, exactly like me, an old man at thirty-five, helpless, useless, who has spent his whole life like cress grown in winter between two pieces of felt—ugh, how vile! No, no, it cannot go on like this, it's too absurd, too rotten! To nature... to nature, to rest!.. Enough building and rebuilding the tower of Babel of the social order! Leave it, and be done with it! Enough striving after impossible things! It's fine for girls in love to dream of wings, von einer besseren Natur, von einem andern Sonnenlichte [^4]. It's time to go home, to the soft bed prepared by nature, to the fresh air, to the wild liberty of self-rule, to the mighty freedom of anarchy!

> And Yevgeny Nikolaevich, flushed in the face, with the veins on his forehead swollen with blood, suddenly puckered up his face, put on a serious look, and stubbornly fell silent.

> *A. I. Herzen.*
