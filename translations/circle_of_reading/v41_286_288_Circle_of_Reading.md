<!-- vol. 41, p. 286 -->

28 April

An undoubted condition of happiness is labor: first, labor that is loved and free; second, bodily labor, which gives appetite and sound, soothing sleep.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 287 -->

1

> The life of Arcadian shepherds and our beloved court life are both absurd and unnatural, though attractive. For there can never be true pleasure where pleasures turn into occupations. Only respites from occupations, rare and brief and unprepared, are truly pleasant and useful.

> *Kant.*

2

Bodily labor not only does not exclude the possibility of mental activity, not only improves its quality, but also encourages it.

3

Manual labor is a duty and a happiness for all; the activity of the mind and the imagination is an exceptional activity; it becomes a duty and a happiness only for those who are called to it. A calling can be recognized and proved only by the sacrifice that the scholar or artist makes of his peace and well-being in order to give himself to his calling.

4

> *Eternal idleness ought to have been included among the torments of hell, but it, on the contrary, has been placed among the joys of paradise*.

> *Montesquieu.*

5

> In the lowest labor a man's soul is calmed as soon as he takes up work. Doubts, sorrow, despondency, indignation, self-despair—all these demons lie in wait for the poor man, as for every man; but he briskly takes up his work, and all the demons dare not come near him and only grumble at him from afar. The man has become a man.

> *Carlyle.*

6

Labor is a need, the deprivation of which constitutes suffering, but by no means a virtue. The elevation of labor into a merit is just as much a monstrosity as the elevation of a man's eating into a merit and a virtue would be.

If you want a good disposition of spirit, labor until you are tired, but not beyond your strength. A good state of soul is always disturbed by idleness, and only sometimes by excessive labor.
