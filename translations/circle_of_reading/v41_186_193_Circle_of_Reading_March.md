## THE SURAT COFFEEHOUSE

There was a coffeehouse in the Indian city of Surat. And travelers and foreigners from different lands would gather there and often conversed.

Once a learned Persian theologian came in there. All his life he had studied the essence of the deity and read and written books about it. Long he had thought, read, and written about God; his mind had gone past his reason, everything had become tangled in his head, and he had come to the point of ceasing to believe in God.

The king learned of this and banished him from the Persian kingdom.

So it was that, reasoning all his life about the first cause, the unhappy theologian became entangled, and instead of understanding that he no longer had reason, he began to think that there was no longer a higher reason governing the world.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 187 --> This theologian had an African slave who followed him everywhere. When the theologian went into the coffeehouse, the African stayed outside in the yard, beyond the door, and sat down on a stone in the heat of the sun; he sat and drove the flies away from himself. And the theologian himself lay down on a divan in the coffeehouse and ordered a cup of opium to be brought to him. When he had drunk the cup and the opium began to stir up his brain, he turned to his slave.

— Well, contemptible slave, — said the theologian, — tell me what you think: is there a God or not?

— Of course there is! — said the slave, and at once took out from behind his belt a little wooden idol. — Here, — said the slave, — here is the god who has guarded me ever since I have lived in the world. This god is made from a bough of that very sacred tree which everyone in our country worships.

Those who were in the coffeehouse heard this conversation between the theologian and the slave and were astonished.

Astonishing to them seemed the master's question, and still more astonishing the slave's answer.

A Brahmin who had heard the slave's words turned to him and said:

— Unhappy madman! Can one really think that God could be found behind a man's belt? There is one God—Brahma. And this Brahma is greater than the whole world, because he created the whole world. Brahma is the one, great God, that God to whom temples have been built on the banks of the river Ganges, that God whom his only priests—the Brahmins—serve. These priests alone know the true God. A hundred and twenty thousand years have passed already, and however many upheavals there have been in the world, these priests remain just as they always were, because Brahma, the one, true God, protects them.

So spoke the Brahmin, thinking to convince everyone; but a Jewish money changer who was there too objected to him.

— No, — he said. — The temple of the true God is not in India!.. And God does not protect the caste of the Brahmins! The true God is not the God of the Brahmins, but the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. And the true God protects only his own people, Israel. Since the beginning of the world God has unceasingly loved and loves our people alone. And if our people are now scattered over the earth, this is only a trial, and God, as he promised, will gather his people again in Jerusalem, so that, having restored the wonder <!-- vol. 41, p. 188 --> of antiquity, the Jerusalem temple, he may set Israel as ruler over all peoples.

So said the Jew, and he began to weep. He wanted to go on with his speech, but an Italian who was there interrupted him.

— What you say is untrue, — said the Italian to the Jew. — You ascribe injustice to God. God cannot love one people more than others. On the contrary, even if he did once protect Israel, it is now 1800 years since God grew angry and, as a sign of his anger, put an end to its existence and scattered this people over the earth, so that this faith not only does not spread but survives only here and there. God shows preference to no people, but calls all those who wish to be saved into the bosom of the one Roman Catholic Church, outside of which there is no salvation.

So spoke the Italian. But a Protestant pastor who was there, turning pale, answered the Catholic missionary:

— How can you say that salvation is possible only in your confession? Know, then, that only those will be saved who, according to the Gospel, will serve God in spirit and truth according to the law of Jesus.

Then a Turk who served in the Surat customhouse, and who was sitting right there smoking a pipe with an air of importance, turned to both Christians.

— In vain are you so sure of the truth of your Roman faith, — he said. — Your faith was replaced some six hundred years ago by the true faith of Muhammad. And, as you yourselves see, the true faith of Muhammad is spreading more and more, in Europe, and in Africa, and in Asia, and even in enlightened China. You yourselves acknowledge that the Jews are rejected by God, and as proof of this you cite the fact that the Jews are in humiliation and their faith does not spread. Acknowledge, then, the truth of the faith of Muhammad, because it is in greatness and constantly spreading. Only those who believe in the last prophet of God, Muhammad, will be saved. And even then only the followers of Omar, and not of Ali, since the followers of Ali are infidels.

At these words the Persian theologian, who belonged to the sect of Ali, wanted to object. But at that moment a great dispute arose in the coffeehouse among all the foreigners of different faiths and confessions who were there. There were Abyssinian Christians, Indian lamas, Ismailites, and fire-worshipers.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 189 --> All argued about the essence of God and about how one must honor him. Each one asserted that only in his country did they know the true God and know how one must honor him.

All argued and shouted. Only a Chinese man who was there, a disciple of Confucius, sat quietly in a corner of the coffeehouse and did not enter the dispute. He drank tea and listened to what was said, but himself kept silent:

The Turk, noticing him in the midst of the dispute, turned to him and said:

— You at least support me, good Chinese. You are silent, but you could say something in my favor. I know that in your country, in China, various faiths are now being introduced. Your merchants have told me more than once that of all the other faiths your Chinese consider the Muslim one the best and willingly accept it. Support my words, then, and say what you think of the true God and his prophet.

— Yes, yes, say what you think, — the others turned to him too.

The Chinese man, the disciple of Confucius, closed his eyes, thought, and then, opening them, drew his hands out of the wide sleeves of his garment, folded them on his chest, and began to speak in a quiet and calm voice.

— Gentlemen, — he said, — it seems to me that people's self-love more than anything else hinders their agreement in matters of faith. If you will take the trouble to hear me out, I will explain this to you by an example.

I left China for Surat on an English steamship that had gone around the world. On the way we put in at the eastern shore of the island of Sumatra to take on water. At noon we went ashore and sat down on the seashore in the shade of coconut palms, not far from a village of the island's inhabitants. Several of us were sitting there, from different lands.

While we were sitting, a blind man came up to us.

This man had gone blind, as we learned afterward, from looking too long and too persistently at the sun, because he wanted to understand what the sun is. He wanted to find this out in order to take possession of the light of the sun.

He struggled long, putting all the sciences to work: he wanted to seize a few rays of the sun, catch them, and cork them up in a bottle.

Long he struggled and kept looking at the sun and could do nothing, and all that happened to him was that his eyes began to ache from the sun, and he went blind.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 190 --> Then he said to himself:

— Sunlight is not a liquid, because if it were a liquid, one could pour it from vessel to vessel, and it would ripple in the wind like water. Sunlight is not fire either, because if it were fire, it would go out in water. Light is not spirit either, because it is visible, and not a body, because one cannot move it. And since sunlight is not a liquid, not fire, not spirit, not a body, then sunlight is nothing.

So he reasoned, and at one and the same time, because he kept looking at the sun and kept thinking about it, he lost both his sight and his reason.

And when he had become completely blind, then he became fully convinced that there is no sun.

With this blind man came his slave too. He seated his master in the shade of a coconut tree, picked up a coconut from the ground, and began to make a night lamp from it. He made a wick of coconut fiber, pressed oil from the nut into the shell, and dipped the wick in it.

While the slave was making his night lamp, the blind man sighed and said to him:

— Well, slave, did I tell you the truth, that there is no sun? You see how dark it is. And they say—the sun... And what is the sun, anyway?

— Well, I don't know what the sun is, — said the slave. — It's no business of mine. But light I do know. Here I've made a night lamp: it will give me light, and I can do you a service with it, and find everything in my hut.

And the slave took his shell in his hand. “Here,” he says, “is my sun.”

A lame man with a crutch was sitting right there. He heard this and laughed.

— You must have been blind from birth, — he said to the blind man, — not to know what the sun is. I will tell you what it is: the sun is a fiery ball, and this ball comes out of the sea every day and every evening sets in the mountains of our island; we all see this, and you would see it too if you had your sight.

A fisherman who was sitting right there heard these words and said to the lame man:

— It's plain, then, that you have never been anywhere farther than your island. If you were not lame and had traveled about the sea, you would know that the sun does not set in the mountains of our island, but just as it comes out of the sea, so in the evening it sets again in the sea. I speak truly, because every day I see it with my own eyes.

An Indian heard this.

— I am surprised, — he said, — that an intelligent man can say such foolish things. Is it possible that a fiery ball <!-- vol. 41, p. 191 --> could go down into the water and not go out? The sun is not a fiery ball at all, but the sun is a deity, and this deity is called Deva. This deity rides in a chariot across the sky around the golden mountain Meru.

— It happens that the evil serpents Rahu and Ketu attack Deva and swallow him, and then it grows dark. But our priests pray that the deity may be freed, and then it is freed. Only such ignorant people as you, who have never gone farther than your own island, can imagine that the sun shines only on their island.

Then the owner of an Egyptian ship who was there too began to speak.

— No, — he said, — and this is untrue too: the sun is not a deity and does not go only around India and its golden mountain. I have sailed a great deal, both on the Black Sea and along the coasts of Arabia, I have been to Madagascar and to the Philippine Islands—the sun lights all lands, and not India alone; it does not go around one mountain, but it rises off the shores of Japan, and that is why those islands are called Ya pen, that is, in their language, the birth of the sun, and it sets far, far in the west, beyond the islands of England. I know this well, because I have seen much myself and heard much from my grandfather. And my grandfather sailed to the very edges of the sea.

He wanted to say more, but an English sailor from our ship interrupted him.

— There is no land but England, — he said, — where they know better how the sun goes. The sun, we all know this in England, rises nowhere and sets nowhere. But it goes ceaselessly around the earth. We know this well, because we ourselves have only just gone around the earth and nowhere ran into the sun. Everywhere, just as here, it shows itself in the morning and hides in the evening.

And the Englishman took a stick, drew a circle in the sand, and began to explain how the sun goes across the sky around the earth. But he could not explain it well and, pointing to the helmsman of his ship, said:

— He, however, is more learned than I and will explain it all to you better.

The helmsman was a reasonable man and had listened to the conversation in silence until he was asked. But now, when all turned to him, he began to speak and said:

— You are all deceiving one another and are deceived yourselves. The sun does not turn around the earth, but the earth turns around <!-- vol. 41, p. 192 --> the sun, and turns itself as well, turning toward the sun in the course of twenty-four hours Japan, and the Philippine Islands, and Sumatra, on which we are sitting, and Africa, and Europe, and Asia, and a multitude of other lands besides. The sun shines not for one mountain, not for one island, not for one sea, and not even for one earth, but for many planets just like the earth. Each of you could understand all this if he looked up at the sky and not down at his feet, and did not think that the sun shines only for him alone or for his native land alone.

So said the wise helmsman, who had traveled much about the world and had looked much up at the sky.

— Yes, the errors and disagreements of people in faith come from self-love, — continued the Chinese man, the disciple of Confucius. — As it is with the sun, so it is with God. Every person wants to have his own special God, or at least the God of his native land. Every people wants to enclose in its temple him whom the whole world cannot contain.

— And can any temple compare with the one that God himself built in order to unite in it all people in one confession and one faith?

— All human temples are made on the model of this temple—God's world. In all temples there are fonts, there are vaults, lamps, icons, inscriptions, books of the law, sacrifices, altars, and priests. But in what temple is there such a font as the ocean, such a vault as the vault of heaven, such lamps as the sun, the moon, and the stars, such icons as living people who love and help one another? Where are inscriptions about the goodness of God as understandable as those benefactions which are scattered everywhere by God for the happiness of people? Where is a book of the law as clear to everyone as the one that is written in his heart? Where are sacrifices like those sacrifices of self-denial which loving people bring to their neighbors? And where is an altar like the heart of a good man, on which God himself accepts the sacrifice?

— The higher a man's understanding of God, the better he will know him. And the better he knows God, the more he will draw near to him, imitate his goodness, mercy, and love for people.

— And therefore let him who sees the whole light of the sun that fills the world, let him not condemn and not despise that <!-- vol. 41, p. 193 --> superstitious man who in his idol sees only one ray of the same light, let him not despise either that unbeliever who has gone blind and does not see the light at all.

So said the Chinese man, the disciple of Confucius, and all who were in the coffeehouse fell silent and argued no more about whose faith was better.

*Bernardin de Saint-Pierre (translated by L. N. Tolstoy).*
