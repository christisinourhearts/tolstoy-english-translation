## WHAT FOR?

## \* No. 1 (MS. No. 1).

IRREPARABLE

It was in the spring of 1828. To the rich estate [^1] of the Przyzdeckis in one of the Polish provinces there came from Warsaw [^2] a handsome young Pole who had just finished his course of studies, [^3] Józef Migurski, the son of Przyzdecki's friend. Pan Przyzdecki himself was a sick, restless old man who tormented his family with his illnesses, both real and invented, and, worse than the illnesses, with his incessantly changing methods of treatment. Panni Przyzdecka had once been a beauty from a family of small means, who had married very young. Of children there were only two daughters: the elder, taking after her mother, the stately beauty Ernestyna, and the younger, the lively, quick, thin Albina. She was 15 when Migurski came.—Everyone thought that Migurski had designs on Ernestyna. She thought so too, and so did he himself. But two days had not passed before it came about that when Albina, winking a shining black eye toward her sister, was about to leave the summerhouse in the park so as to leave Józef alone with Ernestyna, Józef would hold her back, would follow her with an enamored gaze, and without her he was bored. She, without knowing it herself, flirted with him. She did not flirt, but the presence of a young man made her merry, and she gave herself up to her womanly instinct: to do what—she understood it by intuition—he liked. But soon she did not even need to choose what to do: she felt that he liked everything, [^4] whatever she did. He liked the way she raced with the borzois, which jumped up on her and licked her face, he liked the way she climbed the old cherry tree and threw cherries down to him from it, he liked the way she put on a serious look in front of her gloomy father, he liked the way she <!-- vol. 42, p. 493 --> roared with laughter when he fell running after her, [^5] he liked the way she sang Italian arias unskillfully but sweetly and true, he liked the way she, sitting down in the evenings in her father's armchair, instantly fell asleep and suddenly, laughing, woke up. Everyone expected Józef to propose, but, to everyone's surprise, he left without having proposed. Only Albina, in the very depths of her soul, without saying it even to herself, understood why this had happened. In the depths of her soul she knew that he loved her and that she loved him too. She was sorry for her sister. She came to love her even more than before, but what was she to do.

The Przyzdeckis lived by their family, household interests, the old man had discovered a new treatment and was trying it out. The mother ran the large household with difficulty. The daughters embroidered at their frames, played the harp, corresponded with their friends, and were bored. At first they thought of going to Warsaw for the winter, but in Warsaw a revolution was going on.

The whole Przyzdecki family followed the revolution with agitation. The old man abused the Russian government, but expected nothing good. The mother prayed for Poland and for David struggling [^6] with Goliath. Ernestyna [^7] was almost indifferent. Her fading beauty, needed by no one, absorbed almost all her feelings and thoughts. Albina burned with patriotic fire and decided to run away in men's clothes and join the patriots. Her mother found her in front of the mirror in men's clothes. She told her father. Her father began to reprimand her. She said rude things to him. Her father ordered her locked up. [^8] In confinement she cried so piteously that her mother came to comfort her. She kissed her mother's face, her hands, and said that she could not, could not. But she promised not to run away from home, and reconciled to herself her father, who could not help smiling and beaming when she was affectionate with him.

The revolution was crushed, hundreds of thousands of people were shot, beaten to death with sticks, exiled. Among those who suffered, though not among the most important, was Migurski. He was sentenced to be sent into the line battalions and was exiled to Uralsk. Before his departure for Uralsk he wrote a touching letter to the Przyzdeckis, recalling his relations with them, especially his last visit to them. He said that this had been the happiest time of his life, the memory of which had consoled him during his imprisonment in the fortress and his trial. He wrote that he did not repent of having taken part in the holy cause of the liberation of the fatherland and that he was ready to suffer for it. He wrote something more, as if hinting at something: that whatever his plans and dreams might have been during that last visit of his, which would forever remain the <!-- vol. 42, p. 494 --> brightest point in his whole life, he now neither could nor wanted to speak of them. At the end of the letter, sending greetings to everyone, he somehow, in the same playful tone in which he had treated Albina during his visit, addressed her in the letter, asking her whether she still ran as fast, outrunning the borzois, and still passed as quickly from the most violent excitement to the calmest sleep. He wished the father health, the mother success in her household affairs, [^9] Ernestyna a worthy husband, and Albina a continuance of the same joy in life. Józef's wishes did not come true. The old man was already very ill and died soon after the revolution. The mother diligently busied herself with the children, but in a woman's hands everything went badly. Ernestyna married a rich neighbor. But no one fulfilled Józef's wishes less than Albina. Her joy in life, which even before had begun to give way to seriousness, now passed into the most concentrated pensiveness. This pensiveness resolved itself in this, that in 1832, when Albina turned 17, she announced to her mother that she had been corresponding with Józef Przyzdecki, [^10] loved him and he loved her, and she had decided to go to him and marry him.

The mother was horrified, despaired, wept, but in the end had to consent, and, having fitted out her daughter with a faithful maid, sent her off to Uralsk.

Migurski performed a soldier's service, but by special good fortune the regimental commander was a man who, if not educated himself, valued education and respected it. He granted Migurski indulgences, and it was not as hard for him as it might have been. He lived not in the barracks but in separate quarters of his own. In the evenings the colonel's children came to him to learn French. On a winter November evening, in a crackling frost that went off like gunshots in the logs, Migurski was sitting at the table by a single tallow candle, dictating to the colonel's two diligent boys, when at the entrance of the little house a sleigh creaked over the frost and stopped. The children stopped writing, but the zealous teacher went on dictating. “La maison, have you written it? du prisonnier [^11]...” The door came unstuck with a smack, and in came the landlady, a Cossack woman, stout, her skirts tucked up, with her white sleeves rolled up.

— Some little lady has come: is Iosif Stanislavovich here, she says?

— What lady! — said Józef, and the blood rushed to his heart. “It can't be,” he thought. “Can it really be?”

He threw down the book and ran out into the entry. The new arrival had already gone into the landlady's room. When he opened the door, she turned <!-- vol. 42, p. 495 --> toward him. From under her hood, with frosted lashes, there shone out of a rosy face the same joyous, shining, dear eyes to which, he had thought, he had said goodbye forever. — “Albina?” — “Zhozya!” He did not dare and did not think to kiss her; but she threw her arms around his neck, pressed her cold face to his face, and cried and laughed.

Having learned what the matter was, the colonel advised Migurski to marry and arranged the whole affair.

The young couple's life, if not for the [^12] unfree position of a soldier, the possibility of humiliation, and above all the separation from their unhappy homeland, would not have been unhappy. There were Poles they knew, and there were Russian ladies they knew too, the wives of men in the service. Everyone came to love Albina. There was money, so there were no privations. But the main thing was that both turned all the strength of their love upon each other. There were momentary flashes of irritation—Albina was quick-tempered, but these little quarrels only strengthened their love. They experienced among strangers the feeling of two people lost in winter, freezing and warming each other.

After 9 months Albina gave birth to her first child, a boy. The priest christened him Stanisław in honor of Migurski's father, after 1½ years a daughter was born as well.

5 years passed. [^13] Life, compared with that of other suffering Poles, was good, but banishment and the hard position of a soldier made themselves felt more and more. All attempts to petition for pardon, at least for an improvement of their position, for promotion to officer, did not reach their aim. Nikolai Pavlovich held reviews, parades, drills, [^14] went to masquerades, flirted with masks, galloped needlessly about Russia from Chuguyev to Novorossiysk, Petersburg, and Moscow, frightening the people and riding the horse into the ground, and when bold men ventured to report, asking for a softening of the lot of the exiled Decembrists or Poles who were suffering for that very love of the fatherland which Nicholas's lackeys extolled, he would thrust out his chest, fix his tin, senseless eyes on whatever came his way, and say: “Let him serve. Too soon.” As if he knew when it would no longer be too soon, but time.

The Migurskis were, like all people, both unhappy and happy. Unhappy in their banishment, their loneliness, and happy in their family life, in each other, and in their two lovely children. The boy was a repetition of his mother: the same friskiness, the same grace, the same spontaneity. But suddenly there fell upon their family life a misfortune terrible for the mother. The girl fell ill, two days later the boy fell ill: he burned for three days and, in spite of the help <!-- vol. 42, p. 496 --> of doctors, died. Two days after him Stasia died too. As strongly as Albina felt joy and happiness, so strongly did she feel grief too. But as in everything in life she, feeling strongly, had no need to pretend, to exaggerate her feelings and inflame them, so in grief too she did not do what most people do, did not exaggerate her grief, did not inflame it, spoke to no one about it, asked people to stop talking when they began to console her, and, having buried the children, did not go to their grave. But she became pensive and spoke little, and put all her energy into her husband, into easing life. There were no more children.

&lt;Józef suffered, [^15] of course, not as the mother did, but besides that, his grief was lessened by another grief that took hold of him: fear for his wife, that she would go mad. “If she had one child left, she would give herself wholly to it; if she could do something for someone, he thought, she would be saved. If I were in misfortune, she would find herself a task—to help me, and she would get away from herself. What to do? What to do?” He thought from morning till evening, thought on duty, marching or standing sentry, thought when he was teaching the colonel's youngsters. And once, at a time when a visiting general was inspecting the battalion and he was standing in the ranks, he remembered how the priest, burying the children, had said to him: “And their bones, your little ones', lie in a foreign land.” Remembering this, he thought: “The bones can be taken away, that's how to get away myself.” And suddenly such a thought came to him that he did not hear the command, and he alone did not turn right, and threw the whole rank into confusion. The general swore at him in foul words, then softened on learning that he was an exile.—Having somehow got through the review, he ran at a trot onto the porch and into his wife's rooms. She was sitting by the window knitting a scarf. She raised her eyes sadly and in surprise.

And Migurski told her what had come into his head during the review.

His idea was this: he [^16] would leave the house, go to the Ural, throw down his greatcoat on the bank, and on it put a letter to her, in which he would say that he could no longer live like this and be the cause of her banishment, and that he had therefore decided to take his own life. Rosołowski knew the whole affair, which had made a great stir at the time, of Sierociński, who had tried to raise all the exiles and get free. It was the beginning of May. So as not to be caught by the heat and, above all, so as to get into the box at night and set it under the coachman's seat of the tarantass, the departure was fixed for 3 o'clock in the morning. — Stepan, — she said to him. — Why should you travel with us, stay here, and we'll go on alone.

Early in the morning three policemen and the Cossack came into the hotel where they were staying. The Cossack [^35] had lain in wait for her and informed. At 10 o'clock Migurski stood in hand and leg irons before the general, on the floor stood two empty little coffins, and Albina, with lowered eyes, stood by the doorpost. The interrogation was going on.

When the interrogation was over, [^36] the Cossack went up to [^37] Albina.

— The oath. Impossible.

She looked at him as though not understanding who he was.

— What have I done. What for? — she cried out and burst into sobs.

The Cossack turned away and hurriedly went out.

The Cossack took to drink, drank away everything he had on him, and returned home only when he was sent back under convoy.

Migurski was tried, sentenced to 3000 sticks, but pardoned and exiled to Siberia. Albina did not recover, and pined, fell into consumption, prayed unceasingly, and in that same year quietly died.

## \* No. 2 (MS. No. 3, to ch. II).

The whole Jaczewski family followed with agitation everything that was going on. Every day they sent messengers for letters and newspapers. The old man so forgot his illness that he wrote to his acquaintance Dziekoński that he was offering him his services for the formation of new regiments in Warsaw, where he was intending to move. Pani Jaczewska, though wholly absorbed in the household, was also in a state of patriotic excitement, like everyone in the house. Wanda founded a patriotic committee in her district and, having sold her diamonds, collected money for the formation of a detachment, corresponded with her friends, and sympathized, like everyone, with the revolution. Albina went on living her childish girlish life with dogs and horses [^38] and singing and music. [^39] But in the middle of [^40] February, when a letter was <!-- vol. 42, p. 502 --> received from Migurski, in which he described with rapture a battle, [^41] in which he had taken part, where the Poles, under the command of Dwernicki, had routed a Russian brigade and taken prisoners, [^42] Albina's attitude toward the revolution suddenly changed. She questioned her father, studied maps, was in raptures at the victories of the Poles and in despair at their defeats, would not believe them, and stopped singing, laughing, playing with the dogs. Once her mother, coming into her room, found her in front of the mirror in men's clothes. Under the bed her mother found [^43] a suitcase with linen and clothes, with which she had been preparing to run away to join the army. Her mother told her father. Her father began to reprimand her. She said rude things to him. Her father ordered her locked up. Nanny would come up to her door and comfort her. Albina bawled so piteously that her mother came to comfort her, somehow they made peace, and Albina gave her word not to run away from home.

## \* No. 3 (MS. No. 4, to ch. II).

The whole Jaczewski family followed with agitation [^44] what was going on in the land in revolt. The uprising had not yet reached them, but everyone expected it and prepared for it. [^45] Old Jaczewski so forgot his illnesses that he wrote to his acquaintance Dziekoński that he was offering him his services for the formation of new regiments in Warsaw, where he was intending to move. Pani Jaczewska, though sharing the patriotic excitement of the whole household, was afraid for her husband. Wanda founded in her district a patriotic committee to help the combatants and, having sold her diamonds, collected money and secretly sent it on through her friends to Warsaw. Only Albina remained insensible to the revolution and went on living her childishly girlish life, with dogs, horses, music, and singing.

But in the middle of February her attitude toward the revolution suddenly changed. A letter was received from Migurski in which he described a battle in which he had taken part. When Albina, in her rapturously merry mood, sure that now it was all over and that in a few days they would be free, went into the yard of the inn, she was surprised that many people were standing at the gate and under the shed. The people were crowding around her tarantass. Her heart went cold in her breast, but she did not yet believe it. The crowd parted before her. By the tarantass, between two soldiers, stood her husband, looking at her piteously.

— Albina! It's nothing, Albina! It's nothing, — he kept repeating, not knowing how he might calm her.

— And here's the little lady herself, — said the police chief's assistant.

— This way, please, this way. Who is this? Who is he to you?

Albina answered nothing and only glanced now at the police chief, now at her husband.

— Is he your husband? — the police chief repeated.

— He? — cried Albina. — Leave him alone. He is not to blame for anything. I am to blame. Take me.

The police chief ordered irons to be brought and put on Migurski. The Cossack stood right there and glanced now at Migurski, now at her, shaking his head in bewilderment.

— What for? What for? — she cried out and, running up to her husband, put her arms around his neck.

— Razem (together), wszystko razem (everything together), — she said, and pressed herself to him, shaking all over with sobs.

Migurski was tried and, for the escape, sentenced to be driven through 1000. His relatives and Wanda, who had connections in Petersburg, obtained a mitigation of the punishment for him, and he was exiled to permanent settlement in Siberia.

[^85] Albina lived with him, looked after him, holding him back from the craving for drink that was taking ever greater hold of him, tried to comfort him, to cheer him up, but she herself was dying, unstoppably and visibly.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 516 --> Not a trace of her former joy in life remained in her. She could not understand why, what for, this cruelty, and she was dying and was glad that she was leaving this senselessly cruel world.

Migurski was tried and, for the escape, sentenced to be driven through 1000. His relatives and Wanda, who had connections in Petersburg, obtained a mitigation of the punishment for him, and he was exiled to permanent settlement in Siberia.

Albina did not leave him, but she did not live long. She died, as people said, of consumption. But she knew that she was dying of grief. To her last days she could not understand why, what for—this senseless cruelty? No one could explain this to her. Her one hope was that this mystery would be revealed to her after death, and she met the approaching death without fear, at times even with joy. She was sorry to leave weak, good-natured Józek without help, but lately she had seen that she, weak and sick, was only a burden to him.

Migurski, even while she was alive, had begun to drink more than one could without harm to soul and body; and without her he let himself go entirely and, supported by his comrades, lived some 10 more years of a senseless, aimless, and suffering life.

## No. 23 (MS. No. 16, to ch. XII).

Nikolai Pavlovich rejoiced that he had crushed the hydra of revolution not only in Poland but in all Europe; and was proud that he had not violated the precepts of his grandmother, the great Catherine, and, for the good of the Russian people, had kept Poland in the power of Russia. And people in stars and gilded uniforms praised him so for this that he sincerely believed that he was a great man and that his life had been a great good for mankind and especially for Russian people, toward whose corruption and stupefaction all his stupid powers had been directed.
