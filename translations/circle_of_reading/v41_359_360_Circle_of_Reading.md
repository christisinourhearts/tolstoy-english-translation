1 June

*It is better to do nothing than to do what is harmful.*

1

People often proudly refuse innocent amusements, saying that they have no time because they have work to do. And yet, not to mention that good-natured, merry play is more necessary and more important than many kinds of work, the work that busy people boast of is often such that it would be better never to do it.

2

When occupied with work—not bad work (one must never and in no way be occupied with bad work), but indifferent and even good work—and when giving oneself up to good pleasures, one must remember that there are demands of the soul (conscience) which are more important than any pleasures and occupations; and all such occupations must be dropped at once, as soon as conscience calls for the fulfillment of its demand. And yet occupations and pleasures have the property of absorbing people so much that good, moral people answer moral demands: “I have no time, I must try out the oxen I have bought, I must bury my dead father.”

One must remember the meaning of the words: let the dead bury the dead.

3

Cruel people always try to be busy, so as to find in their occupations a justification for their cruelty.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 360 -->

4

Just as a horse on a treadwheel cannot help walking, so a person cannot do nothing. And therefore there is as much merit in a person's working as in a person's breathing; what matters is what a person does.

5

It is a very common error to consider pleasures, amusements, an unimportant and even a bad thing (Islam, old-time Orthodoxy, Puritanism). Pleasure is as important as labor; it is the reward of labor. Labor cannot go on without a break. Necessary rest is naturally adorned with pleasures.

Pleasures are bad only when, first, they require the labor of other people (to prepare tennis, theater, horse races, and the like). Second, when pleasures turn into a sharp struggle of competition, as often happens in games of skill; and, third, when pleasures are arranged only for a few. If none of this is present, then pleasure, especially for the young, is not only not a bad thing but a good one.

6

There is no occupation more empty and useless, and more harmful to the soul, than concern with increasing one's property, and there is no occupation that draws one in as much as this one, and to which such importance is ascribed.

Work and pleasure, alternating properly, make life joyful. But not every kind of work and not every pleasure.
