## KORNEY VASILIEV

## \* No. 1 (MS. No. 1).

## WHAT HE WAS AND WHAT HE BECAME

Korney Vasiliev was a proud man. [^1] He was a black-browed, curly-haired, mighty man, [^2] well able to read and write, and abstemious. He was richer, and healthier, and cleverer, and better read than all his fellow villagers and acquaintances. For two three-year terms he had served as headman, and then he opened a shop in town. In the village he had a two-story house, of stone, roofed with iron. At home in the village lived his wife with the children and his old mother. In the house worked an orphan nephew and a workman. The household was a rich one: three horses, one of them of good breed, foals, two cows, 2 tens of sheep. Both his wife and his mother lived in full contentment. His wife came to Korney in town two or so times a month (30 versts away), and for a holiday he himself came to the village with expensive presents for his mother and family. He was 43 years old when, after a trip to Moscow, [^3] he came back to the village for the last time, for the holiday. In his curly beard and curly hair there was not yet a single gray hair, and his face was young and ruddy, his eyes shone and played like bits of ice in the sun. He always held himself straight, but by 40 his strong body had put on a layer of fat in his calm, luxurious life, and a belly had begun to grow. All his affairs went well; he had had the chance to buy a grove on good terms. And besides that he had been elected a councilman, and he had become acquainted with clever, learned gentlemen, who addressed him with respect, asking his opinion. He came home especially cheerful.

At the railway station the cabman from their village, old Kuzma, undertook to drive him to the village for a ruble. Kuzma was poor and for that reason did not like any of the rich, and did not like Korney, whom <!-- vol. 42, p. 475 --> he had known as Kornyushka, and was always glad of a chance to knock the conceit [^4] off Kornyushka.

— Well, haven't you found any fares, Uncle Kuzma? — said Korney, coming out onto the porch with his little suitcase, in his cloth-covered short fur coat and sheepskin coat, sticking out his belly. — Will you take me, then?

— [^5] Why not, let's have a ruble. I'll take you.

— [^6] Even 7 grivnas is plenty.

— You've eaten yourself a belly, and you want to pull 30 kopecks out of a poor man.

— Well, all right, come on, then. Only take the bundle too.

— All right, all right.

— The little horse is still the same one. Yours is awfully thin.

— Thin, but she pulls. Giddap, giddap.

They drove out of the potholes by the station onto the smooth road.

— Well, Uncle Kuzma? How are things with you in the village? What about our folks?

— Oh, everything's the old way; can't say it's the good way.

— How's that?

— Just so. Not much good.

— And what's bad? Is the old woman alive?

— The old woman's alive all right. Was in church the other day. The old woman's alive all right.

— Well, what then?

— Oh, nothing. You'll come yourself, you'll find out. You can't hide an awl in a sack.

Kuzma rejoiced spitefully that he could cause pain [^7] to the potbellied rich man.

— But what's there to hide?

— Why, she isn't even hiding it.

— Who's she?

— [^8] Oh, I don't know anything; you'll get home, they'll tell you everything.

Halfway there was a tavern; Korney had him stop and went in, and Kuzma too; he served him a drink and got out of him everything that Kuzma had not wanted to tell.

In the meat-eating season the old workman chopped his own leg and went into the hospital. They took on a new workman, Yevstigney Chorny from Kamenka. And Marfa, Korney's wife, is living with him. In the village everyone knows. And she doesn't even hide it. The old woman has scolded her too and frightened her with her husband. Nothing gets through to her. It'd be one thing if it was from need, — Kuzma finished his story, — but it's from fat living.

— Aren't you lying?

— If I'm lying, I'm lying; so much the better for you. [^9]

Korney did not talk any more.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 476 --> At the yard he was met by Yevstigney Chorny.

Korney [^10] greeted him.

— I've come to work for you, your missus hired me, — said Yevstigney. — Is this your luggage? Shall I take it out, then?

— Well, yes.

His mother, with the same black eyes as her son's, was, as always, quiet and joyful. His wife at first met her husband calmly and began helping him undress. But glancing into his eyes, she suddenly flushed, and got angry at her daughter, and began to scold. She behaved somehow especially proudly with her husband and went off to set up the samovar; Korney handed out the presents, and during the day he only watched closely and said nothing to his wife. The workman had gone off for firewood for a long time. He talked with his mother, told her about Moscow, and with the children when they came home from school. The children were: two, 12 and 10 years old, boys, and a girl of 8. Korney began to talk with his wife only at night, when the old woman had gone up onto the stove in [^11] the room with the Russian stove, with the children and the workman, and he and his wife were left alone in the front room with the Dutch stove. After dinner Marfa had gone off somewhere, and when she came back, she was red, and she smelled of liquor. Before, she had avoided his eyes, but now, when he, having taken off his coat and left in just his trousers and vest, stopped in front of her (she was sitting on the bed and setting her braid straight), she looked straight at him and smiled.

— Has Yevstigney been here long? — said Korney, not looking at her.

— Who knows. 5 weeks or 6.

— Are you living with him? — He glanced at her with his shining black eyes. She started, let the braid drop from her hands, but at once caught it again and, quickly working her fingers, looking straight into her husband's face, giggled.

— Living with Yevstigney, the things they think up. Who told you I'm living with Yevstigney? — she repeated, pronouncing Yevstigney's name with particular pleasure.

— Tell me: is it true or not? — he said, holding his breath, so that his high chest rose still higher, and coming up to her and frowning terribly, looking at her braid.

— Enough of your empty talk. [^12] Look at you. Get undressed, why don't you. Let me take your boots off.

— Is it true or not?

— Of course not; and who told you about Yevstigney?

— Never mind who told me, you want to disgrace me, so that people laugh. I can see it in your eyes, you drunken bitch.

He seized her by the braid and jerked it. And, as he remembered Kuzma's mockery, such anger came into his heart that he was ready right then, without sorting anything out, to strangle her with his mighty hands.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 477 --> And a strange thing: both the pain and the threat of death that she felt did not quiet her, but on the contrary, his anger passed into her, and she, seizing the hand that held the braid, screamed at him in an angry, shrill voice, baring her white teeth:

— Well, so I live with Yevstigney. And with you I don't want to live. There, kill me!

Such a terrible feeling of horror, rage, shame, hatred for this woman, who was wholly in his power, seized Korney that he flung her onto the bed and ran [^13] out of the front room.

He knew that his wife was a spiteful woman. He saw it in her dealings with him, with her mother-in-law, with the children, the workmen, but until now he had not known her to be unfaithful to him, and she had always been submissive with him. And this he had not expected of her.

He went out onto the porch. Cooled down and came back into the front room.

— What, come back again? Didn't kill me, I bet.

— Marfa. Don't you joke.

— What's there to joke about? I don't know myself what I said. What did you tear out half my braid for? Look, it's coming out in tufts.

— What did you say?

— I didn't say anything. I said: go, lie down.

— And about that one?

— About Yevstigney? I didn't say anything.

— What are you twisting about for? Say one thing or the other.

— I've nothing to say. You've gone off your head, I can see.

— Marfa!

— [^14] Well, what: Marfa. — And she burst out laughing.

This he could not bear; he threw himself on her and began to beat her on the face, on the sides. Her screaming woke the old woman. She ran into the front room with the workman. Marfa lay on the floor, wheezing. He was not himself and was kicking her.

— Out! — he shouted at Yevstigney, who had come in; Yevstigney backed out the door; after him the old woman came in.

— Mother, she's ruined me, this... I've killed her. — And, sobbing, he ran out into the entryway.

Korney left that same night and from then on did not come back.

Marfa was ill for a long time. Besides the bruises on her face, she had two ribs broken, her head split open, [^15] and her arm dislocated. She got well, and Yevstigney stayed on living there. [^16] And he lived with her as with a wife. There was no word of Korney. In the first month it was heard that he was living in town and drinking, and from spring on he vanished somewhere, and there was no word of him.

## II

15 years went by. It was muddy, dark, late autumn. [^17] In Andreyevka [^18] the herdsman, having served out his term until the fast, had left, and the cattle were driven by the women and children in turn. [^19] It was still early, but the sun went low, and it was getting dark, and those whose turn it was were driving the herd home. One of those whose turn it was was Agafya, Korney's daughter. She had been married off last year into Andreyevka to a good [^20] young fellow from a rich house, and she had not yet given birth. When the herd was driven out onto the road, it caught up with a wanderer, a stooped old man, white as a harrier, who, in a soaked-through [^21] threadbare homespun coat, in bast shoes and a big hat, was dragging himself along the road with his last strength. On the old man's back was [^22] a little sack. He walked, [^23] falling forward with his whole body, at every other step propping himself on a long [^24] staff. [^25] When the herd caught up with him, he stopped and, leaning on his stick, his head lowered and breathing heavily, let the herd go past him. When the temporary herders, a lad and [^26] Agafya, came level with him, he raised his old, thin, wrinkled face and said: [^27]

— God help you, clever girl!

— Christ save you, grandfather, — answered [^28] the fair-haired, pretty Agafya in a kind voice. She was walking with her head covered with a piece of sacking, with a tucked-up skirt and in men's boots.

— Well, for the night, is it, grandfather?

— Yes, looks like it. [^29]

The young woman stopped beside him. [^30]

— The constable, where [^31] is he here?

— [^32] Never mind the constable. Come straight to us. 3rd hut from the end. Our folks let wayfaring people in.

— Christ save you. Zinoveyev's, then?

<!-- vol. 42, p. 479 --> The young woman looked more closely at the old man.

— Why, [^33] do you know it?

— I was told [^34] by a passerby.

— What are you [^35] gawking for, Fedyushka, the lame one's dropped right behind, — cried the young woman, swung a switch with her right hand, and in some strange way, from below, awkwardly caught hold of the sacking on her head with her left arm, and ran back after the lagging, lame, wet, black sheep. The old man was Korney.

— Why, that's Agashka, — [^36] he thought. — It's the left arm that's broken, sure enough. And in the face [^37] she's the living Yevstigney—two drops of water.

And in Korney's soul there rose [^38] the memory of that anger which he had lived through. And he felt pained, sorry, ashamed, and [^39] he wanted to cry. All that had been, had been with him: he had beaten his wife, he had broken the little girl's arm, he had wanted to kill Yevstigney. All this was he. But now he was a different man. [^40]

## III

Korney Vasiliev had lived through much in this time. Having left home, he [^41] <sup></sup> came to Moscow, expecting a trial. He thought that he had killed his wife. But no one summoned him. He began to drink, so as to forget himself. He lived in Moscow two months, leading the most dissolute and debauched life. [^42] But forget himself [^43] he could not. Two or so times he stopped, gave up drinking. But sober he could not master himself at all. “An orphan, a beggar, I took her out of want. Gave her everything. And there you have it... What she's done!” He would remember his mother, his son, would want to go and see them, to drive her out. But as soon as he remembered her and Yevstigney, such anger rose up that his heart stopped beating, and he felt that he would kill her and her illegitimate, foul girl, got while he was away. And he would take to drink again. So he drank up and squandered almost everything he had in town. And then it happened that in a tavern he got into a drunken fight with factory hands. He beat the factory hands, and they beat him and took him to the police station. In the station they held him [^44] with thieves and drunkards for two days and nights and let him out robbed. After this <!-- vol. 42, p. 480 --> disgrace he again wanted to give up drinking and settle down. But his affairs at the shop had gone completely to pieces. There was nothing to live on in Moscow. Home he could not go; he was afraid of himself. Once he did go home, but, having got as far as the station, he turned back. He did not know what to do. His thoughts were all astray. He gathered his last money, 37 rubles, and went to a monastery, to an elder.

He told the elder everything.

— You must forgive. Christ commanded us to forgive 7x70 times, and what is this.

— I can't, father.

— That's just what's precious, that's what God needs, to break a hard heart. Pray. And God will soften your heart. She's a person too, after all.

— She's a bitch.

— You sin, servant of God, a great sin. She's a person. She's not glad of it herself. And you curse her. You're pushing her into sin yourself. Go home. You forgive, and you will be forgiven.

— I can't, father. Lay any penance on me you like, but I can't live at home. I've already been. As soon as I see those places, I'll sin worse.

The elder fell to thinking, closed his eyes, and whispered a prayer. He felt sorry for this man.

— If that's how it is. Go to a monastery. Live there a while. Take on an obedience.

— That I can do.

And Korney Vasiliev went first to Valaam, lived there a year, then to Solovetsky, and lived there as a laborer on the sea for 3 years, then as a baker in the monastery for 6 years. In the 7th year [^45] he went off wandering. He was at New Athos, in Kiev, at Afanasy the Seated's, and everywhere the monks did not please him, [^46] but the main thing was that he could not forgive. And he only thought of how he might show her the evil that she had done. In the Caucasus he was seized by fever, and he began to be ill. His money all ran out. He lived by begging in Christ's name. He felt that [^47] he did not have long to live, and he wanted to go home. [^48] He wanted to expose her, to show all the evil that she had done. His strength grew less and less. The fever shook him every other day. The food was poor. And as he came near the old house, he grew quite weak and thought more about how not to die without telling her everything.

## IV

He did as the young woman had told him. Having dragged himself to Zinoveyev's yard, he asked to stay the night. They let him in.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 481 -->

— [^49] You're frozen, granddad! Go on, go on up on the stove, — said [^50] the woman of the house. She was setting up the samovar in the entryway. The young man of the house, who had come into the hut just before him, also told him to climb up on the stove. Korney undressed, took off his boots, hung his footcloths opposite the stove, and climbed up onto the stove. The young woman too soon came back.

— Hasn't a wayfaring little old man come by? — she asked. — Such a feeble one, could barely walk. I told him to come to us.

— There he is, — said the man of the house, pointing to the stove, where Korney sat rubbing his legs.

When the people of the house [^51] sat down to tea, they called Korney too. He climbed down and sat on the end of the bench. They gave him a cup and a lump of sugar.

They began talking about the weather, about the harvest. The grain won't come to hand. At the landowners' the shocks have sprouted in the field. As soon as they start carting, it rains again. The peasants have carted theirs. But at the gentry's it just rots away for nothing. Korney told how he had seen along the road a whole field full of shocks. The young woman took his [^52] cup, poured for him, and handed it to him.

— Drink, grandfather, and good health to you, — she said when he refused.

— And how come your arm's not right? — he asked her.

— They broke it back in childhood.

— It's her father who wanted to kill her.

— Why's that? — asked Korney.

— Why, there was a man like that in Prysky, near us, [^53] Korney Vasiliev he was called. He got so proud against his wife. Both beat her and crippled the girl.

— Why, then? — asked Korney, glancing out from under his thick brows at the man of the house, wasn't he guessing?

— Over the workman; they said she was living with him.

— Well, and is it true?

— Who knows. About us women [^54] they'll blab all sorts, and then you answer for it, — said the old woman. [^55] — The workman was a good lad. He died in their house, too.

— Died? — the old man asked again.

— Died long ago... Maybe he did sin. Who isn't a sinner before God.

— So it's from them, then, that you took your young one?

— They've a good house. It was the first in the village, while the master was alive.

— Why, has he died?

— Dead, likely. He disappeared after that time. And nothing's been heard. It'll be 10 years or so.

— More than that, surely; Mama told me she had only just stopped nursing me.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 482 -->

— [^56] How was it he broke your arm, clever girl? — asked Korney after a silence, looking at Agafya.

— Oh, they say they got angry over something, he and Mama...

— Well, so you don't hold it against him?

— He didn't do it on purpose, did he. It was just that a sin had to happen. [^57] He's no stranger, is he?

— Why, is he your father?

— How else? Here, drink some more after the cold. Shall I pour you some?

The old man [^58] did not answer and, sobbing, wept.

— What's the matter with you?

— Nothing, just so. Christ save you. — And Korney slowly, holding on to the post and the sleeping shelf, climbed up onto the stove with his big thin legs.

## V

The next day old Korney got up before everyone. There was no fever. He climbed down from the stove, rubbed the dried footcloths soft, put on his boots on the sleeping shelf, and put on his little sack.

— Well, granddad, you might have some breakfast, — said the old woman.

— God save you. I'll be going.

— Then, [^59] at least take [^60] some of yesterday's flatcakes. I'll put them in your sack for you.

The old man thanked her and said goodbye.

— Drop in when you come back this way, if we're alive...

Outside there was that heavy autumn fog that covered everything. But Korney knew the road well, knew every hillock, and although in 15 years the switches had become trees, he knew all the bushes and the rowans and the willows along the road.

The village of Prysky was still the same, only at the edge new houses had been built that had not been there before. And wooden houses had turned into brick ones. His two-story house was the same, only it had grown old. The roof had not been painted for a long time, and at the corner bricks had been knocked out, and the porch had gone crooked.

Just as he was coming up, out of the creaking gates came a sedate mare with a foal, an old roan gelding, and a three-year-old. The old roan was the very image of the mare that Korney had brought from the fair back then. Must be that same one she had in her belly then. The same sloping rump, and the same broad chest and bony legs. A black dog jumped out and barked, but this one was even of a different breed from shaggy Volchok, whom he had left at the house. He went up to the porch, [^61] climbed onto it, and opened the doors into the entryway.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 483 --> — What are you barging in for without asking? — a voice called to him from the room. — He knew Marfa's voice. And there she was herself—old, toothless, wrinkled, leaning out of the door.

— I've come to you, Marfa.

She jumped out into the entryway and locked the door.

— What do you want with me? If it's alms, then folks ask under the window.

— Marfa. I'm Korney, — said the old man, and began to weep. And he saw by her face that she had recognized him. She was frightened and at once grew angry.

— Go on, lie some more. At your age. [^62] Babbling what you shouldn't. Korney's dead. We commemorated him in church.

— Marfa! We're going to die. — He sat down on a tub in the entryway and began to weep.

— I don't know you, — she said. — If you're Korney, then go to the volost office and report yourself. But I don't know you. Korney was a good man, and who are you?—a beggar. Go on, go on, God be with you.

At first Korney had barely recognized her—she had changed so much, but now he recognized her wholly, such as she had been then. She was still just the same.

— Well, and you've nothing to say to me?

He wished for one thing: that she would understand her guilt and confess it. She was silent, and he was silent too.

— Won't you say anything?

— I've nothing to say. I don't know you. Go, God be with you! Go on, go on.

He got up. She went back into the room and slammed the door. He got up, trudged back out onto the porch, and, holding on to the handrails, with difficulty came down from it. She watched him from the window.

## VI

Korney did not go to the headman; that same day he went back to the Zinoveyevs'. They took him in.

— What's this, didn't you go on?

— I won't make it to the saint; I'll go back.

— Well, come on, come on. We're glad of a wayfaring man.

Toward night the fever began to shake Korney, and by morning he had grown so weak that he felt he was dying. Agasha pitied him, took him out into the yard, gave him water to drink, and got him a little apple. He did not eat it.

— Gasha, [^63] — he called her in the night. — You've done me much good, thank you, clever girl. Forgive me. I'm about to die.

— What's there for me to forgive. You did me no harm.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 484 --> — Maybe I did. And another thing... Go, clever girl, to your mother, tell her that yesterday's wanderer...

— Why, have you been to her place?

— I have. So tell her that yesterday's wanderer told me [^64] to say goodbye to her, that she should forgive him. And give her this too. — He gave her his soldier's papers. — Thank her. If none of it had happened, [^65] it would have been worse for the soul. Thank her. And now, if there's a candle, give it to me. I'm dying. Thank God. I've untied all the sins. Thank God. [^66]

Agafya put his soldier's papers away in the little chest, and when she came up to him, the candle had dropped out of his hands, his eyes had stopped, and there was no breathing.

## \* No. 2 (MS. No. 3, ch. I).

At the yard Korney was met by Yevstigney Bely. Korney greeted him.

— I've come to work for you. Your missus hired me, — said Yevstigney, tossing his hair. It seemed to Korney that he was laughing. — Is this your luggage? Shall I take it out, then?

— Whose else? He asks. Carry it into the house.

His mother, with the same black eyes as Korney's, smiling joyfully, met her son. His wife too especially ~~met~~ him. She seemed to him to have grown especially pretty; he stared at her intently. This look confused her. She suddenly flushed and got angry at her two-year-old daughter, who was asking to be taken up in her arms, drove her away, and with quick steps went out into the entryway to set up the samovar. Korney handed out the presents to his mother, his wife, and the boy, who had come home from school (the lad was 8 years old; he was just as swarthy as his father), and, having undressed, over tea talked with his mother and a neighbor who had come in. He began to talk with his wife only at night, when the old woman had gone up onto the stove in the room with the Russian stove with the children and the workman, and he and his wife were left alone in the front room.

## \* No. 3 (MS. No. 4, ch. I).

Korney's mother, with the same black eyes as Korney's, shaking her head and mumbling with her lips, greeted her son cheerlessly.

His wife, a tall, broad, beautiful woman, on the contrary, seemed especially cheerful. She ran out at a trot, laughing, to meet him and, snatching the suitcase from Yevstigney, carried it into the front room herself.

She seemed to Korney to have grown especially pretty.

— Well, how are you living without me? — he said, looking at her intently.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 485 --> — Living the same as ever, — she said, and suddenly flushed, and with vexation pushed away from her the two-year-old girl, who was asking her for something to eat, and went out into the entryway.

Korney went into the front room, where he lived with his wife—undressed, untied the suitcase, and, taking out the presents, went into the big room, where his mother was getting the dishes ready for tea. He handed out the presents to his mother, his wife, and the black-eyed boy and sat down to tea.

Godfather Lipat came over for tea. Korney asked about village affairs and told about Moscow. With his wife he did not speak a word and only now and then glanced at her. Before supper she went off somewhere, and when she came back, she was red, and she smelled of liquor. Having had supper, Korney got out his notebook, sat down under the lamp, and, taking out the abacus, flicked the beads and wrote things down with the gnawed stub of pencil.

— Well, are you coming to bed soon? — said Marfa, taking the kerchief off her head and setting it straight. She stood up and went to the door. — I bet you're worn out from the road, — she said, flashing her eyes at him and smiling slightly.

— I'll come in a moment, — he said, and smiled in spite of himself, and at once got angry at himself for it and knit his brows.

— Mind you do, — she said, and he heard her quick, nimble steps along the entryway and the stairs, and her cheerful voice calling the little girl. Korney had long since finished his accounts, but he was afraid to go to her. He sat a long time, recounting what he had already counted, only to deceive himself and put off the time. When he came, the little girl was already asleep, and Marfa was sitting on the bed in just her shift, setting her braid straight.

He took off his coat and, in just his light, faded calico shirt, stopped in front of her.

## \* No. 4 (MS. No. 4, ch. I).

His anger passed into her, and instead of being frightened, submitting, denying, she, seizing the hand that held the braid, screamed at him in an angry, shrill voice, baring her white teeth:

— I live with him, so I live with him. What will you do to me? Well, kill me. I live with him, so I live with him?

— What? — he shouted, let go of the braid, and shook all over.

She looked at him with the same laughing eyes, waiting. He turned, staggering, and went out of the front room and, holding on to the handrails, went down the stairs and out onto the porch. Outside it was frosty and overcast. A light breeze was driving the hoarfrost; snowflakes fell on his burning cheeks and forehead. He knew that his wife was a spiteful woman. He saw it in her dealings with her mother-in-law, with the children, with the workmen, but he loved her beauty, was proud of it, and tried not to see, not to remember the bad that was in her. She had always been submissive to him. It had never entered his head that she could be unfaithful to him. This was so unexpected <!-- vol. 42, p. 486 --> that he did not believe what she had said. He stood a while on the porch, got his breath back, rubbed the falling hoarfrost over his heated face, took a handful of snow from the railing into his mouth, and went back to her.

## \* No. 5 (MS. No. 4, ch. I).

Having come downstairs, Korney went into the front room, dropped the hook on the door so that no one would come in to him, got out his suitcase, packed his things into it again, took out his wallet, and counted the money. There were four hundred-ruble notes and some change. He did not want to see either his mother or the children. People knocked at his door. He let no one in. Putting his coat under his head, he lay down on the bench and tried to fall asleep. And he lay like that all night. He did not sleep. He heard the women wailing upstairs, and how they then came down and went off into the other room. Through two doors their wailing could barely be heard. Then everything grew quiet. A cock crowed, a second, a third. Day began to break. He still did not sleep; he lay there in torment. In the morning he unlocked the door and called his nephew, told him to harness the horse. Only Fedka, a 10-year-old lad, rubbing his sleepy eyes, shifting from one bare foot to the other, came out onto the porch and silently watched his father—Korney nudged the mute in the side, pointing with his head at the road, and the mute drove Chubary off along the frozen, beaten road of the street.

## \* No. 6 (MS. No. 5, ch. I).

He knew that he would not master himself; a terrible anger of insulted pride was boiling in him, but sooner or later there was no getting out of going. He hung the abacus on its nail, stood up, and was about to go in to her, but heard that she was praying to God. He took off his jacket and vest and stopped, waiting. When she had finished praying and, with some words of prayer drawn in to herself, sat down on the bed, which creaked, he went into the little room.

## \* No. 7 (MS. No. 7, ch. I).

Having had supper, Korney went off to the front room, where he slept with his wife and the little daughter, and Marfa stayed to clear away the dishes in the big room. Korney sat down under the lamp and, taking out the abacus, flicked the beads, pretending that he was counting something, but he was counting nothing. He was only waiting for her, feeling how his anger at her flared up more and more. And at last, after what seemed to him a very long time, her steps were heard, the door gave a jerk, came loose, and she came in with the little girl in her arms.

— I bet you're worn out from the road, — she said, smiling slightly.

“Bitch!” thought Korney, merely glancing at her, and made no answer.

— It's not early now, — she said, glancing timidly at his terrible gray face and at once lowering her eyes. And she went behind the partition.

## \* No. 8 (MS. No. 10, ch. II).

Everything went worse and worse. He could not live without liquor. Once he had been a master himself, and now he began to hire on as a cattle drover, and then they stopped taking him even for that job. He had grown old and weak. And so, the last time, having driven the cattle to Moscow, he got his pay from the owner, he began drinking it away and, drunk, got into a fight in a tavern with factory hands. And they beat him and took him to the police station. In the station they held him with thieves and drunks for two days and nights and let him out robbed.

“Serves me right,” he thought. “She, she, the bitch, brought me to this.”

## \* No. 9 (MS. No. 11, ch. II).

On one of his journeys with the cattle, a Moscow townsman, a former drunkard, went along with him as his companion. This townsman told Korney that he used to drink, but now he had got free of it. There's a priest who talks people out of drink. Korney went to the priest. The priest read prayers over him, and Korney really did stop drinking and hired on again as a drover.

## \* No. 10 (MS. No. 11, ch. V).

And there she was herself, still strong and healthy, but a wrinkled and spiteful old woman, leaning out of the door. Korney saw her old age, and suddenly, instead of the anger toward her that he had carried in his soul so many years, he felt sorry for her. He wanted somehow to forgive her.

— If it's alms, then ask under the window.

— I'm not after alms. But look at me. Do you know me? — said Korney. And he saw by her face that she had recognized him.

She came out of the door and slammed it.

— There's plenty of your sort tramping about. How's anyone to know you all.

— And would you know Korney if he came?

— Go on, lie some more. At your age. You're babbling what you shouldn't, Korney's dead. We commemorated him in church.

— Marfa, we're going to die.

He sat down on a tub and looked at her piteously.

— I don't recognize you. If you're Korney, go to the volost office, report yourself. But I don't know you. Korney Vasilievich was a good man, and who are you—a beggar. Go on, go on, God be with you.

At first Korney had barely recognized her—she had changed so much, but now he recognized her wholly, such as she had been then. She was still just as incomprehensibly impenetrable and implacable. But then all this had been hidden from him by the beauty of her face and body; now everything could be seen bare, and it could be seen that it was hard for her.
