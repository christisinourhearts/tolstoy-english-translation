<!-- vol. 42, p. 374 -->

23 December

Wisdom is the knowledge of eternal truths applicable to life.

1

> Socrates was the first who, having brought philosophy down from the heavens, spread it among people, prompting them to study the science of life and of human morals, and the consequences of good and evil deeds.

> *Cicero.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 375 -->

2

Only by cleanliness are the vermin on the body destroyed; the same is true in human societies. Let human societies become clean and healthy in the spiritual respect, and the parasites of church and state that feed on them will vanish of themselves, as insects vanish on a clean and healthy body.

3

*Learning is rarely combined with wisdom. The learned man knows many things, for the most part unnecessary and doubtful. The wise man, on the other hand, knows few things, but all that he knows is needed by him and by people, and what he knows, he knows for certain.*

4

> He who comes to know his own soul comes to know the divine principle in himself. And having come to know the divine principle in himself, he will always act and think so as to be worthy of the divine gift he has received.

> *Cicero.*

5

He who in the Gospel cannot separate with his heart the fundamental and important from the unimportant and unnecessary will not learn this by any study of criticism. And he who can distinguish does not need this study. And the one who can distinguish is he who needs the guidance of the Gospel for life, and not for philosophizing.

6

> The clever are not learned; the learned are not clever.

> *Laozi.*

7

The people of modern science place the business of science not in what it ought to be in: in determining *what ought to be*, but in describing *what is.* What is, we all know in one way or another, and a description of it is needed by no one. People drink wine, <!-- vol. 42, p. 376 --> smoke tobacco, and science sets itself the task of justifying physiologically the use of wine and tobacco. People kill one another, take away the land or the tools of labor from everyone for the sake of a small number, and the sciences—jurisprudence and economics—justify this. People believe in absurdities, and theological science justifies this. The task of science should be the knowledge of what ought to be, and not of what is. But present-day science, on the contrary, sets itself as its main task to divert people's attention from what ought to be and to draw it to what is and what therefore no one needs to know.

The goods given by wisdom are, in comparison with all other knowledge, as important as a vessel of water in the desert is important in comparison with poods of gold.
