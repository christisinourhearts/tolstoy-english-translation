## ON THE LIBERATION OF THE LAND, ACCORDING TO THE TEACHING OF HENRY GEORGE

“The land is God's,” the people say, beginning more and more to understand that almost all their troubles come from land being able to be private property. The troubles come from this: that some people have much of it, and they do not take care to cultivate it well; they have no need to; they know that even without cultivation the land brings them income by growing dearer from year to year. Other people, however, have little of it, so little that they cannot even make proper use of it. Because of this people run off to the towns, to the factories, to the offices, everywhere beating down the price of labor, and from this come people's chief troubles.

But how is it to be done so that this does not happen and so that all people can make equal use of the land? Take it by force from those who have much of it and divide it equally among those who work it, the same way peasants divide their commune land? But even the commune land of a single village is hard to divide equally among all its inhabitants, and still harder to keep this equal division without increasing the intermingling of strips to the extreme and without hampering the running of the farms. Even now there are people in the villages who count twenty and thirty strips in each field and who, for all their wish, can do nothing sensible with their land precisely because of such a division. So how is all the land to be divided among all who want to hold it in such a way that it is equal for all? For throughout the world lands, much more than in a single village, differ in their quality. There are sandy lands worth some 15–20 rubles a desyatina; there are black-earth lands worth 300 or 400 rubles; there are water meadows worth a thousand rubles and more; there are ore-bearing lands, oil-bearing lands, lands with coal, such that are worth tens of thousands of rubles a desyatina; there are town lands, where a single sazhen is worth a thousand rubles and more. Besides that, the prices of land change. A railway goes through, the population grows somewhere, and the price of land rises.

Nor is that all. There are people who do not need land, who live in the towns: one is a craftsman, a blacksmith, a locksmith, a tailor, a joiner; one keeps an establishment; one works in a factory; one is a teacher; one is a clerk. None of these people need land for cultivation, <!-- vol. 41, p. 595 --> but all of them want to make use, equally with others, of all that the land gives.

What is to be done here? How is it to be made so that all people make equal use of what the land gives?

For this there is no need either to take the land away from those who now hold it or to divide it among everyone. Let whoever now holds any land go on holding it. Let one grow vegetable gardens, another orchards, another keep cattle, another sow grain. Let whoever has ore-bearing land extract ore from it, gold, oil, or coal. Let them all hold it as now, only let the one who holds land pay for the common benefit all that this land is worth for a year's lease. If someone's land is worth, say, as plowland, 3, 5, 10 rubles a year, or as water meadow 50, 80, 100 rubles, or as ore-bearing and town land a thousand rubles, let him pay this money every year for the common benefit. Whoever would get hundreds and thousands of rubles from his land if he let it out for a term of years without buildings and improvements, let him pay these hundreds and thousands for the common benefit; and whoever's land would be worth some five or six rubles, let him pay these five or six rubles. Let everyone pay on his land as much as it can bring in for a lease (rent) in the year in which he holds it. So that if, say, this year he pays 10–15 rubles for it, and next year there were people willing to pay 15–30 for it instead of 10–15, then if he wants to hold the land, let him pay for it what is offered for it.

If this were established, there would be enough land for everyone—enough because those who now hold lands and do not work themselves would promptly give up their lands, because they would not be able, not working on them, to pay rent for them. And these lands would be taken by those who work on the land.

This money collected from the land must go for the common benefit. This income would come to so much that it would replace all other taxes and levies. In Russia the rent from lands is some two or three times greater than what is now collected in various taxes. In Moscow alone about twenty million rubles of it would be collected. Were such an order introduced, then not only would those working on the land be well off, because there would be enough land and there would be no need to pay any duties or taxes, but for townspeople too, craftsmen, factory hands, life would be good: people would disperse to the villages, and there would no longer be, as there is now, so much surplus factory <!-- vol. 41, p. 596 --> and mill folk, of the kind that beats down the price of work; the workers would then set the prices, not the masters, and provisions would all become much cheaper, because there would be neither taxes nor duties on goods.

Under such an arrangement people would receive from the land what God himself has appointed for them to receive from it, all equally. It would not be as it was under serfdom and slavery, that some have everything and others nothing, and as it is now too, because some people can seize land for themselves and hold it as property, taking it away from those who want to work on it.

*After Henry George, retold by S. D. Nikolaev. (Edited by L. N. Tolstoy.)*
