## PRAYER

> ...Your father knows what you need before you ask...

> *Matthew 6:8.*

— No, no, and no! It cannot be... Doctor! Can nothing really be done? Why are you all silent?!

So spoke the young mother, coming out with large, resolute steps from the nursery, where her first and only three-year-old boy was dying of water on the brain.

The husband and the doctor, who had been talking quietly together, fell silent. The husband timidly went up to her, gently touched her disheveled head with his hand, and sighed heavily. The doctor stood with his head lowered, showing by his silence and stillness the hopelessness of the situation.

— What can be done! — said the husband. — What can be done, dear...

— Ah, don't speak, don't speak! — she cried out, as if angrily, reproachfully, and, turning quickly, went back to the nursery.

The husband wanted to hold her back.

— Katya! Don't go...

Without answering, she looked at him with large, tired eyes and went back into the nursery.

The boy lay on the nanny's arm, with a white pillow placed under his head. His eyes were open, but he was not looking with them.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 129 --> Foam bubbled from his closed little mouth. The nanny, with a stern, solemn face, was looking somewhere past his face and did not stir when the mother came in. When the mother came right up to her and slipped her hand under the pillow to take the child from the nanny, the nanny said quietly: “He's passing!”—and moved away from the mother. But the mother did not heed her and with a deft, practiced movement took the boy into her arms. The boy's long, curly hair had become tangled. She smoothed it and looked into his face.

— No, I can't, — she whispered, and with a quick but careful movement gave him to the nanny and left the room.

The child had been ill for the second week. During the whole time of the illness the mother had passed from despair to hope several times a day. During all this time she had slept barely an hour and a half in twenty-four hours. All this time, without ceasing, several times a day, she had gone to her bedroom, stood before a large icon of the Savior in a gold casing, and prayed to God that he would save her boy. The dark-faced Savior held in a small black hand a gilded book on which was written in niello: “Come to me, all who labor and are burdened, and I will give you rest.” Standing before this icon, she prayed, putting all the strength of her soul into her prayer. And although in the depths of her soul, even during prayer, she felt that she would not move mountains and that God would do not as she wished but in his own way, she prayed all the same, read the familiar prayers and her own, which she composed and spoke aloud with particular intensity.

Now, when she understood that he had died, she felt that something had happened in her head, as if something had broken loose and begun to spin, and, coming into her bedroom, she looked around with surprise at all her things, as if not recognizing the place. Then she lay down on the bed and fell with her head not on the pillow but on her husband's folded dressing gown, and lost consciousness.

And now in a dream she sees that her Kostya, healthy and cheerful, with his curly hair and thin white little neck, is sitting in a little armchair, swinging his little legs, plump in the calves, and, pushing out his little lips, diligently seating a boy doll on a cardboard horse that has one leg missing and a hole poked in its back.

“How good that he is alive,” she thinks. “And how cruel that he died. Why? Could God, to whom I prayed so, have allowed him to die? What does God want with this? Was he in <!-- vol. 41, p. 130 --> anyone's way? Doesn't God know that all my life is in him, that I cannot live without him? And suddenly to go and torment this unhappy, dear, innocent creature and shatter my life, and to answer all my entreaties by making his eyes stop, making him stretch out, grow cold, grow stiff.”

And again she sees. Here he comes. So small, he walks through such tall doors, swinging his little arms the way grown-ups walk. And he looks and smiles... “Darling! And God wanted to torment and kill even him! Why pray to him, then, if he can do such horrors?”

And suddenly Matryosha the girl, the nanny's helper, begins to say something very strange. The mother knows that this is Matryosha, and at the same time she is both Matryosha and an angel. “But if she is an angel, why has she no wings on her back?” the mother thinks. However, she remembers that someone—she does not remember who, but someone deserving of trust—had told her that nowadays angels are sometimes without wings too. And the angel-Matryosha says: “You have no cause, madam, to take offense at God. He can't possibly listen to everyone. They often ask for such things that if you do it for one, you wrong another. Right now they're praying all over Russia, and what people! The very foremost bishops, monks in cathedrals, in churches over relics, all are praying that God give victories over the Japanese. But is that a good thing, now? It isn't fitting to pray about that, and besides, he can't please anybody. The Japanese are praying too, that they may win. And he's the one father we all have. What is he to do?”

— What is he to do, ma'am? — says Matryosha.

— Yes, that's so. That's old. Voltaire said that long ago. Everyone knows it and everyone says it. That's not what I mean. But why can't he grant a request when I ask not for something harmful but only that he not kill my dear boy. I can't live without him, you know, — says the mother, and she feels him put his plump little arms around her neck, and with her body she feels his warm little body. — How good that it didn't happen, — she thinks.

— But it's not only that, ma'am, — Matryosha keeps on, as muddle-headedly as always, — it's not only that. It happens that even one person asks, and yet it's quite impossible to do for him what he wants. We know all about that. I know, you see, because I make the reports, — says Matryosha-angel in exactly the same voice in which yesterday, when the mistress sent her to the master, she said <!-- vol. 41, p. 131 --> to the nanny: “I know the master is at home, because I made the report.”

— How many times I've had to report, — says Matryosha, — that here's a good man—one of the young ones—who mostly asks to be helped not to do bad deeds, not to drink, not to lead a dissolute life, asks for the vice to be taken out of him like a splinter.

“How well Matryosha talks, though,” thinks the mistress.

— But he can't possibly do that, because everyone has to make the effort himself. Only from effort does any good come. You yourself, ma'am, gave me the tale of the black hen to read. It tells how a black hen gave a boy, because he had saved her from death, a magic hempseed, such that, as long as it lay in his trouser pocket, he knew all his lessons without studying, and how because of this very seed he stopped studying altogether and lost his memory. He can't, our father, take evil out of people. And they shouldn't ask for it, but tear it out, wash it out, wrench it out of themselves.

“Where does she know these words from,” thinks the mistress, and says:

— All the same, Matryosha, you are not answering my question.

— Give me time, I'll tell everything, — says Matryosha. — And sometimes it's like this too: I report that a family has been ruined through no fault of its own, they're all weeping, instead of good rooms they live in a corner, they haven't even got tea, they ask to be helped somehow or other. And again he can't possibly do it their way, because he knows that it's for their own good. They don't see it, but he, our father, knows that if they lived in plenty, they would get spoiled rotten.

“That's true,” thinks the mistress. “But why does she speak so vulgarly about God? ‘Rotten’... that's not nice at all. I'll be sure to tell her when the chance comes”...

— But that's not what I'm asking about, — the mother repeats again. — I'm asking: why, for what did this God of yours want to take my boy from me? — And the mother sees before her her Kostya alive and listens to his laugh, like a little bell, ringing, childlike, his own special, dear laugh. — Why did they take him from me? If God could do this, then he is a wicked, bad God, and there's no need of him at all, and I don't want to know him.

And what is this: Matryosha is no longer Matryosha at all, but some quite different, new, strange, indistinct being, <!-- vol. 41, p. 132 --> and this being speaks not aloud with its lips but in some special way, straight into the mother's heart.

— You pitiful, blind, and insolent, conceited creature, — says this being. — You see your Kostya as he was a week ago, with his sturdy, springy limbs and long curly hair and his naive, affectionate, and sensible speech. But was he always like that? There was a time when you rejoiced that he could say “mama” and “baba” and understood who was who; and before that you were delighted that he stood upright and, swaying, ran softly on his little feet across to a chair; and before that you all were delighted that he crawled about the drawing room like a little animal; and before that you rejoiced that he recognized you, that he held up his hairless little head with its breathing soft spot; and before that you were delighted that he took the nipple and pressed it with his toothless gums. And before that you rejoiced that he, all red and not yet separated from you, cried pitifully, breaking in his lungs. And before that, a year earlier, where was he, when he did not exist at all? You all think that you stand still and that you and those you love ought always to be as they are now. But you do not stand still for a single minute; you all flow like a river, you all fly like a stone downward, toward death, which, sooner or later, awaits all of you. How is it you do not understand that if out of nothing he had become what he was, then he would not have stopped and would not have remained for a single minute as he was when he died; but as out of nothing he became a suckling, and from a suckling became a child, so from a child he would have become a schoolboy, a youth, a young man, grown, aging, old. You do not know, you see, what he would have been if he had remained alive. But I know.

And now the mother sees, in a private room of a restaurant brightly lit with electricity (once her husband had taken her to such a restaurant), before a table with the remains of supper, she sees a puffy, wrinkled old man with his mustache drawn up at the ends, repulsive, trying to look young. He sits sunk deep in a soft sofa, and with drunken eyes greedily looks over a depraved, painted woman with a bare, white, fat neck, and with a drunken tongue shouts out, repeating it several times, an indecent joke, evidently pleased by the approving guffaws of another couple just like him.

— It's not true, it's not he, it's not my Kostya! — the mother cries out, looking with horror at the vile old man, who is horrible precisely <!-- vol. 41, p. 133 --> because there is something in his look, in his lips, that recalls what was Kostya's own. “Good that it's a dream,” she thinks. “The real Kostya—here he is.” And she sees Kostya, little and white and naked, with his plump little chest, as he sits in the bath and, laughing, kicks his little legs; she not only sees but feels how he suddenly seizes her arm, bare to the elbow, and kisses it, kisses it, and at last bites it, not knowing what else he could do with this hand so dear to him.

“Yes, this is Kostya, and not that horrible old man,” she says to herself. And at these words she wakes and with horror recognizes the reality from which there is no longer anywhere to wake.

She goes into the nursery. The nanny has already washed Kostya and laid him out. With a waxen, thinned little nose, with dimples by the nostrils and his fine hair smoothed back from his forehead, he lies on some sort of raised stand. Around him candles are burning, and on a little table at his head stand white, lilac, and pink hyacinths. The nanny rises from her chair and, raising her eyebrows and pursing her lips, looks at the stone-still little face turned upward. Through the other door Matryosha comes in to meet the mother, with her simple, good-natured face and tear-stained eyes.

“How is it she told me one mustn't grieve, and yet she herself was weeping,” thinks the mother. And she turns her gaze to the dead child. In the first moment she is struck and repelled by the terrible likeness of the dead little face to the face of the old man whom she had seen in her dream, but she drives this thought away and, crossing herself, touches her warm lips to the cold, waxen little forehead, then kisses the folded, chilled little hands, and suddenly the scent of the hyacinths seems to tell her something new about the fact that he is gone and will never be again, and sobs choke her, and she kisses him once more on the forehead, and for the first time she weeps. She weeps, but weeps not hopeless but submissive, tender tears. It hurts her, but she no longer rebels, no longer complains, but knows that what was had to be, and therefore was good.

— It's a sin, mother, to weep, — says the nanny, and, going up to the little dead one, she wipes with a folded handkerchief the mother's tears left on Kostya's waxen forehead. — Tears will weigh on his little soul. He's well now. A sinless little angel. And had he lived, who knows what would have been.

— True, true, but all the same it hurts, it hurts! — says the mother.

*Leo Tolstoy.*
