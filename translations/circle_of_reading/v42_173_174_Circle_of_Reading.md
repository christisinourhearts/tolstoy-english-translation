<!-- vol. 42, p. 173 -->

26 October

For the moral life the importance of a deed is measured not by the material significance of the deed and its possible consequences, but only by the degree of good effort.

1

> Most people, wishing to correct their life, wish rather to accomplish something difficult and extraordinary than to purify their desires and renounce self-indulgence in the ordinary duties of their position. And yet the latter is far more important than the former.

> *After Fénelon.*

2

> He who does not carry out the deed that he feels he ought to do, only because it seems to him too small, deceives himself. He does not do it not because it is too small, but because it is too great for him.

> *Pusey.*

3

> You are not obliged to carry the work through to the end, but neither must you shirk it.

> He who has entrusted the work to you is trustworthy.

> *The Talmud.*

4

> If a person does not consider himself called to carry out a commission, an embassy, he cannot be an enlightened person.

> *Chinese wisdom.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 174 -->

5

> *A person comes to know himself not by thinking, but by acting. Only in efforts to do what ought to be done does he learn his own worth.*

> *Goethe.*

6

To transfer one's *I* from the bodily to the spiritual means consciously to desire only the spiritual. My body may desire what is bodily, but the soul does not desire and does not want to desire anything bodily, yet it cannot leave the body, just as I would not wish to be drawn to the earth, but whatever I do, wherever I am, I will not get away from this. And just as, in spite of the fact that my body is ceaselessly drawn to the earth, I separate myself from it, move, walk, jump—in this is my bodily life—so it is with the soul and the body. The body ceaselessly draws me to itself, and I separate myself from it, even make use of it, live spiritually. In this is my true life.

Nothing so harms moral perfecting as disregard for deeds that are considered unimportant in the worldly sense.
