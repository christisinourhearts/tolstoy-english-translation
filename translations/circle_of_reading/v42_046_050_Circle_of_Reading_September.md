## PETR CHELČICKÝ

There exists, written more than 450 years ago by an unlearned man, Petr from the village of Chelčice, a book that is almost completely unknown.

In this book, entitled “The Net of Faith,” we find not only a simple, clear, strong, and truthful exposure of that terrible deception in which people have lived and still live, believing in what is most alien to true Christianity and imagining that they profess the Christian teaching; we also find in this book a clear indication of that one good path of life which was opened to people by Christ.

Every truth of life that is to serve as a guide for people's conduct, even if it appears in the consciousness of holy people at once, in all its fullness, appears in most people slowly, gradually, imperceptibly, by fits and starts, sometimes seeming to hide completely and appearing again through new efforts like the throes of birth.

So it was, and so it still happens now with Christianity. The Christian truth was at first accepted in all its meaning by a small number of simple, unimportant, not rich people. But as it spread among a great number of people, and among rich and noble people, it was more and more perverted, and since the founding of the church (since the times of Constantine, as Chelčický says) it was so perverted that its chief true meaning for life was completely hidden from people and replaced by outward forms alien to the essence of Christianity.

But a truth that has entered the consciousness of people cannot die out. Outside the church, in what churchmen called heresies, there always remained faithful understanders and fulfillers of the true Christian teaching. And again and again there took place ever new throes of its rebirth. And each time a greater and greater number of people became partakers of the Christian truth in its real meaning.

Such a faithful understander and reviver of the Christian truth was Chelčický. Chelčický's chief work, “The Net of Faith,” is an indication of what Christian society should be according to the teaching of its founder, and of what it has become under the perverted teaching.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 47 --> Here is what is said in the preface to the book:

This book, which bears the title “The Net of Faith,” was composed by Petr of Chelčice, who lived in the time of Master Rokycana, knew him well, and often talked with him. He wrote many useful books on the law of God for the advancement of the church in the struggle against Antichrist and his delusions, and if this book has until now seen little of the light, the cause of this was the clergy, who did not cease and do not cease to represent the books of Petr Chelčický to the people as erring and heretical, and all because he condemns their way of life. For all that, many people of all estates willingly read both this book of Petr Chelčický's and his other writings, notwithstanding that he was a layman and not learned in Latin, because, although he was not a master of the seven arts, he was in truth a fulfiller of the nine beatitudes and of all the commandments of God, and was thus a real Czech doctor. In this book Chelčický touches on all estates, beginning with emperors, kings, princes, lords, knights, townspeople, and craftsmen, and ending with the rural estate; but he turns particular attention to the clergy: to the popes, cardinals, bishops, archbishops, abbots, and all the monks of the orders, deans, parish rectors, and vicars. The first part of this book sets out by what way and means a terrible corruption penetrated into the holy church, and proves that only by removing from the church all human inventions can one get to its true foundation—Jesus Christ; the second speaks of the rise and multiplication in the church of various estates, which only hinder the true knowledge of Christ, for they are filled with the spirit of pride and with all their strength resist the humble and meek Christ.”

And indeed, Chelčický, in this book as in his other writings, does not dispute the papal institutions and dogmas of the church, as did his predecessor Hus and, living and acting after him, Luther, Melanchthon, Calvin; he only shows that the life of people who consider themselves Christians is not Christian; that a Christian cannot make use of power, cannot own lands or slaves, cannot live in luxury, cannot live a dissolute life, cannot execute, cannot, above all, kill and wage war.

Chelčický does not argue about salvation by works or by faith, about predestination, and about dogmas in general; he demands only that <!-- vol. 42, p. 48 --> all the ordinances of the church be accessible to the understanding of the people. He does not reject them, but speaks of the life of Christians, shows that earthly rulers, the army, the courts, and the nobility are incompatible with Christian life (he even considers the town estate incompatible with Christianity). Above all, he shows that executions and wars are unthinkable for a Christian. He shows that the union of Christianity with the state—the one that has now taken place—has ruined, destroyed Christianity, but that it should be the other way around: Christianity, uniting with the state, should destroy the state.

And he proves that this is possible, that the absence of state power not only does not destroy order in people's life, but destroys the disorder and evil from which people suffer.

In this lies the cause of the obscurity of Chelčický's book and activity. Chelčický's book and activity occupy in the sphere of Christian humanity the same position that Christianity occupies in the sphere of all humanity. It is too far ahead of its time. The season of its fruits has not yet come. The abolition of papal authority, of indulgences, and much else that was done by Luther was within the powers of the people of his time, but what Chelčický said could not be accepted, not because it is unclear or unjust—everything he said, on the contrary, is too clear and just—but because what he said was too far ahead of its time.

What Chelčický demanded cannot be accepted even now; all the less could it be accepted in his time. To refute what Chelčický said was impossible; at least people then were still honest enough to consider it impossible to deny that Christ taught what he taught, that is, that people should love not only those who love them but their enemies, bear offenses, repay evil with good, and consider all people brothers, and that such a teaching is incompatible with the existing structure of life. And therefore the question inevitably arose: what to keep, Christianity or the established structure?

If Christianity is kept, then it is clear that those who have power must give up power, the rich give up wealth, those in the middle give up securing themselves by violence, the poor and the subject give up obedience to what is contrary to the Christian law (and in the state all public activity <!-- vol. 42, p. 49 --> is contrary to the Christian law), and therefore subject themselves to persecution. And all this is frightening.

But if the existing structure is kept, knowing that it is un-Christian, this means renouncing Christianity. And this too is frightening. What then remained to be done? One thing: to forget what Christ said, what Chelčický said, and what conscience said: and not to think, not to speak about it.

In this is the cause of the obscurity of Chelčický and his book.

The book was hushed up, forgotten. If some ten scholars know of it, they look on it only as a historical, literary monument.

But the spiritual riches of humanity never perish, but only ripen, like hard fruits. And the longer they wait for their time, the more valuable they are. So it is with Chelčický and his book.

His book was recently, and for the first time, printed by the Russian Academy of Sciences, and no one, of course, has read it, or even heard of it, as with everything that is printed with such great expense and such importance in the publications of the Academy. The works of Nietzsche, Zola, Verlaine are printed in dozens of editions and hundreds of thousands of copies. Everyone knows the slightest details of these people's lives, but the books of Chelčický have still not been printed, even in Bohemia and in Germany, not to speak of England and France.

And about Chelčický himself almost nothing is known. It is supposed that he was born around 1390 and died around 1450. Some think that he was a nobleman, others that he was a peasant, a shoemaker, or a farmer. I think that he was a farmer.

That he was a farmer, a peasant, I conclude, first, from the strong, simple, clear language of the book; second, from the wisdom of the book, owing to which the author always knows what is important and what is less important, and always puts the important in the first place; third, from the warmth of heart and the naivety with which he speaks, sometimes like a peasant, roughly and strongly, with indignation, sometimes with bitter mockery, about what obviously pains his soul.

“The Net of Faith” is an old book in time, but in its significance and content it is the newest book, so new that the people of our time are still far from being prepared by true enlightenment to be able to understand it. But its time will come and is coming.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 50 --> For Christianity is not a human invention, not one of the temporary forms into which human societies take shape, but the truth—which, if it did not appear on stone tablets on Sinai, is written still more firmly than on stone, on the hearts of all people. And once it has been expressed, nothing can any longer be scratched out of the consciousness of people. This truth has waited and will wait still longer, but from this it will only become more evident and will only more insistently demand its fulfillment.

It is impossible to strike out of Christianity that Christians, as Chelčický says, must “not be participants in worldly wisdom,” must not be officials, judges, military men, but must bear all injustices humbly, patiently, not repaying evil for evil, not murmuring and not taking revenge. However much people have tried and still try to talk these truths away—these truths remain truths and, through all the sophistries devised over the centuries to hide them, continue to seize people's hearts directly, immediately.

What is to be done? Until now the dilemma has been resolved by hushing up Christianity or lying crudely about it, and keeping the state.

But people will not escape trying the other, opposite solution as well: to give up the state and give themselves to Christianity.

And this solution will be all the more sensible because all states, with their violent structure, have so far not only not given the good things they promised, but, on the contrary, more and more increase the calamities that people bear, and people more and more lose faith in them.

It is precisely this new and good solution that this wise, heartfelt, and needed book of Chelčický's promotes.

Several excerpts from it are placed in the weekly readings of “Circle of Reading.”

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
