<!-- vol. 41, p. 75 -->

1 February

No reasonings can reduce the spiritual to the material and explain the origin of the spiritual from the material.

1

> *A person considers both his body and his soul to be himself. But a person always cares, especially in youth, only about the body. And yet the main thing in every person is not the body but the soul. And therefore one must care most of all not about the body but about the soul. Accustom yourself to this, remember more often that your life is in the spirit, keep it from all the dirt of everyday life, do not let the flesh suppress it, subordinate the body to the spirit, and then you will fulfill your purpose and live your life joyfully.*

> *After Marcus Aurelius.*

2

> The whole matter is this: to believe or not to believe in the reality of the spirit. People are divided, in the spiritual respect, into the living and the dead, that is, into believers and unbelievers.

> The unbeliever says: “What spirit is there... But what I have eaten and enjoyed, that is mine!” And he, without thinking much, cares only about the external, doing his carnal and evil deeds, lies, exalts himself, plays the slave, and does not feel in himself the higher needs: freedom, truth, love. Such a person hides from the light of reason, because he is dead and because light gives life only to the living, but dries up and rots what is dead.

> The believer in the spiritual life turns his attention inward, tries to sort out his feelings, his thoughts, tries to direct his life in accordance with the higher demands: to make it free, truthful, loving; tries by his actions to build his life out of such thoughts and feelings as most correspond to the aims of good. Such a person seeks the truth and is drawn toward the light, because the life of the spirit is impossible without the light of reason, as the life of the visible world is impossible without the light of the sun.

> Among people there are neither perfect dwellers in darkness nor perfect dwellers in light, but all are at the crossroads, and each, having in himself the power to go, goes this way or that. And everyone who believes in the reality of the spirit, who lives under the light of reason, abides in the kingdom of God and has eternal life.

> *Buka.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 76 --> Faith in the reality of the spiritual life gives a different direction to a person's thoughts.

3

> Let learned men, philosophers, invent their predeterminations, their necessary motions; let them think that the world came into being from a series of chance events. I see in the world that unity of design which, in spite of their assertions, compels me to acknowledge a single principle. It is all the same as if they told me that the Iliad had been composed from printer's type thrown down at random. I would say to them without hesitation: that is not true, although I have no other reason not to believe it except that *I cannot believe it.*

> “All this is superstition,” say the learned men. Perhaps it is superstition—I answer—yet what can your so unclear reason do against a superstition that is more convincing than it?

> You say: “There are not two principles, the spiritual and the bodily.” I say that there is nothing in common between my thought and a tree.

> And what is most amusing of all is that they themselves demolish one another with their sophisms and are ready to ascribe a soul to stones sooner than recognize it in man.

> *Rousseau.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 77 -->

4

> I do not know whether a dog can choose, remember, love, fear, imagine, think; so that when I am told that all this in it is not passions, not feelings, but the natural and necessary action of the structure of its organism, which is composed of various combinations of parts of matter, then I can agree with such an opinion. But I think, and I know that I think. What, then, can there be in common between that which thinks and this or that combination of parts of matter, that is, of space divisible in all its directions and dimensions—in length, width, and depth?

> *La Bruyère.*

5

> If everything is only matter and if thought in me, as in all people, is only a consequence of combinations of particles of matter, then who engendered in the world the thought of any beings other than bodily ones? How can matter be the cause of that which denies it and excludes it from its existence? How can it be, in a person, that which thinks, that is, the very thing that serves a person as the conviction that he is not matter?

> *La Bruyère.*

6

> Metaphysics exists in reality, if not as a science, then as a natural inclination, because human reason, advancing irresistibly forward, impelled to it not only by the vain desire to know a great deal but also by its own need, arrives at such questions as no empirical activity of reason, and no foundations derived from it, can answer. Thus all people in whom reason has expanded to speculation have really always had some metaphysics or other; and they will always have it.

> *Kant.*

The distinction of the spiritual from the material is equally clear to the simplest, childlike mind and to the deepest mind of a sage. Reasonings and disputes about the spiritual and the material are useless. These reasonings will explain nothing, but will only obscure what is clear and indisputable.
