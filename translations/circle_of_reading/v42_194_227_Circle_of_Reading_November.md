## THE DIVINE AND THE HUMAN

## I

This was in the '70s in Russia, at the very height of the revolutionaries' struggle with the government.

The governor-general of the southern region, a healthy German with mustaches that drooped downward, a cold gaze, and an expressionless <!-- vol. 42, p. 195 --> face, in a military frock coat, with a white cross at his neck, was sitting in the evening in his study at a table with four candles in green shades, looking through and signing the papers left for him by the head of his chancellery. “Adjutant General So-and-so,” he would trace out with a long flourish, and lay the paper aside.

Among the papers was the sentence condemning Anatoly Svetlogub, a candidate of Novorossiysk University, to the death penalty by hanging for taking part in a conspiracy having as its aim the overthrow of the existing government. The general, frowning particularly, signed this one too. With his white, well-tended fingers, wrinkled from age and soap, he neatly evened the edges of the sheets and laid them aside. The next paper concerned the allotment of sums for the transport of provisions. He was reading this paper attentively, thinking about whether the sums had been calculated correctly or incorrectly, when suddenly he remembered his conversation with his assistant about the Svetlogub case. The general held that the dynamite found at Svetlogub's did not yet prove his criminal intent. His assistant, however, insisted that besides the dynamite there was much evidence proving that Svetlogub was the head of a gang. And, remembering this, the general fell to thinking, and under his frock coat, with its cotton wadding on the chest and its lapels stiff as cardboard, his heart began to beat unevenly, and he began to breathe so heavily that the large white cross, the object of his joy and pride, stirred on his chest. It was still possible to bring back the head of the chancellery and, if not revoke the sentence, then postpone it.

“Bring him back? Not bring him back?”

His heart beat still more unevenly. He rang. With a quick, noiseless step the messenger came in.

— Has Ivan Matveyevich left?

— No, sir, Your High Excellency, he was pleased to step into the chancellery.

The general's heart now stopped, now gave quick jolts. He remembered the warning of the doctor who had listened to his heart the other day.

“The main thing,” the doctor had said, “as soon as you feel that you have a heart, stop working, amuse yourself. Agitation is worst of all. On no account let yourself come to that.”

— Would you have me call him?

— No, no need, — said the general. “Yes,” he said to himself, “indecision agitates worst of all. Signed <!-- vol. 42, p. 196 --> and done. ‘Ein jeder macht sich sein Bett und muss drauf schlafen,’ [^1]” he said to himself, his favorite proverb. “And besides, this is no concern of mine. I am the executor of a higher will and must stand above such considerations,” he added, drawing his brows together so as to call up in himself the cruelty that was not in his heart.

And at once he remembered his last meeting with the sovereign: how the sovereign, putting on a stern face and fixing his glassy gaze on him, had said: “I rely on you: just as you did not spare yourself in the war, you will act just as resolutely in the struggle with the Reds—you will let yourself be neither deceived nor frightened. Farewell!” And the sovereign, embracing him, offered him his shoulder to kiss. The general remembered this, and how he had answered the sovereign: “My one desire is to give my life to the service of my sovereign and fatherland.”

And, remembering the feeling of servile tender emotion he had experienced at the consciousness of his self-sacrificing devotion to his sovereign, he drove away the thought that had troubled him for a moment, signed the rest of the papers, and rang once more.

— Is tea served? — he asked.

— They are serving it now, Your High Excellency.

— Good, you may go.

The general sighed deeply and, rubbing with his hand the place where his heart was, went with a heavy step out into the large, empty hall and, across the freshly polished parquet of the hall, into the drawing room, from which voices could be heard.

The general's wife had guests: the governor and his wife, an old princess, a great patriot, and an officer of the Guards, the fiancé of the general's last, unmarried daughter.

The general's wife, a dry woman with a cold face and thin lips, sitting at a low table on which stood a tea service with a silver teapot on a warmer, was telling, in a falsely sad tone, a stout lady who was trying to look young, the governor's wife, about her anxiety for her husband's health.

— Every day more and more new reports uncover conspiracies and all sorts of terrible things... And all this falls on Basile—he has to decide everything.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 197 --> — Ah, don't speak of it! — said the princess. — Je deviens féroce quand je pense à cette maudite engeance. [^2]

— Yes, yes, it's terrible! Would you believe it, he works twelve hours a day, and with his weak heart. I'm simply afraid...

She did not finish, seeing her husband come in.

— Yes, you really must hear him. Barbini is an amazing tenor, — she said of the newly arrived singer, smiling pleasantly at the governor's wife, as naturally as if that was all they had been talking about.

The general's daughter, a pretty, plump girl, was sitting with her fiancé in the far corner of the drawing room, behind little Chinese screens. She got up and, together with her fiancé, came over to her father.

— How do you like that, we haven't even seen each other today! — said the general, kissing his daughter and shaking her fiancé's hand.

Having greeted the guests, the general sat down at the little table and got into conversation with the governor about the latest news.

— No, no, no talking about business—it's forbidden! — the general's wife interrupted the governor. — And here, by the way, is Kopyev; he'll tell us something cheerful. Good evening, Kopyev.

And Kopyev, a well-known merrymaker and wit, did indeed tell the latest joke, which made everyone laugh.

## II

— Oh no, this cannot be, it cannot, it cannot! Let me go! — Svetlogub's mother was shouting, shrieking, tearing herself out of the hands of a gymnasium teacher, her son's comrade, and of the doctor, who were trying to hold her back.

Svetlogub's mother was a pretty woman, not old, with graying curls and little wrinkles running in a star from her eyes. The teacher, Svetlogub's comrade, having learned that the death sentence had been signed, had wanted to prepare her cautiously for the terrible news, but he had only just begun to speak about her son when she, from the tone of his voice, from the timidity of his look, guessed that what she had feared had happened.

This was taking place in a small room of the best hotel in the city.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 198 --> — But why are you holding me? Let me go! — she was shouting, tearing herself away from the doctor, an old friend of their family, who with one hand held her by her thin elbow and with the other set a little bottle of drops on the oval table in front of the sofa. She was glad they were holding her, because she felt that she had to do something, but what—she did not know, and she was afraid of herself.

— Calm yourself. Here, drink some valerian drops, — the doctor was saying, handing her a cloudy liquid in a small glass.

She suddenly went quiet and almost doubled over, letting her head sink on her hollow chest, and, closing her eyes, sank down onto the sofa.

And she remembered how her son, three months before, had taken leave of her with a mysterious and sad face. Then she remembered an eight-year-old boy in a little velvet jacket, with bare little legs and long curling ringlets of fair hair.

“And him, him, this very boy... they will do that to him!”

She jumped up, pushed the table away, and tore herself out of the doctor's hands. On reaching the door, she fell into an armchair again.

— And they say—there is a God! What kind of God is he, if he allows this! The devil take him, this God! — she shouted, now sobbing, now going off into hysterical laughter. — They'll hang, they'll hang the one who gave up everything, all his career, gave all his fortune to others, to the people, gave everything, — she was saying, she who had always reproached her son for this before but now set before herself the merit of his self-renunciation. — And him, him, they'll do this to him! And you say there is a God! — she cried out.

— But I'm not saying anything, I'm only asking you to drink the drops.

— I don't want anything. Ha-ha-ha! — she laughed and sobbed, drinking her fill of her despair.

By night she was so worn out that she could no longer either speak or weep, but only stared before her with a fixed, mad look. The doctor injected her with morphine, and she fell asleep.

Her sleep was without dreams, but the awakening was still more terrible. Most terrible of all was that people could be so cruel—not only these terrible generals with shaven cheeks, and the gendarmes, but everyone, everyone: the corridor maid, who came with a calm face to tidy the room, and the neighbors in the next room, who met cheerfully and laughed about something, as if nothing had happened.

## III

Svetlogub had been in solitary confinement for the second month and in that time had lived through a great deal.

From childhood Svetlogub had unconsciously felt the wrongness of his exceptional position as a rich man, and although he tried to stifle this consciousness in himself, often, when he encountered the need of the people, and sometimes simply when he himself felt especially good and joyful, he would feel ashamed on account of those people—peasants, old men, women, children—who were born, grew up, and died not only without knowing all those joys that he enjoyed without valuing them, but who also never got out of strenuous labor and want. When he finished the university, in order to free himself from this consciousness of being in the wrong, he started a school in his village, a model school, a consumers' cooperative shop, and a home for destitute old men and old women. But, strange to say, while occupied with these things he felt still far more ashamed before the people than when he had supper with his comrades or acquired an expensive saddle horse. He felt that all this was not the thing, and worse than not the thing: there was something bad here, something morally unclean.

In one of these states of disappointment with his activity in the village he went to Kiev and met one of his closest comrades from the university. Three years after this meeting this comrade was shot in the moat of the Kiev fortress.

This comrade, an ardent, impulsive man of enormous gifts, drew him into taking part in a society whose aim consisted in enlightening the people, awakening in them the consciousness of their rights, and forming among them united circles striving to free themselves from the power of the landowners and the government. Conversations with this man and his friends brought, as it were, into clear consciousness all that until then had been vaguely felt by Svetlogub. He understood now what he had to do. Without breaking off relations with his new comrades, he went away to the village and began an entirely new activity there. He himself became a schoolteacher, set up classes for adults, read books and pamphlets to them, explained their situation to the peasants; besides that, he published illegal books and pamphlets for the people and gave everything he could, without taking it from his mother, to setting up similar centers in other villages.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 200 --> From the very first steps of this new activity Svetlogub met with two unexpected obstacles: one was that the majority of the common people were not only indifferent to his preaching but looked on him almost with contempt. (Only exceptional individuals understood him and sympathized with him, and often people of doubtful morality.) The other obstacle came from the government. The school was forbidden him, and searches were made at his place and at the places of people close to him, and books and papers were taken away.

Svetlogub paid little attention to the first obstacle—the indifference of the people—since he was too indignant at the second obstacle: the government's oppressions, senseless and insulting. His comrades experienced the same thing in their activity in other places too, and the feeling of irritation against the government, mutually inflamed, reached such a point that the greater part of this circle decided to fight the government by force.

The head of this circle was a certain Mezhenetsky, a man, as everyone considered him, of unshakable strength of will, invincible logic, and wholly devoted to the cause of the revolution.

Svetlogub submitted to the influence of this man and, with the same energy with which he had formerly worked among the people, gave himself up to terrorist activity.

This activity was dangerous, but it was precisely this danger that attracted Svetlogub most of all.

He said to himself: “Victory or martyrdom, and if martyrdom, then martyrdom is the same victory, only in the future.” And the fire that had flared up in him not only did not go out during the seven years of his revolutionary activity, but blazed up more and more, sustained by the love and respect of the people among whom he moved.

To the fact that he had given almost all his fortune—the fortune that had come to him from his father—to this cause he attached no importance, nor did he attach any to the labors and the want that he often endured in this activity. One thing only grieved him: the grief that by this activity he was causing his mother and that girl, her ward, who lived with his mother and loved him.

Lately a terrorist comrade whom he did not much like and found disagreeable, who was being hunted by the police, had asked him to hide some dynamite at his place. Svetlogub agreed without hesitation precisely because he did not like this comrade, and the next day in Svetlogub's apartment <!-- vol. 42, p. 201 --> a search was made and the dynamite was found. To all questions as to how and where he had obtained the dynamite, Svetlogub refused to answer.

And so the martyrdom that he had expected began for him. Lately, when so many of his friends had been executed, imprisoned, exiled, when so many women had suffered, Svetlogub almost desired martyrdom. And in the first minutes of his arrest and the interrogations he felt a peculiar excitement, almost joy.

He experienced this feeling when they undressed him, searched him, and when they led him into the prison and locked the iron door behind him. But when a day had passed, a second, a third, when a week had passed, a second, a third, in a dirty, damp cell full of insects, in solitude and enforced idleness, interrupted only by knocking back and forth with fellow prisoners, who passed on nothing but unkind and joyless news, and now and then by interrogations by cold, hostile people trying to extract from him accusations against his comrades, his moral strength, together with his physical strength, kept weakening, and he only pined and wished, as he said to himself, for some end to this tormenting situation. His anguish was increased still more by his having come to doubt his own strength. In the second month of his confinement he began to catch himself thinking of telling the whole truth, if only to be set free. He was horrified at his weakness, but no longer found his former strength in himself, and he hated and despised himself and pined still more.

The most terrible thing of all was that in confinement he came to feel so sorry for those young energies and joys that he had sacrificed so easily while he was at liberty, and that now seemed to him so enchanting, that he repented of what he had considered good, repented sometimes of his whole activity. Thoughts came to him of how happily, how well he could have lived in freedom—in the village, at liberty, abroad, among beloved and loving friends. To marry her, or perhaps another, and live with her a simple, joyful, bright life.

## IV

On one of the tormentingly monotonous days of the second month of his confinement, the warden, on his usual round, handed Svetlogub a little book with a gilt cross on a brown binding, saying that the governor's wife had visited the prison and left Gospels, which it was permitted to give to the prisoners.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 202 --> Svetlogub thanked him and smiled slightly, laying the little book on the small table screwed to the wall.

When the warden had gone, Svetlogub talked it over by knocking with his neighbors: the warden had come and had said nothing new, but had only brought the Gospel; and the neighbor answered that he had brought him one too.

After dinner Svetlogub opened the shabby little book, whose leaves had stuck together from the damp, and began to read. Svetlogub had never yet read the Gospel as a book. All he knew of it was what the scripture teacher had gone through at the gymnasium and what priests and deacons chanted in church.

“Chapter one. The genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham, Isaac begot Jacob, Jacob begot Judah”... he read. “Zerubbabel begot Abiud,” he went on reading. All this was just what he had expected: some sort of tangled nonsense, of no use for anything. If it had not been in prison, he could not have read through one page, but here he went on reading for the process of reading: “like Gogol's Petrushka,” he thought to himself. He read the first chapter, about the birth from a virgin and about the prophecy, which consisted in this, that they would give the one born the name Emmanuel, which means: “God with us.” “And where is the prophecy in that?” he thought, and went on reading. He read the second chapter too, about the walking star, and the third, about John feeding on dragonflies, and the fourth, about some devil offering Christ a gymnastic exercise from the roof. All this seemed to him so uninteresting that, in spite of the boredom of prison, he was already about to close the book and begin his usual evening occupation—catching fleas in the shirt he had taken off—when suddenly he remembered that at the examination in the fifth class of the gymnasium he had forgotten one of the commandments of the Beatitudes, and the rosy-faced, curly-haired priest had suddenly gotten angry and given him a two. He could not remember which commandment this was, and he read the Beatitudes. “Blessed are those driven out for the truth, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven,” he read. “That, perhaps, applies to us too,” he thought. “Blessed are you when they revile and persecute you. Rejoice and be glad: so they persecuted the prophets too, who were before you.” “You are the salt of the earth. If the salt loses its strength, what will you make it salty with? It is no longer good for anything, except perhaps to be thrown out to be trampled underfoot by people.”

“This really does apply to us,” he thought, and read on further. Having read the whole fifth chapter, he fell to thinking. “Do not be angry, do not commit adultery, endure evil, love enemies.”

<!-- vol. 42, p. 203 --> “Yes, if everyone lived like that,” he thought, “there would be no need of revolution either.” Reading further, he entered more and more into the meaning of those passages of the book that were fully understandable. And the further he read, the more and more he came to the thought that something especially important was said in this book. Important, and simple, and touching, something he had never heard before, but which seemed to have long been familiar to him.

“And to all he said: if anyone wants to come after me, deny yourself and take your cross and follow me. For whoever wants to save his soul will lose it, and whoever loses his soul for my sake will save it. For what profit is it to a man to gain the whole world, and destroy or harm himself?”

— Yes, yes, this is it! — he suddenly cried out with tears in his eyes. — This is just what I wanted to do. Yes, this very thing is what I wanted: precisely, to give my soul; not to save it, but to give it. In this is joy, in this is life. “I did much for people, for human glory,” he thought, “not the glory of the crowd, but the glory of the good opinion of those whom I respected and loved: Natasha, Dmitry Shelomov, and then there were doubts, there was unease. It was good for me only when I did a thing only because my soul demanded it, when I wanted to give myself, to give all of myself”...

From that day Svetlogub began to spend the greater part of his time reading and thinking over what was said in this book. This reading called forth in him not only a state of tender emotion, which carried him out of the conditions in which he found himself, but also such a work of thought as he had never before been conscious of in himself. He thought about why people, all people, do not live as is said in this book. “Why, to live like that is good not for one alone, but for everyone. Only live like that—and there will be no grief, no want, there will be only blessedness. If only this would end, if only I could be at liberty again,” he thought sometimes, “they will let me out sometime, after all, or send me to hard labor. It's all the same: one can live like that anywhere. And I will live like that. It is possible and necessary to live like that; not to live like that is madness.”

## V

On one of those days when he was in such a joyful, excited state, the warden came into his cell at an unusual hour and asked whether he was all right and whether he wanted anything. Svetlogub was surprised, not understanding what this change meant, <!-- vol. 42, p. 204 --> and asked for cigarettes, expecting a refusal. But the warden said that he would send some at once, and indeed the guard brought him a pack of cigarettes and matches.

“Someone must have interceded for me,” thought Svetlogub, and, lighting a cigarette, he began to walk back and forth in the cell, thinking over the meaning of this change.

The next day he was taken to the court. In the court premises, where he had already been several times, they did not interrogate him. But one of the judges, not looking at him, rose from his armchair, the others rose too, and he, holding a paper in his hands, began to read in a loud, unnaturally expressionless voice.

Svetlogub listened and looked at the faces of the judges. None of them looked at him, and they listened with significant, dreary faces.

The paper said that Anatoly Svetlogub, for proven participation in revolutionary activity having as its aim the overthrow, in the nearer or more distant future, of the existing government, was sentenced to deprivation of all rights and to the death penalty by hanging.

Svetlogub heard and understood the meaning of the words pronounced by the officer. He noticed the absurdity of the words: in the nearer or more distant future, and of the deprivation of rights of a man sentenced to death, but he did not at all understand the significance that what had been read had for him.

Only long after he had been told that he could go, and he had come out into the street with a gendarme, did he begin to understand what had been announced to him.

“Something is not right here, not right... There's some sort of nonsense here. This cannot be,” he said to himself, sitting in the carriage that was taking him back to the prison.

He felt such a force of life in himself that he could not imagine death: he could not join the consciousness of his “I” with death, with the absence of “I.”

On returning to his prison, Svetlogub sat down on the cot and, closing his eyes, tried to imagine vividly what awaited him, and could not do it at all. He could not at all imagine that he would be no more, nor could he imagine that people could want to kill him.

“Me, young, kind, happy, loved by so many people,” he thought—he remembered the love for him of his mother, of Natasha, of his friends—“me they will kill, hang! Who will do <!-- vol. 42, p. 205 --> this, and why? And then, what will there be when I am no more? It cannot be,” he said to himself.

The warden came. Svetlogub had not heard him come in.

— Who is it? What is it? — Svetlogub said, not recognizing the warden. — Ah, yes, it's you! When will it be? — he asked.

— I cannot know, — said the warden and, having stood silent for a few seconds, suddenly said in an insinuating, tender voice: — Our priest here would like... to see you off on your w... would like to see you...

— I don't need it, I don't need it, I don't need anything! Go away! — cried Svetlogub.

— Don't you need to write to someone? That is allowed, — said the warden.

— Yes, yes, send it. I'll write.

The warden went out.

“So it's in the morning,” thought Svetlogub. “They always do it that way. Tomorrow morning I will be no more... No, this cannot be, this is a dream.”

But the guard came, the real, familiar guard, and brought two pens, an inkwell, a packet of letter paper and bluish envelopes, and set a stool at the table. All this was real and not a dream.

“I must not think, not think. Yes, yes, write. I'll write to Mama,” thought Svetlogub, sat down on the stool, and at once began to write.

“My dear, my own!” he wrote, and burst into tears. “Forgive me, forgive me for all the grief I have caused you. Whether I was mistaken or not, I could not do otherwise. One thing I ask: forgive me.” — “But I've already written that,” he thought. “Well, it doesn't matter, there's no time now to copy it over.” — “Do not grieve for me,” he wrote further. “A little sooner, a little later... isn't it all the same? I am not afraid, and I do not repent of what I did. I could not do otherwise. Only you forgive me. And do not be angry with them, neither with those with whom I worked nor with those who will execute me. Neither the ones nor the others could do otherwise: forgive them, they do not know what they do. I dare not repeat these words about myself, but they are in my soul, and they lift me up and calm me. Forgive me, I kiss your dear, wrinkled, old hands!” Two tears, one after the other, dropped onto the paper and spread out on it. “I am weeping, but not from grief or fear, but from tender emotion before the most solemn moment of my life, and because I love you. Do not reproach my friends, but love them. Especially Prokhorov, precisely because he <!-- vol. 42, p. 206 --> was the cause of my death. It is such a joy to love one who is not exactly to blame, but whom one could reproach, hate. To come to love such a person—an enemy—is such happiness. Tell Natasha that her love was my consolation and joy. I did not understand this clearly, but in the depths of my soul I was conscious of it. It was easier for me to live, knowing that she exists and loves me. Well, I have said everything. Farewell!”

He reread the letter and, reading Prokhorov's name at the end of it, suddenly remembered that the letter could be read, would surely be read, and this would ruin Prokhorov.

— My God, what have I done! — he suddenly cried out, and, tearing the letter into long strips, began carefully burning them over the lamp.

He had sat down to write in despair, but now he felt calm, almost joyful.

He took another sheet and at once began to write. Thoughts crowded in his head one after another.

“My dear, darling Mama!” he wrote, and again his eyes clouded over with tears, and he had to wipe them with the sleeve of his robe in order to see what he was writing. “How I did not know myself, did not know the full strength of that love for you and gratitude which always lived in my heart! Now I know and feel it, and when I remember our quarrels, the unkind words I said to you, I feel pain and shame, and it is almost incomprehensible to me. Forgive me, then, and remember only what was good, if there was anything of the kind in me.

“I am not afraid of death. To tell the truth, I do not understand it, I do not believe in it. After all, if there is death, annihilation, then is it not all the same whether one dies thirty years or minutes sooner or later? And if there is no death, then it is quite all the same, sooner or later.”

“But why am I philosophizing,” he thought, “I must say what was in that letter—something good at the end. Yes.” — “Do not reproach my friends, but love them, and especially the one who was the cause of my involuntary death. Kiss Natasha for me and tell her that I always loved her.”

He folded the letter, sealed it, and sat down on the bed, laying his hands on his knees and swallowing his tears.

He still did not believe that he must die. Several times, again asking himself whether he was not asleep, he tried in vain to wake up. And this thought led him to another: whether all of life in this world, too, is not a dream, the awakening from which will be death. And if this is so, then is not the consciousness of life in this world only an awakening from the dream of a preceding life, <!-- vol. 42, p. 207 --> the details of which I also do not remember? So that life here is not a beginning, but only a new form of life. I will die and pass into a new form. He liked this thought; but when he wanted to lean on it, he felt that this thought, and indeed any thought whatever, could not give fearlessness before death. At last he grew tired of thinking. His brain no longer worked. He closed his eyes and sat like that for a long time, not thinking.

“But how? What will there be?” he remembered again. “Nothing? No, not nothing. Then what?”

And it suddenly became perfectly clear to him that for a living man there is not and cannot be an answer to these questions.

“So why am I asking myself about this? Why? Yes, why? There is no need to ask; one must live the way I was living just now, when I was writing this letter. After all, we have all been sentenced long ago, always, and we live. We live well, joyfully, when... we love. Yes, when we love. Here I was writing a letter, I loved, and I felt good. That is how one must live. And one can live everywhere and always, at liberty and in prison, today and tomorrow, and to the very end.”

He wanted at once to talk with someone affectionately, lovingly. He knocked at the door, and when the sentry looked in at him, he asked him what time it was and whether he would be relieved soon, but the sentry made him no answer. Then he asked for the warden to be called. The warden came, asking what he needed.

— Here, I have written a letter to my mother; please send it, — he said, and tears came into his eyes at the memory of his mother.

The warden took the letter and, promising to pass it on, was about to go, but Svetlogub stopped him.

— Listen, you are kind. Why do you serve in this hard position? — he said, affectionately touching him on the sleeve.

The warden smiled an unnaturally plaintive smile and, lowering his eyes, said:

— One has to live, after all.

— But you should leave this position. One can always find a place, after all. You are so kind. Perhaps I could...

The warden suddenly gave a sob, quickly turned, and went out, slamming the door.

The warden's agitation moved Svetlogub to still greater tender emotion, and, holding back tears of joy, he began to walk from wall to wall, <!-- vol. 42, p. 208 --> feeling now no fear at all, but only a state of tender emotion that raised him above the world.

The very question of what would become of him after death, which he had tried so hard to answer and could not, seemed resolved for him, and not by some positive, rational answer, but by the consciousness of that true life which was in him.

And he remembered the words of the Gospel: “Truly, truly I say to you, if a grain of wheat, having fallen on the earth, does not die, it will remain alone; but if it dies, it will bear much fruit.” “So I too am falling into the earth. Yes, truly, truly,” he thought.

“I should sleep,” he suddenly thought, “so as not to grow weak afterward.” He lay down on the cot, closed his eyes, and at once fell asleep.

He woke at six o'clock in the morning, wholly under the impression of a bright, merry dream. He had dreamed that he, with some little fair-haired girl, was climbing about in spreading trees strewn with ripe black cherries and gathering them into a big copper basin. The cherries do not land in the basin and scatter onto the ground, and some strange animals, something like cats, catch the cherries and toss them up and catch them again. And, looking at this, the girl goes off into peals of laughter, laughs so infectiously that Svetlogub too laughs merrily in his dream, not knowing himself at what. Suddenly the copper basin slips out of the girl's hands; Svetlogub wants to catch it but is too late, and the basin, with a copper clatter, knocking against the boughs, falls to the ground. And he wakes up, smiling and listening to the continuing clatter of the basin. This clatter is the sound of iron bolts being drawn back in the corridor. Footsteps are heard along the corridor, and the clink of rifles. He suddenly remembers everything. “Ah, if only I could fall asleep again!” thinks Svetlogub, but it is no longer possible to fall asleep. The footsteps came up to his door. He hears the key searching for the lock, and the door creaking as it opens.

A gendarme officer, the warden, and the escort came in.

“Death? Well, what of it? I will go away. Yes, this is good. Everything is good,” thinks Svetlogub, feeling that solemn state of tender emotion in which he had been yesterday coming back to him.

## VI

In the same prison where Svetlogub was held, there was also held an old schismatic, a Bespopovets, who had come to doubt his guides and was seeking the true faith. He rejected not only the Nikonian church but also the government since the time of Peter, <!-- vol. 42, p. 209 --> whom he considered the antichrist—he called the tsar's power “the tobacco power” and boldly spoke out what he thought, denouncing priests and officials, for which he had been tried and held in jail and transferred from one prison to another. That he was not at liberty but in prison, that the wardens reviled him, that they put fetters on him, that his fellow prisoners jeered at him, that all of them, just like the authorities, had renounced God and reviled one another and in every way defiled the image of God in themselves—none of this occupied him; all this he had seen everywhere in the world when he was at liberty. All this, he knew, came about because people had lost the true faith and had all wandered off, like blind puppies from their mother. And yet he knew that the true faith exists. He knew this because he felt this faith in his heart. And he sought this faith everywhere. Most of all he hoped to find it in the Revelation of John.

“Let the unrighteous one still do wrong; let the unclean one still defile himself; let the righteous one still do right, and let the holy one still be made holy. Behold, I am coming soon, and my recompense is with me, to render to each according to his deeds.” And he constantly read this mysterious book and every minute awaited “the one who is coming,” who would not only render to each according to his deeds but would also reveal the whole divine truth to people.

On the morning of Svetlogub's execution he heard drums and, climbing up onto the window, saw through the grating how the cart was brought up and how a youth with bright eyes and curling locks came out of the prison and, smiling, mounted the cart. In the youth's small white hand was a book. The youth was pressing the book to his heart—the schismatic recognized that it was the Gospel—and, nodding to the prisoners at the windows, smiling, he exchanged a glance with him. The horses started, and the cart, with the youth, bright as an angel, sitting in it, surrounded by guards, rumbling over the stones, drove out through the gates.

The schismatic climbed down from the window, sat down on his cot, and fell to thinking. “This one has come to know the truth,” he thought. “That's why the servants of antichrist will strangle him with a rope: so that he won't reveal it to anyone.”

## VII

It was an overcast autumn morning. The sun could not be seen. A damp, warm wind was blowing from the sea.

The fresh air, the sight of the houses, of the city, of horses, of people looking at him—all this diverted Svetlogub. Sitting on <!-- vol. 42, p. 210 --> the bench of the cart, with his back to the driver, he involuntarily peered into the faces of the soldiers escorting him and of the townspeople he met.

It was an early hour of the morning; the streets along which he was being taken were almost empty, and only workers were to be met. Masons spattered with lime, in aprons, who were hurrying toward him, stopped and turned back, keeping level with the cart. One of them said something, waved his hand, and they all turned and went back to their work; carters—draymen hauling clanging strips of iron—having turned their big horses aside to make way for the cart, stopped and looked at him with bewildered curiosity. One of them took off his cap and crossed himself. A cook in a white apron and a little cap, with a basket in her hand, came out of a gateway, but, seeing the cart, quickly went back into the yard and ran out of it with another woman, and both, holding their breath, followed the cart with wide-open eyes for as long as they could see it. Some disheveled, unshaven, grayish man, with energetic gestures, was impressing something evidently disapproving on a yard porter, pointing at Svetlogub. Two boys caught up with the cart at a trot and, with their heads turned, not looking ahead of them, strode beside it along the sidewalk. One, the older, walked with quick steps; the other, a little one, without a cap, holding on to the older one and looking fearfully at the cart, kept up with the older one with difficulty on his short little legs, stumbling. Meeting his eyes, Svetlogub nodded to him. This gesture of the terrible man being driven in the cart so confused the boy that, goggling his eyes and opening his mouth, he was about to cry. Then Svetlogub, kissing his own hand, smiled at him affectionately. And the boy suddenly, unexpectedly, answered with a sweet, kind smile.

During the whole drive the consciousness of what awaited him did not disturb Svetlogub's calmly solemn mood.

Only when the cart drove up to the gallows and he was led down from it, and he saw the posts with the crossbeam and the rope swaying slightly on it in the wind, did he feel something like a physical blow to the heart. He suddenly felt sick. But this did not last long. Around the platform he saw black rows of soldiers with rifles. In front of the soldiers officers were walking. And as soon as they began to lead him down from the cart, there rang out an unexpected rattle of a drum roll that made him start. Behind the rows of soldiers Svetlogub saw the carriages of gentlemen and ladies who had evidently come to look <!-- vol. 42, p. 211 --> at the spectacle. The sight of all this surprised Svetlogub in the first moment, but at once he remembered himself as he had been before prison, and he felt sorry that these people did not know what he knew now. “But they will find out. I will die, but the truth will not die. They will know. And how everyone—no longer I, but all of them—could be and will be happy.”

He was led up onto the platform, and after him an officer came up. The drums fell silent, and the officer read out, in an unnatural voice that sounded especially weak amid the wide field and after the rattle of the drums, that stupid death sentence which had been read to him at the court, about the deprivation of rights of the one whom they are killing, and about the near and distant future. “Why, why do they do all this?” thought Svetlogub. “What a pity that they do not know yet, and that I can no longer pass everything on to them; but they will find out. Everyone will find out.”

There came up to Svetlogub a lean priest with long, sparse hair, in a lilac cassock, with one small gilt cross on his chest and another, large silver cross, which he held in a weak, white, sinewy, thin hand that stuck out from a black velvet cuff.

— Merciful Lord, — he began, shifting the cross from his left hand to his right and holding it up to Svetlogub.

Svetlogub started and drew back. He very nearly said an unkind word to the priest, who was taking part in the deed being done to him and speaking of mercy, but, remembering the words of the Gospel, “they do not know what they do,” he made an effort and timidly said:

— Excuse me, I don't need this. Please, forgive me, but really, I don't need it! Thank you.

He held out his hand to the priest. The priest again shifted the cross to his left hand and, shaking Svetlogub's hand and trying not to look him in the face, went down from the platform. The drums began to rattle again, drowning out all other sounds. After the priest, making the boards of the platform sway, a middle-aged man with sloping shoulders and muscular arms, in a jacket over a Russian shirt, came up to Svetlogub with quick steps. This man, glancing quickly over Svetlogub, came up very close to him and, enveloping him in an unpleasant smell of wine and sweat, seized him by the arms above the wrists with clutching fingers and, squeezing them so that it hurt, bent them behind his back and tied them tightly. Having tied his hands, the executioner stopped for a minute, as if considering, and glancing now <!-- vol. 42, p. 212 --> at Svetlogub, now at some things which he had brought with him and laid on the platform, now at the rope hanging from the crossbeam. Having figured out what he needed, he went up to the rope, did something with it, and moved Svetlogub forward, closer to the rope and to the edge of the platform.

Just as at the announcement of the death sentence Svetlogub had been unable to understand the full significance of what was being announced to him, so now he could not take in the full significance of the coming moment, and he looked with surprise at the executioner, who was carrying out his terrible work hastily, deftly, and with a preoccupied air. The executioner's face was the most ordinary face of a Russian working man, not angry, but concentrated, such as people have who are trying to carry out a necessary and complicated piece of work as exactly as possible.

— Move over here some more... or, if you please, move over... — the executioner said in a hoarse voice, pushing him toward the gallows. Svetlogub moved over.

“Lord, help me, have mercy on me!” he said.

Svetlogub did not believe in God and had even often laughed at people who believe in God. Now, too, he did not believe in God, did not believe because he could not express him in words, nor even embrace him in thought. But what he now meant by the one he was addressing—he knew this—was the most real thing of all that he knew. He knew too that this appeal was necessary and important. He knew this because this appeal at once calmed and strengthened him.

He moved up to the gallows and, involuntarily casting a glance over the rows of soldiers and the motley spectators, thought once more: “Why, why do they do this?” And he felt sorry both for them and for himself, and tears came into his eyes.

— And aren't you sorry for me? — he said, catching the glance of the executioner's lively gray eyes.

The executioner stopped for a minute. His face suddenly turned angry.

— Get away with you! Talking, — he muttered, and quickly bent down to the floor, where his coat and some linen lay, and, with a deft movement of both hands, embracing Svetlogub from behind, threw a canvas sack over his head and hastily pulled it down to the middle of his back and chest.

“Into your hands I commit my spirit”—Svetlogub remembered the words of the Gospel.

His spirit did not resist death, but his strong, young body did not accept it, did not submit, and wanted to struggle.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 213 --> He wanted to cry out, to tear himself free, but at that same instant he felt a jolt, the loss of a point of support, the animal horror of suffocation, a roaring in his head, and the vanishing of everything.

Svetlogub's body, swaying, hung on the rope. Twice the shoulders rose and fell.

After waiting about two minutes, the executioner, frowning darkly, put his hands on the corpse's shoulders and with a strong movement pulled on it. All movements of the corpse ceased, except for the slow swaying of the doll hanging in the sack, with its head thrust unnaturally forward and its legs stretched out in prisoner's stockings.

Coming down from the platform, the executioner announced to the man in charge that the corpse could be taken out of the noose and buried.

An hour later the corpse was taken down from the gallows and carried off to an unconsecrated cemetery.

The executioner had carried out what he wanted and what he had undertaken to carry out. But the carrying out had not been easy. Svetlogub's words, “and aren't you sorry for me,” would not leave his head. He was a murderer, a convict, and the position of executioner gave him relative freedom and luxury of living, but from that day he refused to carry out any longer the duty he had taken upon himself, and in that same week he drank away not only all the money he had received for the execution but also all his relatively rich clothing, and came to such a pass that he was put in the punishment cell, and from the punishment cell transferred to the hospital.

## VIII

One of the chiefs of the revolutionaries of the terrorist party, Ignaty Mezhenetsky, the very one who had drawn Svetlogub into terrorist activity, was being transferred from the province where he had been seized to Petersburg. In the same prison sat the old schismatic too, who had seen Svetlogub's execution. He was being transferred to Siberia. He still kept thinking about how and where he might learn what the true faith is, and sometimes he remembered that bright youth who, going to his death, had smiled joyfully.

Learning that in the same prison with him there was a comrade of that youth, a man of the same faith as he, the schismatic was glad and prevailed on the warder to take him to Svetlogub's friend.

Mezhenetsky, in spite of all the strictness of prison discipline, had not stopped communicating with people of his party, and every day he waited for news of the tunnel which he himself had <!-- vol. 42, p. 214 --> thought up and devised for blowing up the tsar's train. Now, remembering certain details he had overlooked, he was thinking up means of passing them on to his like-minded associates. When the warder came into his cell and cautiously, quietly told him that a prisoner wanted to see him, he was glad, hoping that this meeting would give him a possibility of communicating with his party.

— Who is he? — he asked.

— Of peasant stock.

— What does he want, then?

— He wants to talk about faith.

Mezhenetsky smiled.

— Well, why not, send him in, — he said. — “They, the schismatics, hate the government too. He may come in handy,” he thought.

The warder went out and a few minutes later, opening the door, let into the cell a dry, short old man with thick hair and a sparse, graying goatee, with kind, tired blue eyes.

— What do you want? — asked Mezhenetsky.

The old man glanced up at him and, hastily lowering his eyes, held out a small, energetic, dry hand.

— What do you want? — Mezhenetsky repeated.

— I've a word for you.

— What word?

— About faith.

— About what faith?

— They say you're of the same faith as that lad the servants of antichrist strangled with a rope in Odesta.

— What youth?

— Why, in Odesta, in the autumn, they strangled him.

— Svetlogub, I suppose?

— That's the one. Is he a friend to you? — at each question the old man glanced searchingly with his kind eyes into Mezhenetsky's face and at once lowered them again.

— Yes, he was a man close to me.

— And of the same faith?

— Must be the same, — Mezhenetsky said, smiling.

— That very thing is what my word to you is about.

— What is it, exactly, that you want?

— To come to know your faith.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 215 --> — Our faith... Well, sit down, — said Mezhenetsky, shrugging his shoulders. — Our faith consists in this. We believe that there are people who have seized power and torment and deceive the people, and that we must not spare ourselves but must fight these people in order to deliver from them the people whom they exploit, — Mezhenetsky said from habit, — torment, — he corrected himself. — And it is they who must be destroyed. They kill, and they must be killed until they come to their senses.

The old schismatic kept sighing, not raising his eyes.

— Our faith consists in not sparing ourselves, in overthrowing the despotic government and establishing a free, elected, people's government.

The old man sighed heavily, stood up, straightened the skirts of his robe, went down on his knees, and lay down at Mezhenetsky's feet, knocking his forehead against the dirty boards of the floor.

— Why are you bowing down?

— Don't you deceive me, reveal what your faith consists in, — said the old man, not getting up and not raising his head.

— I have told you what our faith consists in. Now do get up, or else I won't talk at all.

The old man rose.

— And was that what that youth's faith consisted in too? — he said, standing before Mezhenetsky and now and then glancing into his face with his kind eyes and at once lowering them again.

— That very thing, and that's what they hanged him for. And now they're taking me to the Petropavlovka for that same faith.

The old man bowed from the waist and silently went out of the cell.

“No, that's not what that youth's faith consists in,” he thought. “That lad knew the true faith, but this one either was boasting that he is of the same faith, or does not want to reveal it... Well, I'll keep striving. Both here and in Siberia. God is everywhere, people are everywhere. Once on the road, ask about the road,” thought the old man, and again took up the New Testament, which opened of itself at Revelation, and, putting on his glasses, sat down by the window and began to read it.

## IX

Seven more years passed. Mezhenetsky had served out his solitary confinement in the Peter and Paul Fortress and was being transferred to hard labor.

He had endured much in these seven years, but the direction of his thoughts had not changed, and his energy had not weakened. At the interrogations, before <!-- vol. 42, p. 216 --> his confinement in the fortress, he had surprised the investigators and judges by his firmness and his contemptuous attitude toward the people in whose power he was. In the depths of his soul he suffered from having been caught and being unable to finish the work he had begun, but he did not show it: as soon as he came into contact with people, the energy of malice rose up in him. To the questions put to him he kept silent, and he spoke only when there was a chance to sting his interrogators—the gendarme officer or the prosecutor.

When they said to him the usual phrase, “You can ease your situation by a sincere confession,” he smiled contemptuously and, after a silence, said:

— If you think to make me betray my comrades by gain or by fear, then you are judging by yourselves. Surely you don't think that, doing the deed you are judging me for, I did not prepare for the very worst. So there is nothing you can surprise or frighten me with. You can do with me what you like, but I will not talk.

And it was pleasant for him to see how they glanced at each other in embarrassment.

When he was put in the Peter and Paul Fortress into a small, damp cell with dark glass in a high window, he understood that this was not for months but for years—and horror came over him. Terrible was this well-ordered, dead silence, and the consciousness that he was not alone, but that here, behind these impenetrable walls, sat prisoners just like him, sentenced to ten, twenty years, killing themselves and being hanged and going mad and slowly dying of consumption. Here there were women, and men, and friends, perhaps... “Years will pass, and you too will go mad, hang yourself, or die, and no one will know about you,” he thought.

And malice rose up in his soul against all people, and especially against those who were the cause of his confinement. This malice demanded the presence of objects of malice, demanded movement, noise. But here there was dead silence, the soft steps of silent people who did not answer questions, the sounds of doors being unlocked and locked, food at the usual hours, visits from silent people, and through the dim panes the light of the rising sun, darkness and the same silence, the same soft steps and the very same sounds. So today, tomorrow... And the malice, finding no outlet, ate away at his heart.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 217 --> He tried knocking, but no one answered him, and his knocking only called forth again the same soft steps and the even voice of a man threatening him with the punishment cell.

The only time of rest and relief was the time of sleep. But then the awakening was terrible. In his sleep he always saw himself free, and for the most part absorbed in such pursuits as he considered incompatible with revolutionary activity. Now he was playing some strange violin, now courting young ladies, now boating, now going hunting, now for some strange scientific discovery he was proclaimed a doctor of a foreign university and was making a speech of thanks at a dinner. These dreams were so vivid, and reality so dull and monotonous, that memories differed little from reality.

The only thing that was hard in his dreams was that for the most part he woke up at the moment when what he was striving for, what he desired, was just about to come to pass. Suddenly a jolt of the heart—and the whole joyful setting vanished; there remained a tormenting, unsatisfied desire, again this gray wall with streaks of damp, lit by a little lamp, and under his body the hard cot with its straw mattress crushed down on one side.

Sleep was the best time. But the longer the confinement went on, the less he slept. He waited for sleep as for the greatest happiness, desired it, and the more he desired it, the more wide awake he grew. And he had only to ask himself the question: “Am I falling asleep?” and all his drowsiness would pass.

Running, jumping about his cage did not help. From the strenuous movement there came only weakness and a still greater excitation of the nerves, there came a headache at the crown of his head, and he had only to close his eyes for mugs to begin to stand out against a dark background with sparkles: shaggy, bald, big-mouthed, wry-mouthed, each more terrible than the last. The mugs grimaced with the most terrible grimaces. Then the mugs began to appear even when his eyes were open, and not only mugs but whole figures; they began to talk and to dance. It became frightening; he would jump up, beat his head against the wall, and shout. The little window in the door would open.

— Shouting is not permitted, — a calm, even voice would say.

— Call the warden! — Mezhenetsky would shout.

No answer was given him, and the little window would close.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 218 --> And such despair would seize Mezhenetsky that he desired one thing only—death.

Once, in such a state, he decided to take his own life. In the cell there was an air vent on which one could fasten a rope with a noose and, standing on the cot, hang oneself. But there was no rope. He began tearing the sheet into narrow strips, but these strips turned out to be too few. Then he decided to starve himself to death and did not eat for two days, but on the third day he grew weak, and the fit of hallucinations came back on him with particular force. When they brought him his food, he was lying on the floor unconscious, with his eyes open.

The doctor came, laid him on the cot, gave him bromide and morphine, and he fell asleep.

When he woke the next day, the doctor was standing over him and shaking his head. And suddenly Mezhenetsky was seized by the invigorating feeling of malice, familiar to him from before, which he had long not experienced.

— Aren't you ashamed, — he said to the doctor while the latter, his head bent, was counting his pulse, — to serve here! Why are you treating me—so as to torment me again! Why, it's just the same as being present at a flogging and permitting the operation to be repeated.

— Kindly lie on your back, — said the doctor imperturbably, not looking at him and taking the instrument for auscultation out of his side pocket.

— Those men healed the wounds so as to get through the remaining five thousand sticks. To hell, to the devil! — he suddenly shouted, throwing his legs off the cot. — Get out, I'll croak without you!

— That's not nice, young man; for rudeness we have our own answers.

— To hell, to hell!

And Mezhenetsky was so frightening that the doctor hastened to leave.

## X

Whether this came from taking the medicines, or he had passed through a crisis, or the malice that had risen up against the doctor cured him, from that time on he took himself in hand and began an entirely different life.

“They cannot and will not keep me here forever,” he thought. “They'll set me free sometime, after all. Perhaps—what is most likely of all—the regime will change (our people are still working), <!-- vol. 42, p. 219 --> and so I must take care of my life, so as to come out strong, healthy, and able to continue the work.”

He thought out for a long time the best way of life for this purpose and devised this: he went to bed at nine o'clock and made himself lie—asleep or not asleep, it made no difference—until five o'clock in the morning. At five o'clock he got up, tidied up, washed, did gymnastics, and then, as he said to himself, went about his business. And in imagination he walked through Petersburg, from the Nevsky to Nadezhdinskaya Street, trying to picture to himself everything that he might meet on this walk: signboards, houses, policemen, carriages and pedestrians coming toward him. On Nadezhdinskaya he went into the house of an acquaintance and collaborator of his, and there they, together with the comrades who had come, discussed the forthcoming undertaking. There were debates, arguments. Mezhenetsky spoke both for himself and for the others. Sometimes he spoke aloud, so that the sentry admonished him through the little window, but Mezhenetsky paid no attention whatever to him and went on with his imaginary Petersburg day. Having spent about two hours at his friend's, he returned home and had dinner, first in imagination and then in reality, with the dinner that was brought to him, and he always ate moderately. Then, in imagination, he sat at home and occupied himself now with history, mathematics, and sometimes, on Sundays, literature. His study of history consisted in choosing some epoch and people and recalling the facts and chronology. His study of mathematics consisted in working out calculations and geometric problems by heart. (He particularly loved this occupation.) On Sundays he recalled Pushkin, Gogol, Shakespeare, and composed things himself.

Before sleep he made one more little excursion, in imagination carrying on with comrades, men and women, jesting, merry, sometimes serious conversations, sometimes ones that had taken place before, sometimes newly invented. And so it went until night. Before sleep, for exercise, he took 2000 steps in reality in his cage, lay down on his cot, and for the most part fell asleep.

The next day was the same. Sometimes he went to the south and stirred up the people, started a rebellion, and together with the people drove out the landowners, distributed the land to the peasants. All this, however, he imagined not all at once but gradually, with all the details. In his imagination the revolutionary party triumphed everywhere, the government's power weakened and was forced to convene an assembly. The tsar's family and all the oppressors of the people vanished, and <!-- vol. 42, p. 220 --> a republic was established, and he, Mezhenetsky, was elected president. Sometimes he arrived at this too quickly, and then he would begin again from the beginning and reach the goal by another way.

So he lived a year, two, three, sometimes departing from this strict order of life, but for the most part returning to it. By governing his imagination he freed himself from involuntary hallucinations. Only rarely did fits of sleeplessness and visions, mugs, come over him, and then he would look at the air vent and consider how he would fasten the rope, how he would make a noose and hang himself. But these fits did not last long. He overcame them.

So he lived almost seven years. When the term of his confinement ended and he was taken to hard labor, he was perfectly fresh, healthy, and in full possession of his mental powers.

## XI

He was taken, as an especially important criminal, alone, without being allowed to communicate with others. And only in the Krasnoyarsk prison did he manage for the first time to come into contact with other political criminals who were also being exiled to hard labor; there were six of them—two women and four men. These were all young people of a new cast, unfamiliar to Mezhenetsky. These were revolutionaries of the generation following his, his heirs, and so they particularly interested him. Mezhenetsky expected to meet in them people following in his footsteps and therefore bound to value highly everything that had been done by their predecessors, especially by him, Mezhenetsky. He was preparing to treat them affectionately and condescendingly. But, to his unpleasant surprise, these young people not only did not consider him their predecessor and teacher, but treated him, as it were, condescendingly, passing over and excusing his outdated views. In the opinion of these new revolutionaries, everything that Mezhenetsky and his friends had done, all the attempts at stirring the peasants to revolt and, above all, the terror and all the murders—of Governor Kropotkin, of Mezentsov, and of Alexander II himself—all this had been a series of mistakes. All this had led only to the reaction which had triumphed under Alexander III and had turned society back, almost to serfdom. The path to the liberation of the people, in the opinion of the new ones, was quite different.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 221 --> For two days and almost two nights the arguments between Mezhenetsky and his new acquaintances did not cease. One in particular, the leader of them all, Roman, as everyone called him, by his first name only, grieved Mezhenetsky tormentingly with his unshakable confidence that he was right and his condescending, even mocking denial of all the past activity of Mezhenetsky and his comrades.

The people, in Roman's conception, are a coarse crowd, “cattle,” and with the people at the stage of development at which they stand now, nothing can be done. All attempts to rouse the Russian rural population are the same as trying to set fire to stone or ice. The people must be educated, must be accustomed to solidarity, and this can be done only by large-scale industry and the socialist organization of the people that grows up on it. Not only do the people not need the land, but it is precisely the land that makes them conservatives and slaves. Not only here, but in Europe too. And he cited from memory the opinions of authorities and statistical figures. The people must be freed from the land. And the sooner this happens, the better. The more of them go to the factories, and the more the capitalists take the land into their hands, and the more they oppress them, the better. Despotism, and above all capitalism, can be destroyed only by the solidarity of the common people, and this solidarity can be achieved only by unions, workers' corporations, that is, only when the masses of the people cease to be owners of land and become proletarians.

Mezhenetsky argued and grew heated. He was particularly irritated by one of the women, a not bad-looking, thick-haired brunette with very shining eyes, who, sitting on the windowsill and as if not taking a direct part in the conversation, now and then put in little remarks confirming Roman's arguments, or merely laughed contemptuously at Mezhenetsky's words.

— Can one really remake the whole farming people into factory workers? — Mezhenetsky said.

— And why can't one? — Roman objected. — It is a general economic law.

— How do we know that this law is universal? — Mezhenetsky said.

— Read Kautsky, — the brunette put in, smiling contemptuously.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 222 --> — Even if one grants, — Mezhenetsky said (I do not grant it), — that the people will be remade into proletarians, why do you think that they will settle into that form you have assigned them in advance?

— Because it has been scientifically grounded, — the brunette put in, turning from the window.

When the talk turned to the form of activity needed to achieve the goal, the disagreement grew still greater. Roman and his friends held that it was necessary to prepare an army of workers, to assist the passing of peasants into factory workers, and to propagandize socialism among the workers. And not only not to fight the government openly, but to make use of it to achieve their aims. Mezhenetsky, on the other hand, said that one must fight the government directly, terrorize it, that the government is both stronger and craftier than you. — “It's not you who will deceive the government, but it that will deceive you. We both propagandized the people and fought the government.”

— And how much you accomplished! — the brunette said ironically.

— Well, I think that direct struggle with the government is an improper expenditure of forces, — said Roman.

— The first of March an expenditure of forces! — Mezhenetsky shouted. — We sacrificed ourselves, our lives, and you sit quietly in your homes, enjoying life, and only preach.

— We're not enjoying life all that much, — Roman said calmly, looking around at his comrades, and burst out laughing triumphantly with his uninfectious but loud, distinct, self-assured laugh.

The brunette, shaking her head, smiled contemptuously.

— We're not enjoying life all that much, — said Roman. — And if we are sitting here, we owe it to the reaction, and the reaction is the product precisely of the first of March.

Mezhenetsky fell silent. He felt that he was choking with malice, and went out into the corridor.

## XII

Trying to calm himself, Mezhenetsky began to walk back and forth along the corridor. The doors of the cells were open until the evening roll call. A tall, fair-haired prisoner, with a face whose good nature <!-- vol. 42, p. 223 --> was not spoiled by his half-shaven head, came up to Mezhenetsky.

— A little prisoner here, in our cell, saw Your Worship—call him to me, he says.

— What prisoner?

— “The tobacco power,” that's his nickname. A little old man he is, one of the schismatics. Call that man to me, he says. Meaning Your Worship, that is.

— Where is he, then?

— Right here, in our cell. Give that gentleman a call, he says.

Mezhenetsky went with the prisoner into a small cell with plank beds, on which prisoners were sitting and lying.

On the bare boards, under a gray robe, at the edge of the plank bed, lay that same old schismatic who seven years before had come to Mezhenetsky to ask about Svetlogub. The old man's face, pale, had all dried up and wrinkled; his hair was all just as thick as before; his sparse little beard was completely gray and stuck upward. His eyes were blue, kind, and attentive. He was lying on his back and was evidently in a fever: on his jutting cheekbones there was a sickly flush.

Mezhenetsky went up to him.

— What do you want? — he asked.

The old man raised himself with difficulty on his elbow and held out a trembling, dry, small hand. Getting ready to speak, as if rocking himself, he began to breathe heavily and, catching his breath with difficulty, began to speak quietly:

— You didn't reveal it to me then—God be with you, but I reveal it to everyone.

— And what is it you reveal?

— About the Lamb... about the Lamb I reveal... that lad was with the Lamb. And it is said: the Lamb shall conquer them, shall conquer all... And whoever is with him, those are chosen and faithful.

— I don't understand, — said Mezhenetsky.

— And you understand it in the spirit. Kings shall receive dominion with the beast. But the Lamb shall conquer them.

— What kings? — said Mezhenetsky.

— And the kings are seven: five of them have fallen, and one is left, another is not yet come—hasn't come, that means. And when he cometh, it is but little for him... meaning, his end will come... understand?

Mezhenetsky kept shaking his head, thinking that the old man was raving and that his words were meaningless. The prisoners, his cellmates, <!-- vol. 42, p. 224 --> thought so too. That shaven prisoner who had called Mezhenetsky came up to him and, nudging him slightly with his elbow and drawing his attention, winked toward the old man.

— Always babbling, always babbling, our tobacco power, — he said. — And what about, he doesn't know himself.

So, looking at the old man, thought both Mezhenetsky and his cellmates. But the old man knew well what he was saying, and what he was saying had for him a clear and deep meaning. The meaning was that evil has not long left to reign, that the Lamb conquers all by good and humility, that the Lamb will wipe away every tear, and there will be neither weeping, nor sickness, nor death. And he felt that this was already being accomplished, being accomplished in the whole world, because it was being accomplished in his soul, illumined by the nearness of death.

— Yea, come soon! Amen. Yea, come, Lord Jesus! — he said, and smiled slightly, significantly, and, as it seemed to Mezhenetsky, madly.

## XIII

“There he is, a representative of the people,” thought Mezhenetsky, leaving the old man. “This is the best of them. And what darkness! They (he meant Roman and his friends) say: with the people such as they are now, nothing can be done.”

Mezhenetsky had at one time done his revolutionary work among the people and knew the whole “inertness,” as he put it, of the Russian peasant; he had also come together with soldiers, both in service and retired, and knew their dull faith in the oath, in the necessity of obedience, and the impossibility of acting on them by reasoning. He knew all this, but he had never drawn from this knowledge the conclusion that inevitably followed from it. The conversation with the new revolutionaries had upset him, irritated him.

“They say that everything that we did, that Khalturin, Kibalchich, Perovskaya did, that all this was unnecessary, even harmful, that it was precisely this that called forth the reaction of Alexander III, that thanks to them the people are convinced that all revolutionary activity comes from the landowners, who killed the tsar because he took their serfs away from them. What nonsense! What incomprehension, and what impudence, to think so!” he thought, continuing to walk along the corridor.

All the cells were closed except one, the one in which the new revolutionaries were. Approaching it, Mezhenetsky <!-- vol. 42, p. 225 --> heard the laughter of the brunette he hated and Roman's crackling, decisive voice. They were evidently talking about him. Mezhenetsky stopped to listen. Roman was saying:

— Not understanding economic laws, they did not realize what they were doing. And there was a large share here of...

Mezhenetsky could not and did not want to hear out what there was a large share of here, but he had no need to know it either. The tone of this man's voice alone showed the complete contempt that these people felt for him, for Mezhenetsky, the hero of the revolution, who had ruined twelve years of his life for this aim.

And in Mezhenetsky's soul there rose such terrible malice as he had never yet experienced. Anger at everyone, at everything, at this whole senseless world, in which only people like animals could live, like this old man with his Lamb, and such half-animal executioners and jailers as well, these insolent, self-assured, stillborn doctrinaires.

The warder on duty came in and took the political women away to the women's side. Mezhenetsky went off to the far end of the corridor so as not to meet him. When he came back, the warder locked the door of the new politicals and told Mezhenetsky to go into his own cell. Mezhenetsky obeyed mechanically, but asked that his door not be locked.

Having returned to his cell, Mezhenetsky lay down on the cot with his face to the wall.

“Can it really be that all those powers have been ruined so in vain: energy, strength of will, genius (he had never considered anyone above himself in qualities of soul), ruined for nothing!” He remembered the letter he had received recently, already on the road to Siberia, from Svetlogub's mother, who reproached him in a womanish way, stupidly, as he thought, for having ruined her son by drawing him into the terrorist party. On receiving the letter he had only smiled contemptuously: what could this stupid woman understand of the aims that stood before him and Svetlogub. But now, remembering the letter and Svetlogub's dear, trusting, ardent personality, he fell to thinking, first about him, and then about himself too. Could his whole life have been a mistake? He closed his eyes and wanted to fall asleep, but suddenly felt with horror that the state he had experienced in the first month in the Peter and Paul Fortress had returned. Again the pain at the crown of his head, again the mugs, big-mouthed, <!-- vol. 42, p. 226 --> hairy, terrible, against a dark background with little stars, and again figures presenting themselves to his open eyes. What was new was that some common criminal in gray trousers, with a shaven head, was swaying over him. And again, by the association of ideas, he began to look for an air vent on which a rope could be fastened.

Unbearable malice, demanding expression, burned Mezhenetsky's heart. He could not sit still, could not calm down, could not drive away his thoughts.

“How?” he now began to ask himself. “Cut an artery? I won't manage it. Hang myself? Of course, that's the simplest.”

He remembered the rope with which a bundle of firewood lying in the corridor was tied. “Stand on the firewood or on the stool. The warder is walking in the corridor. But he'll fall asleep or go out. I must wait it out, and then carry the rope off to my cell and fasten it on the air vent.”

Standing by his door, Mezhenetsky listened to the warder's steps in the corridor and now and then, when the warder went off to the far end, looked out through the opening in the door. Still the warder did not go away, and still he did not fall asleep. Mezhenetsky listened greedily to the sounds of his steps and waited.

At this time, in the cell where the sick old man was, amid the darkness, faintly lit by a smoking lamp, amid the sleepy night sounds of breathing, muttering, groaning, snoring, coughing, the greatest event in the world was taking place. The old schismatic was dying, and to his spiritual gaze was revealed all that he had so passionately sought and desired throughout his whole life. Amid a dazzling light he saw the Lamb in the form of a bright youth, and a great multitude of people of all nations stood before him in white garments, and all were rejoicing, and there was no longer any evil on the earth. All this had been accomplished, the old man knew it, both in his soul and in the whole world, and he felt great joy and peace.

For the people who were in the cell, however, what happened was that the old man was rattling loudly with the death rattle, and his neighbor woke up and woke the others; and when the rattling ended and the old man fell quiet and grew cold, his cellmates began to knock at the door.

The warder unlocked the door and went in to the prisoners. About ten minutes later two prisoners carried out the dead body and took it downstairs to the mortuary. The warder went out after them and locked the door behind him. The corridor was left empty.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 227 --> “Lock it, lock it,” thought Mezhenetsky, who had been following from his door everything that was going on, “you won't stop me from getting away from all this absurd horror.”

Mezhenetsky no longer felt now the inner horror that had oppressed him before this. He was wholly absorbed in one thought: lest something should prevent him from carrying out his intention.

With a trembling heart he went up to the bundle of firewood, untied the rope, pulled it out from under the wood, and, looking back at the door, carried it to his cell. In the cell he climbed onto the stool and threw the rope over the air vent. Having tied both ends of the rope together, he pulled the knot tight and made a noose out of the doubled rope. The noose was too low. He tied the rope again, again made a noose, tried it against his neck, and, listening uneasily and looking back at the door, climbed onto the stool, put his head into the noose, adjusted it, and, pushing away the stool, hung...

Only at the morning round did the warder see Mezhenetsky, standing on legs bent at the knees beside the stool, which lay on its side. They took him out of the noose. The warden came running and, learning that Roman was a doctor, called him to give help to the strangled man.

All the usual methods of reviving him were used, but Mezhenetsky did not revive.

Mezhenetsky's body was carried down to the mortuary and laid on the plank bed beside the body of the old schismatic.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
