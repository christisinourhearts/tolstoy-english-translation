## PREFACE

The thoughts collected here were taken by me from a very large number of works and [^1] collections of thoughts (a list of all these books and collections is appended at the end), mostly English ones. In printing these thoughts in the form in which they appear here, I must make a reservation about how I translated and compiled them. Often I translated the thoughts of German, French, and Italian thinkers from English, and therefore my translations may prove not to be entirely faithful to the originals. Another and the main reason why these thoughts may not correspond literally to the originals is that, in translating certain passages, I did not always keep strictly to the original, but sometimes shortened it, omitting certain words and [^2] sentences which, in my opinion, weakened the force of the impression, and even replaced, though very rarely, certain words with others when I considered this replacement necessary for clarity of understanding. [^3] I know that such an attitude toward originals, especially of classical works, is not accepted and is considered [^4] criminal, but I believe that such an opinion is a very important and harmful prejudice, which has produced and continues to produce a great deal of evil, and I take this opportunity to express my opinion on this subject.

I remember how [^5], reading some years ago a translation of one [^6] ancient Greek work, [^7] namely “The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles,” I was struck by a strange note of the translator's to one of the passages of this work. The note said: since this passage is [^8] obscure in the original, we shall try to render <!-- vol. 42, p. 471 --> it just as obscure in the translation as well. [^9] It may very well be that by such a translation the [^10] translator earned the approval of his learned brethren, [^11] who always demand a mechanically exact rendering of texts. But, after all, besides the learned gentlemen, who wish to see a translated work in all its inviolability, with all the bad, superstitious, often stupid, crude passages to be found in it, there exists also a class of people—I think the most important for every writer—namely the class of readers, to whom it is desirable to communicate [^12] in the very best, attractive, and, above all, comprehensible form, without the obscure, crude, stupid, repellent passages which, as a tribute to their time, are always met with in every old book. And so [^13] it is for these very people, the most important in relation to every book, that the strange prejudice of learned people is especially harmful, according to which old works must be rendered in such a way, [^14] that all the passages incomprehensible to the modern reader and repellent to him remain whole in them [^15]. If, as a result of a bad rendering or translation, or [^16] an unfortunate expression by the author himself, or as a result of a superstition proper to the author's time, a lofty thought, needed by people, is expressed so that it is not entirely comprehensible, or is bound up with [^17] another thought, one that repels the modern reader, then how can one not wish to render this thought in the most comprehensible and attractive form, without those distortions which deprive it of significance.

It is still possible to understand that the church, having recognized the canonical books as works of the holy spirit, cannot [^18] allow any changes in the texts it has recognized. But why people who are not of the church hold to <!-- vol. 42, p. 472 --> such a strange [^19] superstition is completely incomprehensible. This is explained only by the fact that these people, the learned, are occupied only [^20] with the opinion of their brethren, and not with those people for whom alone their writings are needed. [^21] And yet the works of great writers are, after all, great [^22] only because they are needed by everyone and because it is desirable that as many people as possible should make use of the spiritual good which they give people. And in order that people, as many people as possible, may make use of this good, it is necessary to make these writers as accessible as possible, and for this it is necessary to try to make fully comprehensible what in them is not fully clear; it is necessary to cast off what may confuse and repel the modern reader; it is necessary in general to try to offer them to a larger circle of readers in the most comprehensible and attractive form. But [^23] what can be bad in the fact that people who love authors will try to present them so that they are as comprehensible and attractive to readers as possible? People say: “Everyone will throw out whatever he does not like.” [^24] But there is no harm in this either. What is great in an author will remain with everyone, while what is weak in him will fall away of itself. If people had long ago treated the Bible so, and especially the Gospel, then there would long ago have fallen away from the Gospel all the unclear, muddled words and the offensive parables about the unjust steward, and the miracles of the devils driven into the swine, and of Cana of Galilee, and many others.

“But then it will not be the Gospel, not Epictetus, not Pascal, not Rousseau, but your own work,” people will say to this. [^25] And in the world of the learned this argument is considered—irrefutable, just as if the whole matter lay in *to whom* a work is ascribed. I, however, believe that, rendered though with changes and abridgments and even additions, the thoughts of Epictetus, Rousseau, and others [^26] will still be the thoughts of Epictetus, Rousseau, and that from a person who loves these thoughts trying to render them as he understands them, altering them so that they may be received more easily and more strongly by people of the same understanding as his, not only will there be <!-- vol. 42, p. 473 --> nothing bad, but it will be very useful. And if the one who has rendered a classic in this way has rendered him incorrectly, badly, let others render him better. [^27] And if anyone needs exact translations, there will always be found such workers—craftsmen, who, *in the way* of printers' work, will [^28] carry out this business, also, in its own way, not a useless one.

I have said all this in order to explain why I rendered the thoughts of writers collected here freely, striving for one thing: to make the book as accessible and useful as possible to the majority of readers. So that if there should be people wishing to translate this book into other languages, I would advise them not to search out in their own language the passages of the originals of the Englishman Coleridge, the German Kant, the Frenchman Rousseau, but, if they really do want to translate, to translate from mine.

*1904. 28 August.*
