<!-- vol. 41, p. 170 -->

16 March

The chief evil of the science of our time is that, not being able to study *everything* and not knowing, without the help of religion, *what ought* to be studied, it studies only what is *needed* and *pleasant* for the people of science themselves, who live a wrong life.

*Most needed* by them is the existing order, advantageous to them.

*Most pleasant*—the satisfaction of idle curiosity.

1

> The study of natural history has at last reached the point of madness in Germany. Although for God an insect and a man are of equal value, for our reason this is not so. How much a person must put in order before he gets to birds and moths! Study your soul; accustom your mind to caution in judgments, your heart to peaceableness. Learn to know man, and arm yourself with the courage to speak the truth for the good of your neighbors. Sharpen your mind with mathematics, if you find no other means for this; only beware of the classification of bugs, a superficial knowledge of which is completely useless, while an exact one leads off into infinity.

> “But God is infinite in an insect just as in the sun,” you will say. I readily grant this. He is immeasurable in the sand <!-- vol. 41, p. 171 --> of the sea too, whose varieties no one has yet systematized. If you do not feel a particular calling to fish for pearls in those countries where this sand is found, stay here and cultivate your field: it will demand all your diligence; and do not forget that the capacity of your brain is finite. Where some history of a butterfly sits, there might perhaps be room for the thoughts of wise men, which could inspire you.

> *Lichtenberg.*

2

Wisdom is not in knowing much. We cannot possibly know everything. Wisdom is not in knowing as much as possible, but in knowing which knowledge is most needed, which is less needed, and which is still less needed. And of all the knowledge needed by a person, the most important is the knowledge of how to live well, that is, to live so as to do as little evil as possible and as much good as possible. But in our time people study all sorts of unneeded sciences and do not study this one most needed one.

3

> What is the greatest insolence? Not to admit for God such considerations as are incomprehensible to us.

> *Calvin.*

4

> He speaks much who knows little; he who knows is more silent.

> This happens because he who knows little considers everything that he knows important and wants to tell it to everyone. But he who knows much knows also that one can know still far more than what he knows, and therefore speaks only when this is needed by others, and if he is not asked—keeps silent.

> *After Rousseau.*

5

> When a true scholar understands the demands of reason, he tries to carry them out. When an ordinary scholar hears of the demands of reason, he will at times satisfy them, at times <!-- vol. 41, p. 172 --> carrying them out, and at times not carrying them out. When a poor scholar hears of the demands of reason, he mocks them. If reason were not mocked, reason would not be reason.

> *Laozi.*

6

> A significant and necessary proof of intelligence and understanding is already this: if a person knows what questions may be asked. For if a question is absurd in itself and calls forth unneeded answers, then such a question, besides shaming the one who asks it, has the further disadvantage that an incautious listener will involuntarily give an absurd answer to it. And the result is a ridiculous spectacle, as when, in the words of the ancients, one milks a he-goat and the other holds a sieve under it.

> *Kant*

*If all knowledge were knowledge of truth, then all knowledge would be useful. But since knowledge of the false reasonings of people is often passed off as knowledge, one cannot be strict enough in choosing the knowledge one wants to acquire.*
