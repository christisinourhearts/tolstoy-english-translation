<!-- vol. 41, p. 264 -->

18 April

What matters is not the quantity of knowledge but its quality. One can know very much without knowing what is most needed.

1

*It is not shameful and not harmful not to know. No one can know everything, but it is shameful and harmful to pretend that you know what you do not know.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 265 -->

2

> People cannot know and understand all that is done in the world, and therefore their judgments about many things are wrong. A person's ignorance is of two kinds: one ignorance is pure, natural ignorance, in which people are born; the other ignorance is, so to speak, the ignorance of the truly wise. When a person has studied all the sciences and learned all that people knew and know, then he will see that this knowledge, all taken together, is so insignificant that by it there is no possibility of really understanding God's world, and he will be convinced that learned people, in essence, know nothing just as the simple, unlearned ones know nothing. But there are superficial people who have learned a little of something, have snatched up the tops of various sciences, and have grown conceited. They have left natural ignorance behind, but have not had time to reach the true wisdom of those learned men who have understood the imperfection and insignificance of all human knowledge. It is these very people, who consider themselves clever, who trouble the world. They judge everything self-confidently and rashly and, of course, are constantly mistaken. They know how to throw dust in the eyes, and people often treat them with respect, but the simple people despise them, seeing their uselessness; and they for their part despise the people, considering them ignorant.

> *Pascal.*

3

If only certain people were permitted to produce food, and all the rest were forbidden to do so, or were placed in the impossibility of producing food, then the food would not be good. This very thing has happened with the sciences and arts, the monopoly of which one caste has appropriated, but only with the difference that in bodily food there cannot be very great deviations from naturalness, while in spiritual food there can be the very greatest deviations.

4

> Wisdom is a great and vast subject; it demands all the free time that can be devoted to it. However many questions you may have managed to deal with, you will still have to toil over a multitude of questions <!-- vol. 41, p. 266 --> that are subject to investigation and solution. These questions are so vast, so numerous, that they require the removal from consciousness of everything superfluous, in order to give full scope to the work of the mind. Am I to spend my life on words alone? And it often happens that learned men think more about talk than about life. Notice what evil excessive philosophizing gives birth to and how dangerous it can be to truth.

> *Seneca.*

5

> The methodical chatter of the higher schools is often only a general agreement to evade the solution of questions that are hard to resolve, by giving words an unclear, changeable meaning, because the convenient and for the most part indifferent “I do not know” is listened to unwillingly in academies.

> *Kant.*

6

> Truth has to overcome a thousand obstacles in order to reach paper unharmed and from paper to get back into a head. Liars are the weakest enemies of truth. The most dangerous enemies of truth are, first, the enthusiastic writer who speaks of all things and looks at all things as some people do when they have had a little to drink; second, it is the man who considers himself a connoisseur of people, who in every act of a person sees or wants to see his whole life reflected; and finally, the virtuous, pious man who believes everything out of respect, examines nothing of what he learned before the age of fifteen, and builds the little that he does subject to examination on an unexamined foundation. It is these very people who are the most dangerous enemies of truth.

> *Lichtenberg.*

7

> The most ardent defenders of any science, who cannot bear the slightest sidelong glance at it, are usually such persons as have not gone far in it and are secretly conscious of this deficiency in themselves.

> *Lichtenberg.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 267 -->

8

> Culture is a veneer with which ignorance is more often covered than enlightenment.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

9

> A learned man who produces nothing is like a cloud that gives no rain.

> *Eastern wisdom.*

10

> Bad, chiefly, are those writers who strive to express their immediate thoughts in words suited to thoughts that have been well thought out. If they did not do this, but expressed their thoughts in corresponding words, they would always bring in their part, contributing to the improvement of the whole, and would be worthy of attention.

> *Lichtenberg.*

For true knowledge the most harmful thing of all is the use of concepts and words that are not entirely clear. And this is the very thing that would-be learned men do, inventing for an unclear concept unclear, nonexistent, made-up words.
