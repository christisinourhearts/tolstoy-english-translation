<!-- vol. 41, p. 291 -->

30 April

It would seem that one cannot live without knowing what one lives for, and that the first thing a person must make clear to himself is the meaning of his life—all the more since there have been and are people who know this meaning. And yet the majority of people who consider themselves educated pride themselves on having reached that height, as it seems to them, at which they see that life has no meaning whatever.

1

There are two different and opposite outlooks that people have on life.

Some say: I see myself, a being born of my parents, just like all the other living <!-- vol. 41, p. 292 --> beings around me, living in certain conditions which are subject to my investigation and study, and I study myself and other beings, both living and nonliving, and the conditions in which they are placed, and in accordance with this study I arrange my life. Questions of origin I investigate in exactly the same way, and by observation and experiment I attain ever greater and greater knowledge. But questions as to where this whole world came from, why it exists, and why I exist in it, I leave unanswered, since I see no possibility of answering them as definitely, clearly, and demonstrably as I answer questions about the conditions of what exists in the world. And therefore the answers to these questions, which consist in this, that there exists a God from whom I came, and that this God for a certain purpose of his own has determined the law of my life—these answers to the questions I do not accept, as not having the clarity and demonstrability that answers to questions about the causes and conditions of the various phenomena of life have.

So speaks the unbelieving person, and, not admitting the possibility of any other knowledge than that which is acquired by observation and by reasoning upon these observations, he is, if not right, then perfectly rationally consistent.

But the Christian, who recognizes God, says: I am conscious of myself as living only because I am conscious of myself as rational; and being conscious of myself as rational, I cannot fail to recognize that my life and the life of all that exists must also be rational. And in order to be rational, it must have a purpose. And the purpose of this life must be outside me—in that being for the attainment of whose purpose I and all that exists exist. This being exists, and in life I must fulfill its law (will). But the questions as to what that being is which demands of me the fulfillment of its law, and when this rational life arose in me, and how it arises in other beings in time and space, that is, what God is: personal or impersonal, how he created the world and whether he created it, and when the soul arose in me, at what age, and how it arises in others, and where it came from, and where it will go, and in what place in the body it lives—all these questions I must leave unanswered, because I know in advance that in the realm of observation and reasoning upon them I will never come to a final answer, since everything will vanish in the infinity of time and space. For this very reason I do not accept the answers given by science <!-- vol. 41, p. 293 --> as to how the world was conceived, how the soul is conceived, and in what part of the brain it is located.

In the first case: the unbelieving person, recognizing himself only as an animal being and therefore recognizing only what is subject to the external senses, does not recognize the spiritual principle and reconciles himself to the meaninglessness of his existence, which violates the demands of reason.

In the second case: the Christian, recognizing himself only as a rational being and therefore recognizing only what corresponds to the demands of reason, does not recognize the reality of the data of external experience and therefore considers these data fantastic and erroneous.

Both are equally right. But the difference—and an essential one—between them is that according to the first worldview everything in the world is strictly scientific, logical, and rational, except for the very life of man and of the whole world, which have no meaning whatever; and therefore from such a worldview, in spite of all attempts to the contrary, there follow very many interesting and amusing considerations, but nothing follows that is needed for the guidance of life; whereas according to the second worldview the life of man and of the whole world receives a definite and rational meaning and the most direct and simple application of it to life, accessible to all, while the possibility of scientific investigations is not destroyed either, these being thereby put in their proper place.

2

Life is what is revealed through consciousness, and it always and everywhere is. Our error is that we call life what conceals life from us.

3

*The true purpose of life is to come to know infinite life*.

4

A person cannot know why he lives; but he cannot help knowing how he must live.

A workman in a large factory does not know why he does what he does; but he knows, if he is a good workman, how what he does must be done.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 294 -->

5

> There are two views of life among people. Some look at life from the sensual, personal side, supposing that the world is arranged for them and that God was invented for man's needs, and they are indignant at meaningless sufferings and meaningless death. Others have an opposite, spiritual view of life, according to which, on the contrary, man lives for the world, for God, and according to which it is clear that if a man suffers and dies, then it must be so for the life of the world, so it pleases God. According to this second view there is meaning both in our birth and in our suffering life and in our suffering death; according to this view the world is arranged rationally and purposefully, whereas according to the first view everything is meaningless and purposeless.

> And, in accordance with these two views of life, people come by two paths to the truth, to one goal. According to the first, sensual view, a person, not wishing to be defeated, struggles, meets on every side with failures, griefs, weariness, satiety, and illnesses, fills life with suffering, but in the end submits to the force of things, that is, to the law and will of God; he submits unconsciously, involuntarily, like a slave on a chain, with far greater labor and with far less good for himself. But according to the second, divine view, a person consciously goes to meet the truth and, as a rational child of the heavenly father, the father of truth, passes by all those sufferings which make up the lot of the unconscious slave on a chain. And the joys of life, joys and goods that are not artificial but real, natural, and therefore most precious, are given equally to all, independently of the view; and as people enjoy them according to the first view, so they are not taken away from people according to the second view of life either.

> *Buka.*

Every being has organs that show it its place in the world. For man this organ is reason.

If reason does not show you your place in the world and your purpose, then know that what is to blame for this is not a bad arrangement of the world, nor your reason, but the false direction you have given it.
