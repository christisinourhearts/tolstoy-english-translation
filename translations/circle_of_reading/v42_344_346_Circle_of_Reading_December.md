## GARRISON AND HIS “PROCLAMATION”

Garrison, as a man enlightened by the light of Christianity, having begun with a practical aim—the struggle against slavery—very soon understood that the cause of slavery was not the accidental, temporary seizure by the Southerners of several million Negroes, but the long-standing and universal recognition, contrary to Christian teaching, of a right of violence of some people over others. The occasion <!-- vol. 42, p. 345 --> for recognizing this right has always been that evil which people considered it possible to root out or diminish by brute force, that is, by evil as well. And having understood this, Garrison set against slavery not the sufferings of the slaves, not the cruelties of the owners, not the civil equality of people, but the eternal Christian law of non-resistance to evil by violence, non resistance. Garrison understood what the most advanced fighters against slavery did not understand: that the only irrefutable argument against slavery was the denial of the right of one person to the freedom of another under any conditions whatsoever. The abolitionists tried to prove that slavery was unlawful, unprofitable, cruel, corrupting to people, and so on, but the supporters of slavery just as soundly proved the untimeliness, the danger, and the harmful consequences that might come from emancipation. And neither the one side nor the other could convince the other. Garrison, however, understanding that the slavery of the Negroes was only a particular case of universal violence, put forward a general principle with which it was impossible not to agree—namely, that no person has the right under any pretext to rule, that is, to use violence over his fellows. Garrison did not so much insist on the right of the slaves to be free as deny the right of any person or assembly of people whatsoever to compel another person by force to do anything. For the struggle against slavery he put forward the principle of struggle against all the evil of the world.

This principle put forward by Garrison was irrefutable, but it touched, it destroyed, all the foundations of the established order, and therefore people who valued their position under the existing order were frightened by the proclamation, and still more by the application to life, of this principle; they tried to pass it over in silence, to get around it; they hoped to attain their aim without proclaiming and applying to life the principle of non-resistance to violence, which destroyed, as it seemed to them, all good order in human life. And the consequence of this evasion of recognizing the unlawfulness of violence was that fratricidal war which, having resolved the question in an external way, brought a new and perhaps even greater evil into the life of the American people, through the corruption that accompanies every war. But the essence of the question remained unresolved, and the same question, only in a new form, now stands before the people of the United States. Then the question was how to free the Negroes from the violence of the slaveholders; <!-- vol. 42, p. 346 --> now the question is how to free the Negroes from the violence of all the whites, and the whites from the violence of the blacks.

And the resolution of this question in its new form will be accomplished, of course, not by the lynching of Negroes and not by any skillful and liberal measures of American politicians, but only by the application to life of the same principle that was proclaimed by Garrison half a century ago.

Whether people want it or not, only in the name of this principle can people be freed from the enslavement and oppression of one another. Whether people want it or not, this principle lies at the foundation of all the true improvements in the life of people that have been accomplished and are yet to be accomplished. It seems to people that the application of the principle of non-resistance to life in all its fullness at once destroys the whole arrangement of this life, which has cost so dear or been established with such labor; but people forget that the principle of non-resistance is not a principle of violence but of agreement and love, and therefore cannot be made obligatory for people. This principle can only be freely accepted. And to the extent that it is freely accepted by people and applied to life, only to that extent is true progress accomplished in the life of people.

Garrison was the first to proclaim this principle as a rule for the arrangement of the life of people, and in this is his great merit.

Even if he did not then achieve the peaceful liberation of the slaves in America, he pointed out the path to the liberation of all people in general from the power of brute force.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
