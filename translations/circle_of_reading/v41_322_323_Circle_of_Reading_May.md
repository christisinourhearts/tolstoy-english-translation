> DEATH IN THE HOSPITAL

> And now, as I write this, I vividly recall one dying man, a consumptive, that same Mikhailov who lay almost opposite me. Mikhailov himself, however, I knew little. He was still a very young man, about twenty-five, no more, tall, thin, and of extremely comely appearance. He lived in the special section and was strangely silent, always somehow quietly, somehow calmly sad. It was as if he were “withering away” in the prison.

> So at least the convicts spoke of him afterward, among whom he left a good memory of himself. I remember only that he had beautiful eyes. He died at about three in the afternoon, on a frosty and clear day. I remember the sun was piercing right through the green, slightly frosted panes in the windows of our ward with strong, slanting rays. A whole stream of them poured over the unfortunate man. He died unconscious, and hard; he was a long time passing, several hours on end. From the morning on his eyes had already begun not to recognize those who came up to him. They wanted to relieve him somehow; they saw that it was very hard for him; he breathed with difficulty, deeply, with a rattle; his chest rose high, as if he had too little air. He threw off his blanket, all his clothes, and at last began to tear off his shirt. It was terrible to look at this long, long body, with legs and arms withered to the bone, with sunken belly, with raised chest, with ribs standing out distinctly, as in a skeleton. On his whole body there remained only a wooden cross with an amulet bag, and the shackles, through which, it seemed, he could now have slipped his withered leg. Half an hour before his death everyone among us seemed to grow hushed; they began to talk almost in a whisper. Whoever walked about stepped somehow inaudibly. They talked little among themselves, about unrelated things, only occasionally glancing at the dying man, who rattled more and more. At last with a wandering and unsteady hand he felt for the amulet bag on his chest and began to tear it off, as if it too were a burden to him, troubled him, pressed on him. They took off the amulet bag too. About ten minutes later he died. They knocked on the door for the sentry, let it be known. A watchman came in, looked dully at the dead man, and went off for the doctor's assistant. The doctor's assistant, a young and kind fellow, came soon; with quick steps, treading loudly through the hushed ward, he went up to the dead man and with a somehow especially offhand air took his pulse, <!-- vol. 41, p. 323 --> felt it, waved his hand, and went out. At once they went to inform the guard: the criminal was an important one, of the special section; even recognizing him as dead had to be done with special ceremonies.

> While waiting for the guards, one of the convicts suggested in a low voice that it would not be a bad thing to close the dead man's eyes. Another listened to him attentively, silently went up to the dead man, and closed his eyes. Seeing the cross lying right there on the pillow, he took it, looked it over, and silently put it on Mikhailov's neck again, put it on and crossed himself. Meanwhile the dead face was stiffening; a ray of light played on it; the mouth was half open; two rows of white young teeth gleamed from under the thin lips stuck to the gums. At last the noncommissioned officer of the guard came in, wearing his short sword and his helmet, followed by two watchmen. He came forward, slowing his steps more and more, glancing in perplexity at the hushed convicts, who were looking sternly at him from all sides. Coming within a step of the dead man, he stopped as if rooted to the ground, as though he had lost his nerve. The completely naked, withered corpse, in nothing but its shackles, struck him, and he suddenly unfastened the chin scales, took off his helmet, which was not at all required, and crossed himself broadly. It was a stern, gray, soldierly face. I remember that at this same moment Chekunov was standing right there, also a gray-haired old man. All the while he had been looking silently and intently into the noncommissioned officer's face, point-blank, and peering with a sort of strange attention at his every gesture. But their eyes met, and Chekunov's lower lip suddenly for some reason trembled. He twisted it somehow strangely, bared his teeth, and quickly, as if inadvertently, nodding to the noncommissioned officer toward the dead man, said:

> — He had a mother too, after all! — and walked away.

> But now they began to lift the corpse, lifted it together with the cot; the straw crunched; the shackles, amid the general silence, clanked ringingly against the floor... They were picked up. The body was carried out. Suddenly everyone began talking loudly. One could hear the noncommissioned officer, already in the corridor, sending someone for the blacksmith. The dead man had to be unshackled...

> *Dostoevsky (from “Notes from the House of the Dead”).*
