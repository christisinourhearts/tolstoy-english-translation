> THE FUGITIVE

> It was a long procedure. First Pashka walked with his mother in the rain, now across a mown field, now along forest paths, where yellow leaves stuck to his boots; he walked until it grew light. Then for about two hours he stood in the dark entryway and waited for the door to be unlocked. In the entryway it was not as cold and damp as outside, but when the wind blew, spatters of rain flew in here too. When little by little the entryway became packed with people, Pashka, squeezed in, pressed his face against someone's sheepskin coat, which smelled strongly of salted fish, and dozed off. But now the bolt clicked, the door flew open, and Pashka and his mother went into the waiting room. Here again they had to wait a long time. All the patients sat on the benches, did not stir, and were silent. Pashka looked them over and was silent too, although he saw much that was strange and funny. Only once, when some young fellow came into the waiting room hopping on one leg, Pashka himself wanted to hop like that too; he nudged his mother under the elbow, snorted into his sleeve, and said:

> — Mama, look: a sparrow!

> — Hush, child, hush! — said his mother.

> A sleepy doctor's assistant appeared in the little window.

> — Come up and register! — he boomed in a bass voice.

> Everyone, including the funny hopping fellow, moved toward the little window. The doctor's assistant asked each one his name and patronymic, his age, his place of residence, whether he had been sick long, and so on. From his mother's answers Pashka learned that his name was not Pashka but Pavel Galaktionov, that he was seven years old, that he was illiterate, and that he had been sick ever since Easter.

> Soon after the registering they had to stand up for a short while; a doctor in a white apron and belted with a towel passed through the waiting room. As he passed the hopping fellow, he shrugged his shoulders and said in a singsong tenor:

> — Well, what a fool! Why, aren't you a fool? I told you to come on Monday, and you come on Friday. As far as I'm concerned, you needn't come at all, but, you great fool, the leg will be lost!

> The fellow made such a pitiful face, as if he were getting ready to beg for alms, blinked, and said:

> — Do me the kindness, Ivan Mikolaich!

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 26 --> — Never mind your Ivan Mikolaich! — the doctor mimicked. — You were told Monday, and you have to obey. A fool, and that's all...

> The receiving of patients began. The doctor sat in his little room and called out the patients in turn. Every now and then piercing howls, a child's crying, or the doctor's angry exclamations could be heard from the little room:

> — Well, what are you bawling for? Am I cutting you, or what? Sit still!

> Pashka's turn came.

> — Pavel Galaktionov! — shouted the doctor.

> His mother was struck dumb, as if she had not expected this call, and, taking Pashka by the hand, led him into the little room. The doctor was sitting at the table and mechanically tapping on a thick book with a little hammer.

> — What hurts? — he asked, without looking at those who had come in.

> — The boy has a sore on his elbow, sir, — answered his mother, and her face took on such an expression as if she really were terribly grieved by Pashka's sore.

> — Undress him!

> Pashka, puffing, unwound the kerchief from his neck, then wiped his nose on his sleeve and began without hurrying to pull off his little sheepskin coat.

> — Woman, you haven't come here on a visit! — the doctor said angrily. — What are you fussing about? You're not the only one I've got here, you know.

> Pashka hurriedly threw the little coat down on the floor and with his mother's help took off his shirt... The doctor looked at him lazily and patted him on his bare belly.

> — That's a grand paunch you've grown yourself, brother Pashka, — he said, and sighed. — Well, show me your elbow.

> Pashka looked askance at the basin of bloody slops, looked at the doctor's apron, and began to cry.

> — Me-e! — the doctor mimicked. — Time to marry the little scamp off, and he's bawling! Shameless!

> Trying not to cry, Pashka looked at his mother, and in this look of his a request was written: “Now don't you tell at home that I cried in the hospital!”

> The doctor examined his elbow, pressed it, sighed, smacked his lips, then pressed it again.

> — You ought to be beaten, woman, but there's no one to do it, — he said. — Why didn't you bring him sooner? Why, the arm is as good as lost! Look here, you fool, it's the joint that's ailing!

> — You know best, sir... — sighed the woman.

> <!-- vol. 42, p. 27 --> — Sir... You've let the boy's arm rot, and now it's sir! What kind of workman is he without an arm? Now you'll be nursing him your whole life long. I'll bet when you get a pimple on your own nose, you run off to the hospital straightaway, but the boy you let rot for half a year. You're all like that.

> The doctor lit a cigarette. While the cigarette smoked, he scolded the woman and nodded his head in time to a song which he was humming in his mind, and kept thinking about something. Naked Pashka stood before him, listening and looking at the smoke. But when the cigarette went out, the doctor roused himself and began speaking a tone lower:

> — Well, listen, woman. Ointments and drops won't help here. He has to be left in the hospital.

> — If it's needed, sir, then why not leave him?

> — We'll do an operation on him. And you, Pashka, stay, — said the doctor, patting Pashka on the shoulder. — Let your mother go, and you and I, brother, will stay here. It's nice at my place, brother, a regular paradise! You and I, Pashka, as soon as we've finished here, will go and catch siskins, I'll show you a fox! We'll go visiting together! Eh? Want to? And your mother will come for you tomorrow! Eh?

> Pashka looked questioningly at his mother.

> — Stay, child! — she said.

> — He's staying, he's staying! — the doctor cried merrily. — And there's nothing to talk about! I'll show him a live fox! We'll go to the fair together to buy candies! Marya Denisovna, take him upstairs!

> The doctor, evidently a cheerful and easygoing fellow, was glad of company; Pashka wanted to oblige him, all the more since he had never in his life been to a fair and would gladly have had a look at a live fox, but how could he do without his mother? After thinking a little, he decided to ask the doctor to leave his mother in the hospital too, but before he had time to open his mouth, the woman assistant was already leading him up the stairs. He walked along and, with his mouth open, looked from side to side. The stairs, the floors, and the doorposts—everything huge, straight, and bright—were painted a magnificent yellow and gave off a delicious smell of vegetable oil. Everywhere lamps hung, runners stretched along the floors, copper taps stuck out of the walls. But most of all Pashka liked the bed on which they sat him, and the gray, rough blanket. He felt the pillows and the blanket with his hands, looked around the ward, and decided that the doctor lived very nicely.

> The ward was small and consisted of only three beds. One bed stood empty, the second was taken by Pashka, <!-- vol. 42, p. 28 --> and on the third sat an old man with sour eyes, who coughed all the time and spat into a mug. From Pashka's bed part of another ward with two beds could be seen through the door: on one slept a very pale, thin man with a rubber bladder on his head; on the other, with his arms spread, sat a peasant with a bandaged head, very much like a woman.

> The woman assistant, having sat Pashka down, went out and a little later came back holding a heap of clothes in her arms.

> — This is for you, — she said. — Get dressed.

> Pashka undressed and, not without pleasure, began to put on his new clothes. Having put on the shirt, the trousers, and the little gray robe, he looked himself over complacently and thought that it would not be bad to take a stroll through the village in such a costume. His imagination pictured how his mother sends him to the kitchen garden by the river to pick cabbage leaves for the piglet; he walks along, and the boys and girls have surrounded him and are looking with envy at his little robe.

> A nurse came into the ward, holding two tin bowls, spoons, and two pieces of bread. One bowl she set before the old man, the other before Pashka.

> — Eat! — she said.

> Glancing into the bowl, Pashka saw rich cabbage soup, and in the soup a piece of meat, and again he thought that the doctor lived very nicely and that the doctor was not at all as cross as he had seemed at first. For a long time he ate the soup, licking the spoon after each spoonful, then, when nothing but the meat was left in the bowl, he glanced at the old man and envied him because he was still slurping. With a sigh he started on the meat, trying to eat it as long as possible, but his efforts came to nothing: soon the meat too disappeared. Only a piece of bread was left. It is not tasty to eat bread alone with nothing to go with it, but there was nothing to be done. Pashka thought a while and ate the bread. At that moment the nurse came in with fresh bowls. This time the bowls held roast meat with potatoes.

> — And where's your bread? — asked the nurse.

> Instead of answering, Pashka puffed out his cheeks and blew out the air.

> — Well, what did you gobble it up for? — the nurse said reproachfully. — And what are you going to eat the roast with?

> She went out and brought a fresh piece of bread. Pashka had never in his life eaten roast meat and, having tried it now, found that it was very tasty. It disappeared quickly, and after it a bigger piece of bread was left over than after the soup. The old man, having had his dinner, put away his <!-- vol. 42, p. 29 --> leftover bread in the little table; Pashka wanted to do the same, but thought a while and ate his piece.

> Having eaten his fill, he went for a stroll. In the next ward, besides those he had seen through the door, there were four more people. Of these only one drew his attention. This was a tall, extremely emaciated peasant with a gloomy, hairy face; he sat on his bed and all the time, like a pendulum, nodded his head and waved his right arm. For a long time Pashka could not take his eyes off him. At first the peasant's pendulum-like, measured noddings seemed to him comical, done for everyone's amusement, but when he looked closely into the peasant's face, he felt frightened, and he understood that this peasant was unbearably ill. Going on into the third ward, he saw two peasants with dark-red faces, as if smeared with clay. They sat motionless on their beds, and with their strange faces, on which it was hard to make out the features, they looked like little heathen gods.

> — Auntie, why are they like that? — Pashka asked the nurse.

> — They've got the smallpox, boy.

> Returning to his own ward, Pashka sat down on the bed and began waiting for the doctor, so as to go with him to catch siskins or to drive to the fair. But the doctor did not come. In the doorway of the next ward the doctor's assistant appeared briefly. He bent over the patient who had a bag of ice lying on his head, and shouted:

> — Mikhailo!

> The sleeping Mikhailo did not stir. The doctor's assistant waved his hand and went away. While waiting for the doctor, Pashka examined his neighbor, the old man. The old man coughed without stopping and spat into the mug; his cough was long-drawn and creaking. Pashka liked one peculiarity of the old man's: when, coughing, he drew in breath, something in his chest whistled and sang in different voices.

> — Grandad, what's that whistling in you? — asked Pashka.

> The old man made no answer. Pashka waited a little and asked:

> — Grandad, and where's the fox?

> — What fox?

> — The live one.

> — Where would it be? In the forest!

> Much time passed, but the doctor still did not appear. The nurse brought tea and scolded Pashka for not having saved himself any bread for his tea; the doctor's assistant came once more and set about waking <!-- vol. 42, p. 30 --> Mikhailo; outside the windows it turned blue, lights were lit in the wards, but the doctor did not show himself. It was already too late to drive to the fair and catch siskins; Pashka stretched out on his bed and began to think. He remembered the candies the doctor had promised, his mother's face and voice, the darkness in his hut, the stove, grumbling old Granny Yegorovna... and he suddenly felt dreary and sad. He remembered that tomorrow his mother would come for him, smiled, and closed his eyes.

> He was awakened by a rustling. In the next ward someone was walking about and speaking in a half-whisper. By the dim light of the night-lights and icon lamps, three figures were moving about near Mikhailo's bed.

> — Shall we carry him with the bed, or just as he is? — asked one of them.

> — Just as he is. You won't get through with the bed. There now, he picked the wrong time to die, the kingdom of heaven be his!

> One took Mikhailo by the shoulders, another by the legs, and they lifted him: Mikhailo's arms and the skirts of his robe hung limply in the air. The third—it was the peasant who looked like a woman—began crossing himself, and all three, stamping their feet out of step and treading on the skirts of Mikhailo's robe, went out of the ward.

> In the chest of the sleeping old man the whistling and the many-voiced singing could be heard. Pashka listened, glanced at the dark windows, and in terror jumped up from the bed.

> — Ma-a-ma! — he moaned in a deep bass.

> And without waiting for an answer, he rushed into the next ward. Here the light of the icon lamp and the night-light barely lightened the darkness; the patients, disturbed by Mikhailo's death, were sitting on their beds; mingling with the shadows, disheveled, they appeared broader, taller, and seemed to be growing bigger and bigger; on the end bed in the corner, where it was darker, a peasant sat nodding his head and his hand.

> Pashka, not making out the doors, rushed into the smallpox ward, from there into the corridor, and from the corridor flew into a big room where monsters with long hair and old women's faces lay and sat on the beds. Running through the women's section, he again found himself in the corridor, saw the banisters of the familiar staircase, and ran down. Here he recognized the waiting room in which he had sat in the morning, and began looking for the outer door.

> The bolt clicked, a cold wind blew in, and Pashka, stumbling, ran out into the yard. He had one thought—to run and run! He did not know the way, but he was sure that if he ran, he would certainly find himself at home with his mother. The night was overcast, <!-- vol. 42, p. 31 --> but the moon was shining behind the clouds. Pashka ran from the porch straight ahead, went around a shed, and ran into bare bushes: after standing a little and thinking, he dashed back toward the hospital, ran around it, and stopped again in indecision: behind the hospital building grave crosses showed white.

> — Ma-ammy! — he cried, and rushed back.

> Running past the dark, grim buildings, he saw one lighted window.

> The bright red patch in the darkness seemed terrible, but Pashka, out of his mind with fear, not knowing where to run, turned toward it. Next to the window was a porch with steps and a front door with a white plate; Pashka ran up the steps, glanced in at the window, and a sharp, breathtaking joy suddenly seized him. Through the window he saw the cheerful, easygoing doctor, who was sitting at the table and reading a book. Laughing with happiness, Pashka stretched out his hands toward the familiar face, wanted to shout, but an unknown force squeezed his breath and struck at his legs; he staggered and fell senseless on the steps.

> When he came to himself, it was already light, and a very familiar voice, which yesterday had promised the fair, siskins, and a fox, was saying beside him:

> — Well, what a fool, Pashka! Aren't you a fool? You ought to be beaten, but there's no one to do it.

> *Anton Chekhov.*
