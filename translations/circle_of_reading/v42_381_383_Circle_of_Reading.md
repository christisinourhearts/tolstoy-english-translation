<!-- vol. 42, p. 381 -->

27 December

Everything we see and know we see and know not as it is in reality, but as it is presented to our cognitive faculty.

1

> Heaven and earth are great, but they have color, form, and size. In man, however, there is something that has neither color, nor form, nor number, nor size—and this something is rational.

> If the world in itself were inanimate, it would be animated only by the reason of man. But the world is infinite, while the reason of man is limited, and therefore the reason of man cannot be the reason of the whole world.

> From this it is clear that the world must be animated by reason, and this reason must be infinite.

> *Confucius.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 382 -->

2

> When people speak of heaven as the place where the blessed are, they usually imagine it somewhere high above themselves in the immeasurable spaces of the universe. But in doing so they forget that our earth, viewed from those spaces of the universe, likewise appears as one of the stars of heaven, and that the inhabitants of those worlds could with the same right point to the earth and say: “Do you see that star over there—the place of eternal bliss, the heavenly refuge prepared for us, where we will someday go.” The fact is that, by a strange error of our mind, the flight of our faith is always joined with the notion of ascending upward, and people do not think that however high we may rise, we will still have to come down again in order to stand with a firm foot in some other world.

> *Kant.*

3

> Instead of saying that the world is reflected in us, one should rather say that our reason is reflected in the world. We cannot do otherwise: we must recognize order and wise governance in the world—this follows from the structure of our thinking faculties. But it by no means follows from this that something necessary for our thinking is so in reality as well, since of the real structure of the external world we have no notion whatever.

> *Lichtenberg.*

4

> Look at this dressed-up shadow, frail, possessed by desires, there is no strength in it, it cannot defend itself, this body is exhausted, frail and infirm, as if it were ready to fall to pieces, life in it is already passing into death... The bare skull is like a gourd plucked in autumn... Can one rejoice, can one still make merry?

> This fortress was made for bones, covered with flesh and soaked with the juice of blood, and now—old age and death dwell in it, pride and arrogance... The precious chariots of kings fall into ruin, old age brings the body near to ruin; only the teaching of the good does not grow old, does not fall into ruin.

> *Buddhist wisdom.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 383 -->

5

*A person need only look at himself as a merely bodily being, and he becomes an insoluble riddle, a contradiction that cannot be untangled.*

To understand the true meaning of things, it is necessary to reduce everything visible to the invisible, everything bodily to the spiritual.
