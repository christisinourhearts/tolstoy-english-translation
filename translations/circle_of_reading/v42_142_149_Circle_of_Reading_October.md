## THE LAW OF GOD AND THE LAW OF THIS WORLD

Only *faith* can preserve a person in the world from delusions and the snares of the devil; it alone teaches us to distinguish good and evil; by it alone do we partake of things spiritual and divine.

In our time people believe much that they should not believe; the true Christian faith is considered a delusion and heresy, and dead customs are taken for faith. A division has arisen among people: *one side accuses the other of heresy*, and because of this there arise wars and quarrels, killings, the burning of people, and many other sins; so that faith is not easy to recognize now, for all of it reeks of heresy and enmity. In such circumstances reasonable people must keep the true faith, which was set forth by the apostles and was given once by God through J. Christ, and not be carried away by those new beliefs to which people are now being urged.

Among the first Christians the apostles established equality: no one was obliged to anyone in anything, but all were to love one another and serve one another out of love, forming one body joined together of many members and having Christ as the head. Among them there were no rulers with pagan offices: judges, city councilors. Although the Christians lived under the power of pagans, to whom they had to pay tribute, they themselves did not hold pagan offices. *This went on for more than three hundred years*, until Constantine: he was the first to intrude into the midst of the Christians with pagan dominion and with officials such as befitted pagans. The aim toward which the apostles led the Christians was far loftier and more perfect than the one that the pagan authorities pursued, for to form one body and to be guided by the one spirit of God in religious and moral aims is far higher than to observe that earthly justice which is maintained by the pagan authorities by means of various coercive measures.

Lawsuits in the courts, although they help to recover property that has been taken away, lead into sins from which Christians can be freed in no other way than by renouncing such lawsuits. Christians must cause injustice to no one and must not <!-- vol. 42, p. 143 --> deceive anyone, and *the injustice* done to them *they must patiently bear, not repaying evil for evil.*

The mutual relations established by the apostles among the first Christians were based on the law of Christ, which determines how one must deal with opponents of the faith, tempters, heretics: they must be exhorted and reproved *first one on one*, and if this fails, before witnesses, and finally the church must be told about them; and if they disobey the church too, then one must deal with them as with pagans and tax collectors, that is, not associate with them. In the same sense the apostle forbids association with adulterers and others. Such a Gospel structure of society can correct the corrupted human race sooner than the pagan one, with the help of earthly kings and city judges: under the first, the sinner can again acquire the grace of God, of which his sins have deprived him, while under the second, death is appointed for all such sinners.

And so the one law of Christ was fully sufficient for ordering the communities of the first Christians, and, guided by it alone, they prospered morally; but afterward, when two laws were mixed in with them, the civil and the papal, morality began to decline. This is acknowledged by those who write chronicles, and we see with our own eyes how these two laws destroy and kill faith and the law of God. Therefore we, late descendants, sitting as it were under the shadow of these laws, speak uncertainly of the law of God and the governance of God, for the darkness of these two laws screens our eyes. Therefore, groping, so to speak, and guessing, I put the question: *is the law of Christ sufficient*, without the human laws added to it, to found and order here, on the earthly way, a fully Christian religion? I answer, though with trembling: yes, it is sufficient now as well, because before, too, it was sufficient for the ordering of a Christian society. The law of Christ is weakened *neither by the resistance* that is offered to it *nor by the multitude* of those converted to it: on the contrary, from this it has acquired still greater strength, and therefore it alone is always sufficient. Further, if it was *sufficient for the conversion* of unbelievers to the faith, *then it is sufficient for ordering* life and morals as well, for the latter is easier. And since governance with the help of the teaching of Christ is better than with the help of human admixtures, who will doubt that people would be more perfect being guided <!-- vol. 42, p. 144 --> by the law of God than giving themselves various admixtures to drink, like poison.

Civil law, or *the law of pagan kings*, has the aim of establishing justice among people in everything that concerns the human body and bodily property; the law of the Gospel, on the contrary, has as its aim the spiritual perfecting of people. Since pagans place their good only in the safety of body and property, they hold to civil governance. In just the same way, those Christians who have turned to paganism, having rejected God and his law, and who strive only for earthly pleasures, for freedom and ease in the world, and for bodily enrichment, they too stand for the secular power, which indulges their desires and, in case of danger threatening their life or property, puts weapons to use or makes it possible to recover lost property through the courts. The justice that the secular power seeks to establish is necessary for the rulers themselves: if one went against another and in general did evil to another, the kingdom too would be destroyed. The secular power does not concern itself with the other virtues and therefore, apart from injustice, permits all other sins.

*The governance of Christ* orders a person spiritually in the virtues and brings him to such innocence in which he can please God and earn a reward in eternity. Under this governance a person relates to bodily privations quite differently: he does not take revenge for them and does not seek satisfaction in court, but patiently bears them.

Equality was established among Christians, and no one was to raise himself above others; therefore a true Christian would never dare to make himself king over Christians. Besides, the apostolic commandment is binding on Christians—to bear one another's burdens: *how then can a good Christian bring himself to be a burden to others, by becoming king?*

That royal power is a heavy burden for subjects is evident from the fact that after the death of Solomon the Jews asked his son to relieve them of the cruel labor of his father and of his heavy yoke, and Rehoboam, having taken counsel with fools such as he was himself, answered harshly: “My finger will be heavier to you than my father's back.” From this it is evident that even the most wise Solomon was by his power a heavy burden for the people.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 145 --> Jesus Christ himself forbade his disciples to exalt themselves over one another: “Kings rule over the peoples, and those who have power over them are called benefactors. But you are not to be so: rather, whoever among you is greater, let him be as the lesser, and he who governs as he who serves.” And in the Old Testament Gideon refused the offer of the Jews to be king over them and answered: “I shall not have dominion over you, neither shall my son have dominion over you: let the Lord rule over you.”

Love for God cannot be called forth in a person by coercive measures: it is based on the free will of man and is born of the word of God. And if the king is to correct evil people by preaching the word of God, then he will turn into a priest and will not resort to that power which corrects people in no other way than by hanging them.

To people who appropriate pagan power in order to arrange a luxurious life for themselves at the cost of the sufferings of others, the Old Testament *parable of the trees,* which turned to the olive, the fig tree, and the vine with the request to reign over them, can be applied. Neither the first, nor the second, nor the third agreed, because they would have had to lose everything that made up their charm, and only the thornbush answered: “If you have chosen me king, then come under my shade, and if you do not want to, then let fire come out of me and devour the cedars of Lebanon.”

People who possess the gifts of God's grace will not exchange them for the goods of the body and of the world, for dominion and advancement, knowing that all this brings with it cruelty, mercilessness, violence, and robbery of their own brothers; but the thornbush, sharp and cruel, boldly says: “Since you have chosen me master, know that I am your master and will rule over you so that on some not even the skin will remain whole: I will clip their wings, I will strip the peasant like a linden.” And another will say to this besides: “Never mind! Strip the peasant: he will recover, like a willow by the water.” People living in luxury, with a fat belly overgrown with lard, justify such treatment of the common people.

No human law can further the moral perfecting of people to such a degree as the law of God. The law of Moses was a good law, but a Christian ruler cannot be guided by this law, since it has already been transferred and replaced by another law—the law of Christ, and the law of Christ is all founded on love for God and neighbor.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 146 --> The interference of two lords, the secular and the spiritual, in the Christian church disrupted that condition of purity and innocence in which it was established by the apostles and remained for three hundred and twenty years. And although many people consider this interference useful for the faith, this poison never was and never will be faith, but will always remain a poison, poisoning people and deadening faith; and therefore Christians must remember that, keeping the true faith, they cannot rule over others after the pagan custom. And meanwhile the apostles of Antichrist consider this secular power the third part of the church.

According to the teaching of the Roman church, the secular power is founded on holy scripture, and first of all on the following text: “Soldiers also asked him: And what shall we do? And he said to them: Do not wrong anyone, do not slander, and be content with your pay” (Luke 3:14).

These words in themselves could not have sharpened a sword for Christians, so that they could shed human blood with them, but the great pillar of the Roman church (Augustine), who strongly props it up so that it should not fall, gave this passage the meaning of a sharp sword among Christians. He expresses himself thus: “If Christian teaching wholly condemned war, then the soldiers who turned to John would rather have been given the saving counsel to lay down their arms and leave the military calling; but if he bids them be content with their stipends, then he does not reject the military calling and does not censure war.”

The second passage to which the Roman church refers is the following: “Let every soul be subject to the higher powers, for there is no power not from God; the powers that exist are established by God,” and so on (Romans 13:1 ff.). This is the chief foundation on which learned men establish the secular power, and one master of the University of Prague told me that I too must acknowledge this, and if I do not acknowledge it, I shall be a heretic.

Here are some more of the master's arguments in proof that human law, which punishes people with death for certain acts, does not contradict the law of God: 1) The commandment “do not kill” does not forbid punishing the guilty with death, for in such cases it is not the judge who kills, but the law compels him to it; 2) God multiplies life and death, therefore he can also kill: I shall kill, and I shall make alive; and kings are set up by God and therefore can act in the same way; 3) the apostle Paul says: “They that do such things <!-- vol. 42, p. 147 --> are worthy of death, and not in vain does he bear the sword”; 4) in the Gospel: “But those enemies of mine who did not want me to reign over them, bring here and slay before me”; 5) Cyprian, concerning the Old Testament commandment to kill idolaters, says that if such a commandment existed before the coming of Christ, all the more must it be kept after his coming, as is confirmed by the words of the apostle Paul: “They that do such things are worthy of death.”

Augustine and Jerome interpret the commandment “do not kill” in the same vein.

St. Gregory, and again St. Augustine, reason about this in the same way. From all these arguments it follows that they want to make God two-mouthed, so that with one mouth he says: do not kill, and with the other—kill.

Jesus is now very poor; crowds of people no longer follow him, unless some outcast and unreasoning person drags pitifully after him, like a fly out of slops. But the learned men are very rich and renowned in the world, they have begotten many servants of God with the sword, and the whole world looks up to them. The wise man of the world will look at Jesus, see that he is abandoned by all, clothed in poverty, suffering hardships, and he will leave him and go to the learned men, who, according to their laws, serve God in whole crowds in churches, in war, at tortures, in government offices, at the pillories, and at the gallows. The wise man of the world will lay hold of such a broad service of God, and only a madman will follow Jesus, and the world will hiss at him.

Most of all, service with the sword is contrary to the teaching of Christ, because it consists wholly in repaying evil for evil. Although they make the reservation that the sword is raised not for their own cause but for God's, God knows how sincere this reservation is: if it were so, people would not take revenge for the offenses and injustices done to themselves; but in fact it turns out that they do not leave the slightest offense in word without retribution, while they allow outrage against God. Christ, on the contrary, commanded love for one's enemies and repaying them with good for evil. Not received by the Samaritans, he did not allow the apostles to bring down fire from heaven. Christ cared more for the souls of his enemies than for his own temporal sufferings. If people believed the words of Christ and followed his example, there would be no wars on earth. Both battles, and other killings, and all hostile attacks on others, and all repaying of evil for evil come only from this, that we do not love <!-- vol. 42, p. 148 --> our enemies and do not bear with patience the offenses done to us.

With the order of a truly Christian society, as shown in holy scripture, the sword and all its deeds, that is, battles and every kind of bloodshed, have nothing in common, as being contrary to the calling of Christians and to those virtues which befit them. Christians are united by the one faith of Christ, pray for one another: forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors, are bound together by a union of love and peace: can, after all this, any of the old monks glorified as saints prove on the basis of faith that Christians must have battles and killings? Christians who raise wars and commit other bloodshed are Christians only in name and follow the pagans, with only this difference, that the pagans did not know God and had no share in those spiritual goods to which Christians lay claim. Nor can one compare battles between Christians with the battles of the Jews, for the latter were permitted by the law.

Christians who kill one another in battles are in any case deprived of a share in the spiritual goods promised by Christ. If they justify themselves by saying that they have too much to do with the world and therefore have no time to think about higher spiritual matters and to comprehend them, then one can answer them briefly: in vain do they believe in Christ, in vain are they baptized. But if Christians consider themselves sharers in the sufferings of Christ and hope for salvation, and at the same time crucify Christ in themselves by killing one another, then punishment and curses greater than those of the pagans await them.

Battles between Christians are contrary to the law of Christian love, which forbids every hostile attack on one's neighbor, on his body, soul, property, honor—by deed or by word, and teaches one to bear without murmuring those injustices which others do to us.

The apostle defines the mutual relations between Christians thus: “Owe no one anything except mutual love” (Romans 13:8). In these words the difference between the works of faith and the works of pagan dominion makes itself felt: the one cannot be the other. Therefore the union of paganism with Christianity could not take place at the very beginning either. In the beginning some found consolation in drinking the blood of Christ, others in shedding <!-- vol. 42, p. 149 --> human blood; but now both the one and the other have united in a common service of God: they drink the blood of Christ and draw the blood of their neighbors.

There are two extremes: either to reject God completely and fall away from him, or to cleave to him with all one's heart. But neither the one nor the other is easy for people; for man is not so vicious as to reject God wholly; on the other hand, few will be found who would want to cleave to God with all their heart. Faith founded on papal laws represents something midway between the one and the other, and most people rest content with this. It prescribes various good acts, false and imagined, which are expressed in various external rites, and people think that they keep the true faith, confessing God with the mouth alone and expressing their reverence by external signs alone.

*Petr Chelčický (in the 15th century).*
