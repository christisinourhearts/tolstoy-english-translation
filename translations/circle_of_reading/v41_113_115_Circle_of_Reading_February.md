## II THE FREE MAN

Nekhlyudov stood at the edge of the ferry, looking at the broad, swift river. From the town came over the water the hum and brassy trembling of the big volunteers' bell. The driver standing beside Nekhlyudov and all the carters one after another took off their caps and crossed themselves. But a short, shaggy old man standing nearest of all at the railing, whom Nekhlyudov had not noticed at first, did not cross himself, but, raising his head, stared at Nekhlyudov. This old man was dressed in a patched peasant coat, cloth trousers, and worn-out, <!-- vol. 41, p. 114 --> patched high boots. On his back was a small bag, on his head a tall fur cap, rubbed bare.

— What's this, old man, you don't pray? — said Nekhlyudov's driver, putting on his cap. — Or are you unbaptized?

— Pray to whom, then? — said the shaggy old man, pronouncing syllable after syllable decisively, aggressively, and quickly.

— Everyone knows to whom—to God, — the driver said ironically.

— And you show me, where is he? God, I mean?

There was something so serious and firm in the expression of the old man's face that the driver, feeling that he was dealing with a strong man, became somewhat confused, but did not show it, and, trying not to fall silent and not to disgrace himself before the listening public, answered quickly:

— Where? Everyone knows, in heaven.

— And have you been there?

— Been or not been, everyone knows that one has to pray to God.

— God hath no man seen anywhere at all. The only-begotten son, which is in the bosom of the father, he hath revealed, — said the old man, frowning sternly, in the same rapid patter.

— You must be a heathen, a hole-worshipper. You pray to a hole, — said the driver, thrusting the whip handle into his belt and straightening the breeching on the trace horse.

Someone laughed.

— And what faith are you of, grandfather? — asked a man, no longer young, who was standing with a cart at the edge of the ferry.

— I have no faith of any kind. Because I believe no one, no one, except myself, — the old man answered just as quickly and decisively.

— But how can one believe oneself? — said Nekhlyudov, entering the conversation. — One can be mistaken.

— Never in my life, — the old man answered decisively, shaking his head.

— Then why are there different faiths? — asked Nekhlyudov.

— There are different faiths just because people believe people and don't believe themselves. I believed people too, and wandered as in the taiga; got so lost I had no hope of getting out. And Old Believers, and New Believers, and Sabbatarians, and Khlysty, and Popovtsy, and Bespopovtsy, and Austrians, and Molokans, and Skoptsy. Every faith praises itself alone. So they have all crawled apart like blind kutyata [^1]. There are many faiths, but the spirit is one. In you, and in <!-- vol. 41, p. 115 --> me, and in him. That means, let everyone believe his own spirit, and then all will be united. Let everyone be his own, and all will be as one.

The old man spoke loudly and kept looking around, evidently wishing as many people as possible to hear him.

— And have you professed this for long? — asked Nekhlyudov.

— Me? A long time now. This is the twenty-third year they have been persecuting me.

— How do they persecute you?

— As they persecuted Christ, so they persecute me. They seize me and lead me around to the courts, to the priests—to the scribes, to the Pharisees; they put me in a madhouse. But nothing can be done to me, because I am free. “What, they say, is your name?” They think I'll take some title upon myself. But I take none. I have renounced everything: I have no name, no place, no fatherland—I have nothing. I am my own. “Called what?” — Man. — “And how many years?” — I don't count, I say, and it can't be counted, because I always was and always will be. — “Of what father and mother, they say, are you?” — I have, I say, neither father nor mother, except God and the earth. God is father, the earth is mother. — “And the tsar, they say, do you recognize him?” — Why not recognize him? He is his own tsar, and I am my own tsar. — “Well, they say, there's no talking with you.” I say: I'm not asking you to talk with me. And so they torment me.

— And where are you going now? — asked Nekhlyudov.

— Wherever God leads. I work, and when there's no work—I beg, — the old man finished, noticing that the ferry was coming up to the far bank, and he looked round triumphantly at all who had been listening to him.

The ferry put in at the other bank. Nekhlyudov took out his purse and offered the old man money. The old man refused.

— I don't take that. I take bread, — he said.

— Well, farewell.

— There's nothing to forgive. You have not offended me. And it isn't possible to offend me, — said the old man, and began putting on his shoulder the bag he had taken off.

Meanwhile the post cart had been rolled out and the horses harnessed.

— And why bother talking, sir, — the driver said to Nekhlyudov when, having tipped the ferrymen, he had climbed into the cart. — Just a good-for-nothing tramp.

*L. N. Tolstoy. (From the novel “Resurrection”.)*
