> II <br> A SEA VOYAGE

> I was sailing from Hamburg to London. There were two of us passengers: I and a little monkey, a female of the ouistiti breed, which a Hamburg merchant was sending as a present to his English partner.

> She was tied by a thin chain to one of the benches on the deck, and she tossed about and squealed plaintively, like a bird.

> Every time I passed by, she stretched out her black, cold little hand to me and glanced up at me with her sad, almost human little eyes. I would take her hand, and she would stop squealing and tossing about.

> There was a dead calm. The sea stretched all around like a motionless tablecloth of a leaden color.

> Ceaselessly and plaintively, no worse than the monkey's squealing, a small bell clinked at the stern.

> Now and then a seal would surface and, turning a sharp somersault, go back under the barely ruffled smooth surface.

> And the captain, a taciturn man with a sunburned, gloomy face, smoked a short pipe and spat angrily into the stilled sea.

> To all my questions he answered with an abrupt grumble; willy-nilly I had to turn to my only fellow traveler—the monkey.

> I would sit down beside her; she would stop squealing and again stretch out her hands to me.

> The motionless fog drenched us both with a sleep-inducing dampness; and, sunk in the same unconscious musing, we stayed beside each other like kin.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 162 --> I smile now... but then there was another feeling in me.

> We are all children of one mother, and it was pleasant to me that the poor little beast so trustingly grew quiet and leaned against me, as if against its own kin.

> *Iv. Turgenev.*
