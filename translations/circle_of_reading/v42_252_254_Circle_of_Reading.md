<!-- vol. 42, p. 252 -->

14 November

More important than all others is that knowledge which guides the activity of life.

1

> It is very important to know the laws of life; but the knowledge that leads us to self-perfection is knowledge of the first importance.

> *Spencer.*

2

It is harmful to eat when you are not hungry and to call forth hunger in yourself by artificial means. Still more harmful is to give yourself up to sexual lust without an irresistible attraction and to call it forth in yourself. But most harmful <!-- vol. 42, p. 253 --> of all is to force yourself to think when there is no need for it, and artificially to call forth mental activity in yourself, as people do who use their mental faculties to improve their social position.

3

> It is better to possess with humility a small share of common sense than with self-satisfaction the greatest treasures of the sciences. There is nothing bad in learning, and every knowledge of anything whatever is pleasant in itself, but a good conscience and a virtuous life must always be put ahead of knowledge.

> *Thomas à Kempis.*

4

> The development of science does not contribute to the purification of morals. Among all the peoples whose life we know, the development of the sciences has contributed to the corruption of the peoples. That we now think the contrary comes from our confusing our empty and deceptive knowledge with true, higher knowledge. *Science in its abstract sense, science in general, cannot but be respected, but present-day science, what madmen call science, deserves only mockery and contempt.*

> *Rousseau.*

5

> From a teacher we expect that he will make of his hearer first of all a man of understanding, then a man of reason, and only at the end a learned man.

> Such a method has the advantage that even if the pupil never reaches the last stage, as usually happens in reality, he will still gain from the instruction and become better prepared, if not for school, then for life.

> But if this method is turned inside out, then the pupil snatches at something like reason before understanding has been worked out in him, and carries away from his instruction a borrowed science that is only, as it were, glued on, but has not grown together with him, while his spiritual capacities remain as barren as <!-- vol. 42, p. 254 --> before, but at the same time are already badly spoiled by imaginary learning. This is the reason why we so often meet learned men (or rather, people who have been taught) devoid of both understanding and reason, and why more absurd heads go out into life from the academies than from any other social class.

> *Kant.*

6

> *Only he is truly learned who acts well.*

> *Indian Hitopadesha.*

7

> The will will not be just until the habits of the mind are corrected, since they influence the will most of all. The habits of the mind, however, will become the best only when they are based on the eternal laws of life as a whole.

> *Seneca.*

8

> Attend to the words of a wise man with attention, even if his deeds do not correspond to his teaching. A man must take instruction, even if the instruction is written on a wall.

> *Saadi.*

In knowledge what matters is not quantity but the right valuation of it. It is important to know which knowledge is the most important, which is of second importance, of third, and so on, and which is of the very last.
