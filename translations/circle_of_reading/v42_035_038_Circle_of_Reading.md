<!-- vol. 42, p. 35 -->

10 September

The directions of conscience are unerring when they demand of us not the affirmation of our animal personality but the sacrifice of it.

1

> A Christian who does not know where the spirit that animates him comes from and where it goes (John 3:8), that spirit which God gives not by measure <!-- vol. 42, p. 36 --> (John 3:34), cannot set himself an external goal of life.

> The concept of a goal is borrowed from the usage of earthly affairs and undertakings. But the goal of the whole universe is inaccessible to man, and therefore in his life he is compelled to be guided not by an external goal but by the directions of the will of God, known within himself.

> Just as a sailor, in order to choose the right direction for the course of his vessel, can be guided by the sight of the shore only when it is within his view—for example, when crossing a river—but when crossing the ocean must turn to the directions of the compass, so a Christian, in order to choose the right path of life, can be guided by external goals only in everyday affairs; but in the matter of seeking the general meaning of life he must turn to the direction of the inner voice of *conscience*, which always clearly and distinctly warns a man in cases of a departure from the path of truth that he is committing or even only intending.

> *Fyodor Strakhov.*

2

> The satisfaction which we notice following every disinterested act depends on this: that such an act, proceeding from the immediate recognition of our own being in an alien phenomenon, in its turn confirms that we were right in recognizing that our true “I” exists not only in our personality, not only in this separate phenomenon, but in everything living. And as self-love constricts the heart, so this consciousness gives it room. Indeed, just as self-love concentrates all our interest on our separate personality, while knowledge constantly paints for us the countless dangers that unceasingly threaten this personality, so that anxiety and care become the keynote of our mood, so the knowledge that everything living, to the same degree as our own personality, is our own being, of itself extends our interest to everything living; and this gives room to the heart. And as a result of such a lessening of interest in ourselves, anxious care is cut down at the root and limited; hence—the calm, confident joy that a virtuous disposition of spirit and a clear <!-- vol. 42, p. 37 --> conscience give, hence the livelier feeling of joy at every good deed, which makes the ground of this mood clear to ourselves. The egoist feels himself alone among alien and hostile phenomena, and all his hopes are in his own well-being. But the good man lives in a world of friendly beings: the good of each of them is his own good.

> *Schopenhauer.*

3

> How many partitions there are between us and objects! The state of mind, of health, all the tissues of the eye, the panes of our room, fog, smoke, rain or dust, and even light—and all of this endlessly changing. Heraclitus said: “One cannot bathe twice in the same river”; I would say: one cannot see the same landscape twice, because both the one who observes and that which is observed are always endlessly changing.

> Wisdom consists in *submitting to the universal illusion, without being deceived by it.*

> I think that *reason inevitably leads us to the consciousness that everything material is only a dream within a dream.* Only the sense of duty, moral demands, lead us out of the sphere of magical dreams. Only *conscience* tears us away from the enchantment of Maya; it disperses the fumes of kef, the hallucinations of opium, and the calm of contemplative indifference. It, conscience, pushes us into the consciousness of human responsibility.

> It is the alarm clock, it is the cock's crow which scatters the phantoms, it is the archangel armed with a sword who drives man out of his artificial paradise.

> *After Amiel.*

4

> A man who lives for the body may lose his way in the tangled labyrinths of contemplative or sensual life, but the soul always knows the truth unerringly.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

5

> The passions may be stronger than conscience, their voice may be louder, but their cry is entirely different from the one in which <!-- vol. 42, p. 38 --> conscience speaks. They do not have the power which the voice of conscience has. In their triumph they nevertheless quail before this quiet, deep, and menacing voice.

> *Channing.*

*The voice of conscience can always be distinguished from all other impulses of the soul by this: that it always demands something useless, intangible, but beautiful and attainable by our own effort alone.*

*This is what distinguishes the voice of conscience from the voice of love of glory, which is often confused with it.*
