## PASCAL

No passion keeps people in its power so long, hides from them so firmly, sometimes to the very end, the futility of temporal worldly life, and none so distances people from understanding the meaning of human life and its true good, as the passion for human glory, in whatever form it shows itself: petty vanity, ambition, love of glory.

Every lust carries its punishment within itself, and the sufferings that accompany its gratification expose its worthlessness. Besides this, every lust weakens with the years, while love of glory flares up more and more with the years. But the main thing is that concern for human glory is always joined with the thought of serving people, and it is easy for a person, when he seeks people's approval, to deceive himself that he lives not for himself but for the good of those people whose approval he is trying to win. And therefore this is the most insidious and dangerous passion and the hardest of all to uproot. Only people with great strength of soul free themselves from this passion.

Great strength of soul gives these people the possibility of quickly attaining great glory, and this same strength of soul gives them the possibility of seeing its worthlessness.

Pascal was such a man. Such too was a Russian man close to us, Gogol (through Gogol, I think, I understood Pascal). Both the one and the other, though with quite different qualities and with an entirely different cast and scale of mind, lived through one and the same thing. Both very soon attained the glory they passionately desired; and both, having attained it, at once understood all the futility of what had seemed to them the highest, the most precious good in the world, and both were horrified at the temptation in whose power they had been. They put all the powers of their souls into showing people all the horror of the delusion from which they had just emerged, and the stronger the disappointment was, the more urgent appeared to them the necessity of such a goal, such a purpose of life, as nothing could disturb.

This is the cause of the passionate attitude toward faith of both our Gogol and Pascal; this is also the cause of their disregard <!-- vol. 41, p. 478 --> for everything they had done before. For all of this had been done for glory. And the glory passed, and there had been nothing in it but deception. So all that had been done to acquire it was unnecessary and worthless. One thing matters, only one: that which had not been there, that which had been screened off by worldly desires for glory. One thing was important and necessary: that faith which gives meaning to this passing life and a firm direction to all its activity. And this consciousness of the necessity of faith and of the impossibility of living without it so strikes such people that they cannot help being amazed at how they themselves could, how people in general can, live without a faith that explains to them the meaning of their life and of the death awaiting them.

Pascal was such a man, and in this lies his great, inestimable, and far from appreciated merit.

Pascal was born in Clermont in 1623. His father was a well-known mathematician. The boy, imitating his father as all children do, took up mathematics from his earliest childhood, and extraordinary abilities showed themselves in him. The father, not wishing to develop the child prematurely, did not give him books on mathematics; but the boy, listening to his father's conversations with his mathematician acquaintances, began to invent geometry anew by himself. The father, on seeing these works, extraordinary for a child, was so struck by this that he was filled with delight, burst into tears of tenderness, and from then on began to teach his son mathematics himself. The boy soon not only mastered all that his father revealed to him but began to make mathematical discoveries himself. His successes drew the attention not only of those close to him but also of learned men, and Pascal, while very young, acquired the renown of a remarkable mathematician. The glory of a scholar outstanding despite his young years encouraged him in his studies, his great abilities gave him the possibility of increasing his glory, and Pascal devoted all his time and strength to scientific studies and research. But his health had been weak since childhood, and the intensified studies weakened it still more, and as a youth he fell gravely ill. After the illness, at his father's request, he cut his studies down to two hours a day, and he used his free time for reading philosophical works.

He read Epictetus, Descartes, and Montaigne's essays. Montaigne's book especially struck him—it outraged him by its skepticism and indifference to religion. Pascal had always been religious <!-- vol. 41, p. 479 --> and believed like a child in the Catholic teaching in which he had been brought up. Montaigne's book, having aroused doubts in him, made him ponder questions of faith, and especially how necessary faith is for the reasonable life of man, and he became still stricter with himself in fulfilling his religious duties and, besides philosophical works, began to read books of religious content. Among these books he came upon a book by the Dutch theologian Jansen, “The Transformation of the Inner Man.”

In this book there was an argument that, besides the lust of the flesh, there is also a lust of the spirit, consisting in the gratification of human inquisitiveness, at the base of which lies the same thing as in every lust: egoism and self-love, and that such a refined lust removes a person from God more than anything else. This book struck Pascal powerfully. With the truthfulness proper to great souls, he felt the truth of this argument in relation to himself and, despite the fact that to give up the pursuit of science and the glory connected with it was a great deprivation for him, or precisely because it was a great deprivation for him, he decided to abandon the pursuit of science that tempted him and directed all his powers to clarifying, for himself and for others, those questions of faith that occupied him more and more strongly.

Nothing is known about Pascal's relations with women and what influence the temptations of a woman's love had on his life. His biographers, on the basis of his small work “Discours sur les passions de l'amour,” [^1] in which he says that the greatest happiness accessible to man—love—is a pure, spiritual feeling and should serve as the source of everything lofty and noble, conclude that Pascal in his youth was in love with a woman who stood above him in position and did not return his love. In any case, if there was such a love, it had no consequences for Pascal's life. The main interests of his young life lay in the struggle between his strivings toward the pursuit of science and toward the glory it gave him, and the consciousness of the emptiness, the worthlessness of these pursuits and of the harmfulness of the temptation of love of glory, and the desire to devote all his powers solely to the service of God.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 480 --> Thus, already in that period of his life when he had decided to give up the pursuit of science, he happened to read Torricelli's researches on the void. Feeling that this question was being decided incorrectly and that a more precise determination was possible, Pascal could not restrain the desire to verify these experiments. And in verifying them he made his famous discovery of the weight of air. This discovery turned the attention of the whole learned world to him. Learned men wrote to him, visited him, and praised him. And the struggle with the temptation of human glory became still harder.

For this struggle Pascal wore on his body a belt with nails turned toward the body, and every time it seemed to him that, while reading or listening to praises of himself, a feeling of ambition, of pride, was rising in him, he pressed the belt to his side with his elbow, the nails pricked his body, and he recalled that whole train of thoughts and feelings that drew him away from the temptation of glory.

In 1651 an event happened to Pascal that would seem unimportant, but that struck him strongly and had a great influence on the state of his soul. On the bridge at Neuilly he fell from a carriage and was within a hair of death. At this same time Pascal's father died. This double reminder of death made Pascal go still deeper than before into questions of life and death.

The religious mood took hold of Pascal's life more and more, so that in 1655 he withdrew from the world completely. He moved to the Jansenist community of Port-Royal and began to live there an almost monastic life, thinking over and preparing that large work in which he wanted to show, first, the necessity of religion for the reasonable life of people, and, second, the truth of the religion that he himself professed. But even here the temptations of human glory did not leave Pascal.

The Jansenist community of Port-Royal, in which Pascal lived, provoked the hostility of the powerful order of the Jesuits, and the intrigues of the Jesuits brought it about that the boys' and girls' schools that existed at Port-Royal were closed, and the monastery of Port-Royal itself was threatened with the danger of being closed.

Living among the Jansenists and sharing their teaching, Pascal could not remain indifferent to the position of his fellow believers and, carried away by their dispute with the Jesuits, wrote in defense <!-- vol. 41, p. 481 --> of the Jansenists a book that he called “Letters of a Provincial.” In this work Pascal did not so much justify and defend the teaching of the Jansenists as condemn their enemies—the Jesuits—exposing the immorality of their teaching. The book had great success, but this glory could no longer tempt Pascal.

His whole life was by now an unceasing service to God.

He set himself rules of life and strictly followed them, not departing from them either out of laziness or because of illness. He considered poverty the foundation of virtue. “In poverty and destitution,” he said, “not only is there no evil, but in them is our good. Christ was poor and destitute and had nowhere to lay his head.” Giving everything he could to the poor, Pascal lived so that he had only what was necessary; he did without servants as far as possible, allowing them only when because of illness he could not move. His dwelling was of the simplest, as were his food and clothing. He tidied his rooms himself and brought himself his dinner. His illness kept growing worse, and he did not cease to suffer. But he bore his sufferings not only with a patience that amazed those close to him, but even with joy and gratitude. “Do not be sorry,” he said to those who sympathized with his condition, “illness is the natural state of a Christian, because in this condition the Christian is what he should always be. It accustoms one to the privation of all goods and sensual pleasures, accustoms one to refrain from the passions that assail a person all his life, to be without ambition, without greed, to be always in expectation of death.”

The luxury with which his loving relatives tried to surround him weighed on him. He asked his sister to move him to the hospital for the incurable poor, so as to spend the last days of his life with them, but his sister did not fulfill his wish, and he died at home.

In his last hours he was unconscious. Only just before the very end did he raise himself up from the bed and, with a clear and joyful expression, say: “Do not leave me, Lord.” These were his last words.

He passed away on 19 August 1662.

A person needs two faiths for his good: one—to believe that there *is* an explanation of the meaning of life, and the other—to *find* this best explanation of life.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 482 --> Pascal did the first thing as no one else did. Fate, God, did not let him do the second.

As a man dying of thirst throws himself on the water that is before him, without examining its quality, so Pascal, without examining the quality of the Catholicism in which he had been brought up, saw in it truth and the salvation of people. It is enough that it is water, enough that it is faith.

It goes without saying that no one has the right to guess at what might have been, but it is impossible to imagine Pascal, a man of genius and truthful with himself, believing in Catholicism. He did not have time to subject it to that force of thought which he directed at proving the necessity of faith, and therefore in his soul dogmatic Catholicism remained whole. Without touching it, he relied on it. He relied on what was and is true in it. He took from it the intense work of self-perfection, the struggle with temptations, the aversion to wealth, and firm faith in a merciful God, to whom, dying, he gave up his soul.

He died having done only one part of the work—without finishing, without even beginning to do the other. But because this second part of the work has not been done, the first is no less precious: the remarkable book of “Thoughts,” gathered from scattered scraps of paper on which the sick, dying Pascal wrote down his thoughts.

The fate of this book is remarkable.

A prophetic book appears—the crowd stands in perplexity, struck by the power of the prophetic word, is alarmed, wants to understand and make it clear, to find out what to do. And then comes one of those people who, as Pascal said, think that they know and therefore trouble the world; these people come and say: “There is nothing here to understand and make clear; all this is very simple. This Pascal (the same thing happened with Gogol), as you see, believed in the Trinity, in communion; clearly he was sick, abnormal, and therefore, through his weakness and sickness, understood everything inside out. The best proof of this is that he rejected, renounced even the good that he himself had done and that we like (because we understand it), and attributed great importance to completely useless ‘mystical’ reasonings about the destiny of man, about the future life. Therefore one must take from him not what he himself considered important, but what we can understand and what we like.”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 483 --> And the crowd is glad: before, it did not understand, it needed an effort to rise to the height to which Pascal wanted to raise it, but here everything is perfectly simple. Pascal discovered the law by which pumps are made. Pumps are very useful, and that is very good; but all that he says there about God, immortality, all that is trifles, because he believed in the Trinity, the Bible. We need no effort to rise to him; on the contrary, from the height of our normality we can patronizingly and condescendingly acknowledge his merits, despite his abnormality.

Pascal shows people that people without religion are either animals or madmen, rubs their noses in their ugliness and madness, shows them that no science can replace religion. But Pascal believed in God, in the Trinity, in the Bible, and therefore for them it is a settled matter that what he told them about the madness of their life and the futility of science is also untrue. That very science, that very vanity of life, that very madness which he made so irresistibly clear—this very vanity, this very science, this very madness they consider real life, truth, and Pascal's reasonings they consider the fruit of his morbid abnormality. They cannot help acknowledging the power of this man's thought and word, and they count him among the classics, but the content of his book is unnecessary to them. It seems to them that they stand immeasurably higher than that highest state of soul, of religious consciousness, which a person can possibly reach and on which Pascal stands, and therefore the significance of the remarkable book is hopelessly hidden from them.

Yes, nothing is so pernicious, so ruinous for the true progress of humanity, as these reasonings, deftly fitted out with every kind of modern adornment, of people qui croyent savoir (who think they know) and who, in Pascal's opinion, bouleversent le monde (trouble the world).

But the light shines in the darkness too, and there are people who, not sharing Pascal's faith in Catholicism, but understanding that he, despite his great mind, could believe in Catholicism (preferring to believe in it rather than believe in nothing), also understand the whole significance of his remarkable book, which irresistibly proves to people the necessity of faith, the impossibility of human life without faith, that is, without a definite, firm relation of man to the world and its Source.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 484 --> And having understood this, people cannot help finding also those answers of faith, corresponding to the degree of their moral and intellectual development, to the questions posed by Pascal.

In this lies his great merit.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
