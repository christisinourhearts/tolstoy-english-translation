<!-- vol. 42, p. 34 -->

9 September

That knowledge which in our time is considered science hinders more than it furthers the good of human life.

1

Astronomy, mechanics, physics, chemistry, and all the other sciences, together and each separately, work out each the side of life that falls to it, without arriving at any results about life in general. Only in the times of their wildness, that is, of their unclarity and indefiniteness, did some of these sciences attempt from their own point of view to embrace all the phenomena of life, and they got muddled, themselves inventing new concepts and words. So it was with astronomy when it was astrology, with chemistry when it was alchemy. The same is happening now with that experimental evolutionary science which, examining one side or several sides of life, lays claim to the study of the whole of life.

2

> Science fulfills its task not by explaining the causes of the appearance of spots on the sun, but by making clear the laws of our own life and the consequences of violating them.

> *John Ruskin.*

3

> In relation to nature, experience puts rules into our hands and is the source of truth; but in relation to moral laws, experience proves, unfortunately, to be the mother of error. Therefore it would be in the highest degree unworthy to wish to derive the laws about what I ought to do from, or to limit them by, what takes place in nature or has taken place in history.

> *Kant.*

4

Knowledge humbles the great man, astonishes the ordinary one, and puffs up the small one.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 35 -->

5

> The sciences are food for the mind. And this food can be just as harmful to the mind as bodily food is to the body, if one takes it beyond measure. So that one can overeat on mental food too and fall ill from it. In order that this not happen, one must, just as with bodily food, take it only when it is truly needed.

> *After Ruskin.*

6

In order for knowledge to be important, it must serve the good—the union of people. People are united with one another by recognizing a truth that is one for all. The expressions of this truth must be clear and understandable. But in present-day science the expressions are unclear and incomprehensible.

7

Socrates said that for people who desire nothing more than to become good, every science is easy, because in every science they desire to know only what all people need.

8

> *The wisdom of Socrates consisted in this, that he did not think he knew what he did not know.*

> *Cicero.*

However great knowledge may be, it cannot help toward the fulfillment of the chief aim of life—moral perfecting.
