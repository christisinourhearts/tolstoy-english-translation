<!-- vol. 42, p. 20 -->

7 September

If life is a good, then death, which constitutes a necessary condition of life, is also a good.

1

> Death is liberation from the one-sidedness of personality. It is on this, apparently, that the expression of peace and calm on the faces of most of the dead depends. The death of every good person is usually calm and easy; but to die with readiness, willingly, to die joyfully—this is the privilege <!-- vol. 42, p. 21 --> of one who has renounced himself, of the one who refuses the will to live, denies it. For only such a person wishes to die really, and not seemingly, and consequently neither needs nor demands the further existence of his personality.

> *Schopenhauer.*

2

> *Where are the dead? In the same place as the unborn.*

> *Seneca.*

3

If death is terrible, the cause of this is not in it but in us. The better a person is, the less he fears death.

For a saint there is no death.

4

Fleshly death destroys what unites the body—destroys the consciousness of temporal life. But this happens to us incessantly and every day when we fall asleep. The question is whether fleshly death destroys what unites all the successive consciousnesses into one, that is, my particular relation to the world? And in order to assert this, one must first prove that this very particular relation to the world, which unites all the successive consciousnesses into one, was born with my fleshly existence and therefore will also die with it. And this is just what is not so.

Reasoning on the basis of my consciousness, I see that what united all my consciousnesses into one—a certain receptivity to one thing and coldness to another, as a result of which one thing remains in me and another disappears—the degree of my love of good and hatred of evil—that this particular relation of mine to the world, which constitutes precisely me, the particular me, is not the product of some external cause but is the fundamental cause of all the other phenomena of my life.

Reasoning on the basis of observation, it seems to me that the causes of the particularity of my *I* are found in the particularities of my parents and of the conditions that influenced me and them; but, reasoning further along this path, I cannot fail to see that if my particular *I* lies in the particularity of my parents and of the conditions <!-- vol. 42, p. 22 --> that influenced them, then it lies also in the particularity of all my ancestors and in the conditions of their existence—to infinity, that is, outside time and outside space—so that my particular *I* arose outside space and outside time, that is, the very thing that I am conscious of.

5

> Before I reached old age, I tried to live well; in old age I try to die well. And to die well means to die willingly.

> *Seneca.*

6

People who do not understand life cannot help fearing death.

You fear death, but think what would be if you were condemned, in your personality that is always one and the same, to eternal life?
