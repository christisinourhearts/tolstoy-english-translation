## II SISTERS

## I

On May 3, 1882, the three-masted ship “Our Lady of the Winds” sailed from Le Havre for the China seas. It delivered its cargo in China, took on a new cargo there, carried it to Buenos Aires, and from there carried goods to Brazil.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 299 --> Passages, damage, repairs, calms of several months at a time, winds that drove the ship far off its course, sea adventures and misfortunes held it back so that it sailed for four years in foreign seas, and only on May 8, 1886, did it put in at Marseille with a cargo of tin boxes of American canned goods.

When the ship left Le Havre, there were on board the captain, his mate, and 14 sailors. During the voyage one sailor died, four were lost in various adventures, and only nine came back to France. In place of the sailors who had dropped out, the ship took on two Americans, a Negro, and a Swede, whom they found in a tavern in Singapore.

On the ship they took in the sails and tied the rigging crosswise on the mast. A tugboat came up and, puffing, towed it to the line of ships. The sea was calm; by the shore what was left of the swell barely lapped. The ship entered the line where, side by side along the quay, stood ships from all the countries of the world, large and small, of every size, shape, and rig. “Our Lady of the Winds” took up a place between an Italian brig and an English galliot, which squeezed over to give room to the new companion.

As soon as the captain had finished with the customs and port officials, he let half the sailors go ashore for the whole night.

The night was warm, a summer night. Marseille was all lit up; the streets smelled of food from the kitchens; from all sides came the sound of talk, the rumble of wheels, and merry shouts.

The sailors from the ship “Our Lady of the Winds” had not been on land for some four months, and now, having come ashore, they walked through the city timidly, two by two, like strangers, people who had lost the habit of cities. They looked about, sniffing out the streets nearest the wharf, as if they were looking for something. For four months they had not seen women, and lust tormented them. In front of them walked Célestin Duclos, a strapping and nimble fellow. He always led the others when they went ashore. He knew how to find good places, knew how to get away when he had to, and did not get mixed up in fights, as often happens with sailors when they go ashore; but if a fight started, he did not hang back from his comrades and knew how to stand up for himself.

For a long time the sailors pushed about the dark streets, which all ran down to the sea like gutters and from which came a heavy <!-- vol. 42, p. 300 --> smell of cellars and closets. At last Célestin chose a narrow lane in which convex lanterns burned over the doors, and went into it. The sailors, joking and humming, followed him. On the frosted painted glass of the lanterns were huge numbers. Under the low ceilings of the doorways, women in aprons sat on straw chairs; they jumped up at the sight of the sailors and, running out into the middle of the street, barred their way and each lured them into her own den.

Now and then, deep in a hallway, a door would suddenly fly open. Out of it would appear a half-dressed girl in coarse, tight cotton drawers, a short little skirt, and a black velvet bodice with gilt braid. “Hey, handsome fellows, come in!” she would call from far off, and sometimes she would run out herself, catch hold of one of the sailors, and drag him with all her strength toward the door. She fastened onto him like a spider when it drags a fly stronger than itself. The fellow, gone soft with lust, resisted feebly, and the others stopped and watched to see what would happen; but Célestin Duclos would shout: “Not here, don't go in. Farther on!” And the fellow obeyed his voice and tore himself away from the girl by force. And the sailors went on, followed by the abuse of the angry girl. At the noise, others jumped out all along the lane, threw themselves at them, and in hoarse voices praised their wares. So they went on, farther and farther. Now and then they met soldiers clinking their spurs, or a lone townsman or shop clerk making his way to a familiar place. In other lanes the same lanterns shone, but the sailors went on farther and farther, stepping through the stinking ooze that seeped from under the houses full of women's bodies. But now Duclos stopped by one house, better than the others, and led his lads in there.

## II

The sailors were sitting in the large hall of the tavern. Each of them had chosen a companion for himself and did not part from her the whole evening; such was the custom in the tavern. Three tables had been pushed together, and the sailors first of all drank together with the girls, then they got up and went upstairs with them. For a long time the thick shoes of twenty feet clattered loudly on the wooden steps, until they had all tumbled through the narrow doors and scattered into the <!-- vol. 42, p. 301 --> bedrooms. From the bedrooms they would come down again to drink, then go up again.

The carousing was in full swing. The whole half-year's pay went in four hours of revelry. By 11 o'clock they were all drunk already and, with bloodshot eyes, were shouting incoherently they knew not what. On each one's knees sat a girl. Some sang, some shouted, some pounded the table with their fists, some poured wine down their throats. Célestin Duclos sat among his comrades. Astride his knee sat a big, fat, red-cheeked girl. He had drunk no less than the others, but was not yet quite drunk; some thoughts or other were wandering in his head. He had grown tender and was looking for something to talk about with his companion. But thoughts came to him and went away at once, and he could not catch them, remember them, and say them.

He laughed and said:

— So, so that's how it is... that's how... And have you been here long?

— Six months, — the girl answered.

He nodded his head, as if he approved of her for it.

— Well, now, and is it good for you here?

She thought a little.

— I've got used to it, — she said. — One has to live somehow. It's better anyway than being a servant or a washerwoman.

He nodded his head approvingly, as if he approved of her for this too.

— And you're not from here?

She shook her head to show that she was not from here.

— From far away?

She nodded.

— Where from, then?

She thought a little, as if she were trying to remember.

— I'm from Perpignan, — she said.

— So, so, — he said, and fell silent.

— And you, then, are you a sailor? — she asked now in her turn.

— Yes, we're sailors.

— Well, have you been far?

— Yes, not close. We've seen everything there is.

— Maybe you've even gone around the world?

— Not just once, we've gone around nearly twice.

She seemed to be pondering, recalling something.

— I suppose you've met a lot of ships? — she said.

— How could we not?

<!-- vol. 42, p. 302 --> — Didn't you come across “Our Lady of the Winds”? There is such a ship.

He was surprised that she had named his ship, and took it into his head to make a joke.

— Of course, we met it last week.

— Is it true, really? — she asked, and turned pale.

— It's true.

— You're not lying?

— By God, — he swore.

— Well, and didn't you see Célestin Duclos there? — she asked.

— Célestin Duclos? — he repeated, and was surprised, and even frightened. How could she know his name?

— Why, do you know him? — he asked. It could be seen that she too was frightened of something.

— No, not me, but there's a woman here who knows him.

— What woman? From this house?

— No, near here.

— Where near here?

— Oh, not far.

— Who is she, then?

— Just a woman, the same as me.

— And what does she want with him?

— How should I know? Maybe she's from his parts.

They looked searchingly straight into each other's eyes.

— I'd like to see this woman, — he said.

— What for? You want to tell her something?

— To tell her...

— Tell her what?

— To tell her that I've seen Célestin Duclos.

— Oh, you've seen Célestin Duclos? And he's alive and well?

— He's well. Why?

She fell silent, again collecting her thoughts, and then said quietly:

— And where is “Our Lady of the Winds” going?

— Where? To Marseille.

— Is it true?! — she cried out.

— It's true.

— And you know Duclos?

— Why, I told you I know him.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 303 --> She thought a little.

— So, so. That's good, — she said softly.

— But what do you want with him?

— And if you see him, you tell him... No, never mind.

— Well, what?

— No, nothing.

He looked at her and grew more and more uneasy.

— And do you yourself know him? — he asked.

— No, I don't.

— Then what do you want with him?

Without answering, she suddenly jumped up, ran to the counter where the proprietress sat, took a lemon, cut it, squeezed the juice into a glass, then poured water into it and handed it to Célestin.

— Here, drink this, — she said, and sat down on his knees as she had sat before.

— What's this for? — he asked, taking the glass from her.

— So the drink will wear off. I'll tell you afterward. Drink.

He drank it and wiped his lips with his sleeve.

— Well, tell me, I'm listening.

— But you won't tell him that you saw me? You won't tell who you heard it from, what I'm going to tell you?

— All right, I won't.

— Swear to God!

He swore.

— By God?

— By God.

— Then tell him that his father died, and his mother died, his brother died too. It was a fever. All three died in one month.

Duclos felt all his blood crowd to his heart. For several minutes he sat in silence, not knowing what to say; then he brought out:

— And you know it for certain?

— For certain.

— Who told you, then?

She put her hands on his shoulders and looked him straight in the eyes.

— Swear you won't blab.

— There, I've sworn: by God.

— I am his sister.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 304 --> — Françoise! — he cried out.

She looked at him intently and very, very quietly moved her lips, hardly letting out the words.

— So it's you—Célestin!

They did not move; they froze as they were, looking into each other's eyes.

And around them the others were bawling in drunken voices. The clinking of glasses, the pounding of palms and heels, and the shrill squealing of women mingled with the din of songs.

— How can this be? — he said quietly, so quietly that even she could barely make out his words.

Her eyes suddenly filled with tears.

— Just like that, they died. All three in one month, — she went on. — What was I to do? I was left alone. The pharmacy, and the doctor, and the funerals for three... I sold what things there were, paid what I owed, and was left with what I had on. I went into service as a maid with the gentleman Kasho—you remember, the lame one. I had only just turned fifteen; why, I wasn't even fourteen yet when you went away. I sinned with him... We sisters are fools, you know. Then I went as a nursemaid to a notary. He too. At first he kept me; I lived in lodgings. But not for long. He threw me over, I lived three days without eating, nobody would take me on, and so I came in here, like the rest.

She spoke, and tears ran in streams from her eyes, from her nose, wet her cheeks, and flowed into her mouth.

— What is this we've done? — he said.

— I thought you had died too, — she said through her tears. — Is it my doing? — she whispered.

— How did you not recognize me? — he said, also in a whisper.

— I don't know, it's not my fault, — she went on, and wept harder still.

— How could I recognize you? Were you like this when I left? But how did you not recognize me?

She waved her hand in despair.

— Ah! I see so many of them, these men, that to me they all have the same face.

His heart was wrung so painfully and so hard that he wanted to shout and howl like a little boy when he is being beaten.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 305 --> He got up, pushed her away from him, and, seizing her head in his big sailor's paws, began to peer intently into her face.

Little by little he recognized in her at last that small, thin, merry little girl whom he had left at home with those whose eyes she had closed.

— Yes, you're Françoise! sister! — he said.

And suddenly sobs, the heavy sobs of a man, like a drunkard's hiccups, rose in his throat. He let go of her head, struck the table so that the glasses overturned and flew into pieces, and cried out in a wild voice.

His comrades turned to him and stared at him.

— Look how he's tanked up! — said one. — Enough of your bawling, — said another. — Hey! Duclos! What are you bawling for? Let's go upstairs again, — said a third, tugging Célestin by the sleeve with one hand and with the other embracing his companion, laughing, flushed, with shining black eyes, in a pink silk low-cut bodice.

Duclos suddenly fell silent and, holding his breath, stared at his comrades. Then, with that strange and resolute expression with which he used to go into a fight, he went staggering up to the sailor who was embracing the girl and struck with his hand between him and the girl, separating them.

— Get away! Don't you see, she's your sister! They're all somebody's sisters. Here, this one is sister Françoise. Ha-ha-ha-ha... — he burst into sobs that were like laughter, and he staggered, raised his arms, and crashed face down on the floor. And he began to roll about on the floor, beating against it with his arms and legs, wheezing like a dying man.

— We must put him to bed, — said one of his comrades, — or else they might lock him up out in the street.

And they lifted Célestin and dragged him upstairs into Françoise's room and laid him on her bed.

*L. N. Tolstoy (after Maupassant).*
