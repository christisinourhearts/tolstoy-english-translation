<!-- vol. 42, p. 13 -->

3 September

God is incomprehensible to the human mind. We know only that he is, and, whether we want this or not, we know it without doubt.

1

Formerly I saw the phenomena of life without thinking of where these phenomena come from and why I see them.

But when I began to think about the fundamental cause of everything, I came to the conviction that the source of everything is the light of understanding, and I was so carried away by this thought that I reduced everything to one and was entirely satisfied with recognizing understanding alone as the source of all.

But afterward I saw that understanding is a light that reaches me through some kind of frosted glass. The light I see, but what gives this light I do not know, though I do know that it is.

And that which is the source of the light that illuminates me, which I do not know but of whose existence I know without doubt, is God.

2

> Do not try to penetrate into the essence of the divine nature; it is impious to wish to know what has not been revealed by God.

> *Menander.*

3

> Believe in God, serve him, but do not try to know his essence; you will get nothing from your vain effort except disappointment and weariness.

> Do not even concern yourself with learning whether he exists or not; serve him as though he exists and is present everywhere. Nothing more is needed.

> *Philemon.*

4

> No one has yet penetrated the mystery of the great source. No one has taken a step outside himself. O you, in search of whom the whole world is in turmoil! The saint as well as the vicious man, the beggar <!-- vol. 42, p. 14 --> as well as the rich man, are all equally far from the possibility of reaching you: your name sounds together with the existence of all, but all are deaf; you are before all eyes, but all are blind.

> *The Persian Khayyam of the 11th century.*

5

We come to know the existence of God not so much by reason as by the consciousness of that complete dependence on him in which we feel ourselves to be, something like the feeling that a nursing infant experiences in his mother's arms.

The child does not know who holds him, who warms him, who feeds him, but he knows that this someone exists, and not only does he know it—he loves the one in whose power he is.

6

> Man strove to become like God; the priests made God like man, and human frivolity was content with this idea of God.

> *D'Agoult.*

*Do not be troubled that the concept of God is expressed unclearly for you. The more clearly it is expressed, the further it is from truth and the less reliable it is as a support.*
