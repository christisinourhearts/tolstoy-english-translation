## LOVE ONE ANOTHER

*(An address to a circle of young people)*

I would like, by way of farewell (at my age every meeting with people is a farewell), to tell you briefly how, as I understand it, people should live in order that our life should not be evil and grief, as it now seems to most people, but should be what God wishes and what we all wish, that is, the good and joy, as it should be.

The whole matter is how a person understands his life. If one understands one's life so, that this life is given to me in my body, to Ivan, Pyotr, Marya, and that the whole matter of life is to obtain as many as possible of all sorts of joys, pleasures, and happiness for this “I” of one's own, Ivan, Pyotr, Marya, then life will always and for everyone be unhappy and embittered.

Life will be unhappy and embittered because whatever one person wants for himself, every other person wants the very same. And since each wants as much of every kind of good for himself as possible, and this good is one and the same for all such people, there is never enough of this good for all. And therefore, if people live each for himself, they cannot avoid taking from one another, fighting, being angry at one another, and because of this their life is not happy. And if at times people do obtain for themselves what they want, it is always too little for them, and they try to obtain more and more, and besides that they are also afraid that what they have obtained will be taken from them, and they envy those who have obtained what they do not have.

So that if people understand their life each in his own body, the life of such people cannot but be unhappy.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 325 --> And such it is now for all such people. But life should not be such, that is, unhappy. Life is given to us for the good, and that is how we all understand life. And for life to be so, people must understand that our real life is not at all in our body, but in that spirit which lives in our body, and that our good lies not in pleasing and doing what the body wants, but in doing what is wanted by this spirit, one and the same, living in us just as in all people. And what this spirit wants is the good for itself, for the spirit. And since this spirit is one and the same in all people, it wants the good for all people. And to wish the good for all people means to love people. And no one and nothing can hinder one from loving people; and the more a person loves, the freer and more joyful his life becomes.

So it turns out that a person, however hard he may try, is never able to please the body, because what the body needs cannot always be obtained, and if one is to obtain it, one has to fight with others; but a person can always please the soul, because the soul needs only love, and for love one need not fight with anyone; not only need one not fight with others, but on the contrary, the more you love, the closer you draw to other people. So that nothing can hinder love, and every person, the more he loves, the more and more not only becomes happy and joyful himself, but also makes other people happy and joyful.

So this, dear brothers, is what I wanted to tell you by way of farewell: to tell you what all holy and wise people, and Christ, and all the sages of the world have taught you, namely, that our life is unhappy because of ourselves, that the power which sent us into life and which we call God sent us not in order that we should be tormented, but in order that we should have that very good which we all desire, and that we fail to receive this good intended for us only when we understand life not as we should and do other than what we should.

As it is, we complain of life, that our life is badly arranged, and do not think that it is not our life that is badly arranged, but that what we do is not what is needed. And this is just as if a drunkard began to complain that he had drunk himself into ruin because so many taverns and saloons had sprung up, whereas many taverns and saloons had sprung up only because so many drunkards like him had multiplied.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 326 --> Life is given to people for the good, if only they would use it as it should be used. Only let people live not by envy of one another but by love, and life would be an unceasing good for all.

Now from all sides people say only one thing: our life, they say, is bad and unhappy because it is badly arranged—let us remake the bad arrangement into a good one, and our life will be good.

Dear brothers, do not believe this, do not believe that from this or that arrangement your life can be worse or better. I will not even speak of the fact that all those people who are concerned with the arranging of a better life all disagree among themselves, all argue among themselves: some propose one arrangement, considering it the very best, while others say that this arrangement is the very worst, and that the only good one is the one they propose. And a third group rejects this one too and proposes its own very best, and so on. But even if there were such a very best arrangement, even if one agreed that the very best arrangement had been devised, then how is one to make people live according to this arrangement, how is one to keep this good arrangement when people have grown used to, and love, living badly? As it is, we have now grown used to, and love, living badly; whatever we take up, we foul it all, and we say that we will live well when there is a good arrangement. But how is there to be a good arrangement when people are bad?

So that if there is such a very best arrangement of life, then in order to attain it people must become better. But you are promised a good life after you, in addition to your present bad life, will also fight with people, do violence to people, even kill them, in order to introduce this good arrangement; that is, you are promised a good life after you yourselves have become still worse than you are now.

Do not believe, do not believe this, dear brothers. For life to be good there is only one means: for people themselves to be better. And let people be better, and the life that should be among good people will arrange itself of itself.

Your salvation and that of all people lies not at all in a sinful, violent arrangement of life, but in the arrangement of one's own soul. Only by this, by such an arrangement of the soul, will each person obtain both for himself and for other people the greatest good and the best arrangement <!-- vol. 42, p. 327 --> of life that people can possibly desire. The true good, the good that every human heart seeks, is given to us not in some future arrangement of life maintained by violence, but now, to all of us, everywhere, at every moment of life and even of death, attained by love.

This good has been given to us from all time; but people did not understand it and did not take it. But now the time has come when we can no longer fail to accept it—cannot fail to accept it, first, because the outrages and sufferings of our life have brought us to the point where our life is becoming unbearably tormenting. The second is that the true teaching of Christ, being revealed to us more and more, has now become so clear that we can no longer, for our salvation, fail to acknowledge and accept it. Our salvation now is in one thing: in acknowledging that our true life is not in our body, but in that spirit of God which lives in us, and that therefore all those efforts which we formerly put into improving our bodily life, both individual and social, we can and must put into the one single matter that is needed and important for a person: that each should foster and establish in himself love not only for those who love us, but, as Christ said, for all people, and especially for people who are strangers to us, for those who hate us.

Our life is now so far from this that at first such a transfer of all one's efforts, instead of care for worldly affairs, to one inconspicuous matter unfamiliar to us—to love for all people—seems impossible.

But it only seems so: love for all people, even for those who hate us, is far more natural to the soul of a person than struggle with one's neighbors and hatred of them. A change in the understanding of the meaning of life is not only not impossible in our time, but, on the contrary, what is impossible is the continuation of that embittered life of all against all which we are leading now. This change is not only not impossible, but, on the contrary, it alone can lead people out of the calamities from which they suffer, and therefore this change must inevitably, sooner or later, come about.

Dear brothers, why, what for, do you torment yourselves? Only remember that the greatest good is destined for you, and take it. Everything is in yourselves. It is so easy, so simple, and so joyful.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 328 --> But perhaps people who are suffering, poor, oppressed will say: “Yes, this may be good for the rich and those in power; it is easy for the rich and those in power to love their enemies when these enemies are in their power. But it is hard for us, the suffering and oppressed.” But this is not true. Dear brothers, to change one's understanding of life is equally necessary both for those in power, the rich, and for the subordinate, the poor. And for the subordinate and the poor it is easier than for the rich. The subordinate and the poor need only, without changing their position, not only not do deeds contrary to love, but not take part in these deeds, such as deeds of violence, and all this arrangement hostile to love will fall of itself; whereas for those in power it is much harder to accept and fulfill the teaching of love. For them to fulfill this teaching, they must renounce the temptations of power and wealth that possess them; and this is harder for them; but the poor and the subordinate need only not commit new acts of violence and, above all, not take part in the old.

As a person grows, so humanity grows. The consciousness of love has grown, is growing in it, and has grown in our time to the point that we cannot fail to see that it must save us and become the foundation of our life. For what is going on now are the last convulsions of a dying violent, malicious, loveless life.

Surely it can no longer be unclear now that all these struggles, all this hatred, all these violent arrangements, all of these are senseless deceptions, leading to nothing except ever-increasing calamities. And it cannot but be clear that the only, the simplest and easiest salvation from all this is the consciousness of the fundamental source of the life of all people—love—of this source, which inevitably, without any effort, replaces the greatest evil with the greatest good.

There is a tradition that the apostle John, having reached a deep old age, was wholly absorbed by one feeling and kept expressing it in one and the same words, saying only one thing: “Children, love one another.” Thus old age expressed itself, that is, the life of one person who had lived to a certain limit of life. In just the same way must the life of humanity express itself, having lived to a certain limit.

Surely this is so simple, so clear: you live, that is, you were born, you grow, you mature, you grow old, and any moment now you will die. Can the aim of your life really be in you?—certainly not. What is <!-- vol. 42, p. 329 --> it, then—a person then asks himself—what am I?—And the answer is one: I am something that loves—at first it seems that it loves only itself, but one need only live a little, think a little, to see that one cannot, and there is no reason to, love oneself, passing through life, dying. You feel that I must love, and do love, myself. But, loving myself, I cannot help feeling that the object of my love is unworthy of it; but I cannot not love. In love there is life. What is one to do here? To love others, those near to one, friends, those who love one? At first it seems that this satisfies the need for love, but all these people, in the first place, are imperfect, in the second, change, and above all, die. What, then, is one to love? And the answer is one: to love all, to love the source of love, to love love, to love God. To love not for the sake of the one you love, not for yourself, but for love. One need only understand this, and at once all the evil of human life is destroyed, and its meaning becomes evident and joyful.

“Yes, that would be good. What could be better?” people will say. “It would be good to love and live for love if everyone lived so. But as it is, I will live for love, give everything to another, and the others will live for themselves, for their body; what will become of me, and not of me alone, but of my family, of those whom I love, whom I cannot help loving? Talk of love has been going on for a long time, but no one follows it. And indeed it cannot be followed. One could give one's life to love only if all people at once, by some miracle, exchanged their worldly, bodily life for a spiritual, divine life. But there is no such miracle, and therefore all this is words, not deeds.” So people say, reassuring themselves in their false, habitual life. They say so, but in the depths of their souls they know that they are wrong. They know that these arguments are incorrect. They are incorrect because it is only for the advantage of worldly, bodily life that people all need to change their life at once; but it is not so for the spiritual life: love, love for God and people. Love gives the good to a person not in its consequences, but in love itself; it gives him the good completely independently of how other people act and of what in general is happening in the outer world. Love gives the good in that a person, loving, unites with God, and not only desires nothing for himself, but desires to give everything he has, and his life, to others, and in this giving of himself to God he finds the good. And therefore all that other people do, all that can happen in the world, cannot have <!-- vol. 42, p. 330 --> an influence on his actions. To love means to give oneself to God, to do what God wants; and God is love, that is, he wants the good for all and therefore cannot want a person to perish while fulfilling his law.

A loving person, even alone among those who do not love, does not perish. And if he does perish among people, as Christ perished on the cross, then his death is both joyful for him and significant for others, and not despairing and insignificant, as the deaths of worldly people tend to be.

So that the excuse that I do not give myself up to love because not everyone will do the same, and I will be left alone, is both wrong and not good. It is the same as if a person who needs to work in order to feed himself and his children were not to set to work because others do not work.

Yes, dear brothers, let us put our life into strengthening love in ourselves, and let the world go as it wants, that is, as is determined for it from above. Let us act so, and believe me, we will receive the greatest good for ourselves and will do all the good to people that we can possibly do.

Surely this is so simple, so easy, and so joyful. Only let every person love, love not only those who love him, but all people, especially those who hate him, as Christ taught, and life is an unceasing joy, and all the questions that people who have gone astray so vainly try to resolve by violence are not only resolved, but cease to exist. “And we know that we have passed from death into life if we love the brothers. He who does not love his brother does not have eternal life. Only he who loves his brother has eternal life abiding in him.”

One more word, dear brothers.

About no matter can one learn whether it is good or bad unless one has tested it in practice, in life. If a farmer is told that it is good to sow rye in rows, or a beekeeper that it is good to make frame hives, then a sensible farmer and beekeeper, in order to know for certain whether what he is told is true, will make an experiment and will follow or not follow what was proposed to him, according to how far he finds it confirmed in the experiment.

It is the same in the whole matter of life. In order to know for certain how far the teachings about love are applicable in life, test them.

Try it: take it upon yourselves for a certain period to follow the demands of love in everything: to live so that in all your doings <!-- vol. 42, p. 331 --> you remember above all not to depart from love with any person, with a thief, a drunkard, a rude superior or subordinate, that is, when having dealings with him, to remember what is needed for him, and not for yourself. And, having lived so for the appointed period, ask yourselves: was it hard for you, and did you spoil your life or improve it; and, according to what the experiment gives you, then decide whether it is true that the fulfillment of love gives the good in life, or whether these are only mere words. Test this: try, instead of repaying the offender evil for evil, instead of condemning behind his back a person who lives badly, and the like—instead of this try to answer evil with good, to say nothing bad about a person, not to treat roughly even a beast, a dog, but with kindness and affection; live so for a day, two, or more (as an experiment), and compare your state of soul during this time with what it used to be before. Test this, and you will see how, instead of a gloomy, angry, and heavy state, you will be bright, cheerful, joyful. And live so a second and a third week, and you will see how the joy of your soul will keep growing and growing, and your affairs not only will not go wrong, but will go better and better.

Only test this, dear brothers, and you will see that the teaching of love is not words but a deed, the very, very nearest deed, understood by all and needed by all.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
