## WHAT FOR?

## I

In the spring of 1830, the only son of his late friend, young Józef Migurski, came to Pan Jaczewski at his ancestral estate, Rożanka. Jaczewski was a 65-year-old, broad-browed, broad-shouldered, broad-chested old man with long white mustaches on a brick-red face, a patriot from the time of the second partition of Poland. As a youth he had served together with the elder Migurski under the banners of Kościuszko, and with all the strength of his patriotic soul he hated the apocalyptic harlot, as he called her, Catherine II, and the traitor, her vile lover Poniatowski, and he believed in the restoration of the Rzeczpospolita just as he believed at night that by morning the sun would rise again. In the year '12 he had commanded a regiment in the army of Napoleon, whom he adored. Napoleon's ruin grieved him, but he did not despair of the restoration of a Kingdom of Poland, crippled, it is true, but a kingdom all the same. The opening of the Sejm in Warsaw by Alexander I <!-- vol. 42, p. 85 --> revived his hopes, but the Holy Alliance, the reaction throughout Europe, the despotic caprice of Constantine put off the fulfillment of his cherished wish. From '25 on, Jaczewski had settled in the country and lived in his Rożanka without ever leaving it, occupying his time with the estate, with hunting, and with reading newspapers and letters, by means of which he still ardently followed the political events in his fatherland. He was married, for the second time, to a poor, beautiful woman of the szlachta, and this marriage was unhappy. He did not love or respect this second wife of his, found her a burden, treated her badly, rudely, as though taking out on her his mistake of a second marriage. There were no children by the second wife. By the first wife, however, there were two daughters: the elder, Wanda, a stately beauty who knew the worth of her beauty and was bored in the country, and the younger, Albina, her father's favorite, a lively, bony girl with curly fair hair and big, shining blue eyes set wide apart, like her father's.

Albina was 15 when Józef Migurski came. Migurski had visited the Jaczewskis before, as a student, in Vilna, where they lived in the winters, and had courted Wanda; but now for the first time he came to them in the country as a fully grown, free man. The arrival of young Migurski was pleasant to all the inhabitants of Rożanka. To the old man, Józio Migurski was pleasant because he reminded him of his friend, his father, at the time when they had both been young, and also because he talked with ardor and the rosiest hopes about the revolutionary ferment not only in Poland but also abroad, from where he had just come. To Pani Jaczewska Migurski was pleasant because in the presence of guests old Jaczewski restrained himself and did not scold her for everything, as he usually did. To Wanda he was pleasant because she was sure that Migurski had come for her and intended to propose to her; she was preparing to give him her consent, but intended, as she said to herself: lui tenir la dragée haute. [^1] Albina was glad because everyone was glad. Wanda was not the only one sure that Migurski had come with the intention of proposing to her. Everyone in the house thought so—from old Jaczewski to Nanny Ludwika—though no one said it.

And this was true. Migurski had come with this intention, but, having stayed a week, he, troubled and upset by something, <!-- vol. 42, p. 86 --> left without having proposed. Everyone was surprised by this unexpected departure, and no one except Albina understood its cause. Albina knew that the cause of this strange departure was she. All the time he was staying at Rożanka she had noticed that Migurski was especially animated and cheerful only with her. He treated her like a child, joked with her, teased her, but she sensed with a woman's instinct that in this way of treating her there was not the relation of a grown-up to a child, but of a man to a woman. She saw it in that admiring look and tender smile with which he met her when she came into the room and followed her when she went out. She did not give herself a clear account of what this was, but this attitude of his toward her made her merry, and she involuntarily tried to do what he liked. And he liked everything, whatever she did. And so in his presence she did everything she did with a special animation. He liked the way she raced with the beautiful khorty (a borzoi dog), which jumped up on her and licked her flushed, radiant face; he liked the way she burst at the slightest occasion into infectiously ringing laughter; he liked the way she, while her eyes went on laughing merrily, put on a serious look during the priest's dull sermon; he liked the way she mimicked, with extraordinary accuracy and comedy, now the old nanny, now a drunken neighbor, now him himself, Migurski, passing instantly from the portrayal of one to the portrayal of another. He liked, above all, her rapturous joy in life, just as if she had only this moment come to know the full charm of life and were hurrying to make use of it. He liked this particular joy in life of hers, and this joy in life was roused and heightened precisely by her knowing that this joy in life delighted him. And so Albina alone knew why Migurski, who had come to propose to Wanda, had left without proposing. Though she would not have dared to say this to anyone, and did not say it clearly even to herself, in the depths of her soul she knew that he had wanted to fall in love with her sister and had fallen in love with her, Albina. Albina was very surprised at this, considering herself utterly insignificant in comparison with the clever, educated beauty Wanda, but she could not help knowing that it was so, and could not help rejoicing at it, because she herself had fallen in love with Migurski with all the strength of her soul, fallen in love the way people love only the first time and only once in their life.

## II

At the end of the summer the newspapers brought news of the Paris revolution. After this, news began to come of disturbances being prepared in Warsaw. Jaczewski awaited with fear and hope, with every post, news of the murder of Constantine and of the beginning of the revolution. At last, in November, there arrived at Rożanka first the news of the attack on the Belweder, of the flight of Konstantin Pavlovich, then that the Sejm had declared the Romanov dynasty deprived of the Polish throne, that Chłopicki had been proclaimed dictator and the Polish people were free again. The uprising had not yet reached Rożanka, but all its inhabitants followed its course, expected it to come to them, and prepared for it. Old Jaczewski corresponded with an old acquaintance, one of the leaders of the uprising, received mysterious Jewish factors, not on estate business but on revolutionary business, and prepared to join the uprising when the time came. Pani Jaczewska, not only as always, but even more than always, saw to her husband's material comforts and, as always, by this very thing irritated him more and more. Wanda sent her diamonds to a friend in Warsaw so that the money they fetched could be given to the revolutionary committee. Albina was interested only in what Migurski was doing. Through her father she knew that he had joined Dwernicki's detachment, and she tried to find out everything that concerned this detachment. Migurski wrote twice: the first time he let them know that he had joined the army; the other time, in the middle of February, he wrote a rapturous letter about the victory of the Poles at Stoczek, where they took 6 Russian guns and prisoners: “Zwycięstwo Polakòw i klęska Moskali! Wiwat!” [^2] he ended the letter. Albina was in raptures. She studied the map, calculated where and when the Muscovites must be finally defeated, and turned pale and trembled when her father slowly unsealed the packets brought from the post. Once her stepmother, coming into her room, found her in front of the mirror in trousers and a konfederatka. Albina was preparing to run away from home in men's clothes to join the Polish army. Her stepmother told her father. Her father called his daughter to him and, hiding his sympathy for her, even his admiration for her, gave her a stern reprimand, demanding that she put out of her head her foolish thoughts of taking part <!-- vol. 42, p. 88 --> in the war. “A woman has another task: to love and to comfort those who sacrifice themselves for the fatherland,” he said to her. Now he needed her, she was his joy and consolation, and the time would come when her husband would need her too. He knew what would work on her. He hinted to her that he was lonely and unhappy, and kissed her. She pressed her face against him, hiding her tears, which all the same wet the sleeve of his dressing gown, and promised him to undertake nothing without his consent.

## III

Only people who have experienced what the Poles experienced after the partition of Poland and the subjection of one part of it to the power of the hated Germans, and of another—to the power of the still more hated Muscovites, can understand the rapture the Poles felt in '30 and '31, when, after earlier unhappy attempts at liberation, a new hope of liberation seemed realizable. But this hope did not last long. The forces were too unequal, and the revolution was crushed again. Again tens of thousands of senselessly obedient Russian people were driven into Poland and, under the command now of Diebitsch, now of Paskevich, and of the supreme manager Nicholas I, not knowing themselves why they were doing it, having soaked the earth with their own blood and the blood of their brothers the Poles, crushed them and gave them over again into the power of weak and insignificant people who wanted neither the freedom nor the oppression of the Poles, but only one thing: the satisfaction of their greed and childish vanity.

Warsaw was taken, the separate detachments were routed. Hundreds, thousands of people were shot, beaten to death with sticks, exiled. Among the exiled was young Migurski. His estate was confiscated, and he himself was assigned as a soldier to a line battalion in Uralsk.

The Jaczewskis spent the winter of 1832 in Vilna for the sake of the health of the old man, who since '31 had suffered from heart disease. Here a letter came to them from Migurski from the fortress. He wrote that, hard as were for him the things he had endured and the things that lay ahead of him, he was glad that it had fallen to him to suffer for the fatherland, that he did not despair of the holy cause to which he had given a part of his life and was ready to give the rest of it, and that if a new opportunity appeared tomorrow, he would act the same way. Reading <!-- vol. 42, p. 89 --> the letter aloud, the old man burst into sobs at this place and for a long time could not go on. In the rest of the letter, which Wanda read aloud, Migurski wrote that *whatever his plans and dreams might have been* during that last visit of his, which would forever remain the brightest point in his whole life, he now neither could nor wanted to speak of them.

Wanda and Albina each understood the meaning of these words in her own way, but did not explain to anyone how they had understood them. At the end of the letter Migurski sent greetings to everyone and, among other things, in the same playful tone in which he had treated Albina during his visit, he addressed her in the letter too, asking her whether she still ran as fast, outrunning the khortys, and still mimicked everyone so well. He wished the old man health, the mother success in her household affairs, Wanda a worthy husband, and Albina a continuance of the same joy in life.

## IV

Old Jaczewski's health went from bad to worse, and in 1833 the whole family moved abroad. In Baden Wanda met a rich Polish émigré and married him. The old man's illness grew rapidly worse, and at the beginning of 1833 he died abroad in Albina's arms. He did not allow his wife to look after him, and to the last minute he could not forgive her the mistake he had made in marrying her. Pani Jaczewska returned to the country with Albina. The chief interest of Albina's life was Migurski. In her eyes he was the greatest hero and martyr, to serving whom she resolved to devote her life. Even before leaving for abroad she had begun to correspond with him, at first on her father's instructions, then on her own account. After her father's death, having returned to Russia, she went on corresponding with him and, when she turned 18, announced to her stepmother that she had decided to go to Uralsk to Migurski in order to marry him there. Her stepmother began to reproach Migurski for selfishly wanting to ease his own hard position by enticing a rich girl and making her share his misfortune. Albina grew angry and declared to her stepmother that only she alone could ascribe such base thoughts to a man who had sacrificed everything for his people, that Migurski, on the contrary, had refused the help that she was offering <!-- vol. 42, p. 90 --> him, and that she had irrevocably decided to go to him and marry him, if only he would be willing to give her this happiness. Albina was of age, and she had money—the 300,000 zlotys that the late uncle had left to his two nieces. So nothing could hold her back.

In November 1833 Albina took leave of her household, who saw her off with tears, as if to her death, into the distant, unknown land of barbarous Muscovy, got into her father's covered sleigh, newly repaired for the long road, with her old, devoted nanny Ludwika, whom she was taking with her, and set off on the long road.

## V

Migurski lived not in the barracks but in separate quarters of his own. Nikolai Pavlovich required that the Poles reduced to the ranks not only bear the whole burden of the harsh soldier's life but also endure all the humiliations to which private soldiers were subjected at that time; but most of those simple people who had to carry out these orders of his understood the full hardship of the position of these men reduced to the ranks and, despite the danger of not carrying out his will, did not carry it out wherever they could. The half-literate commander of the battalion in which Migurski had been enrolled, a man who had risen from the ranks, understood the position of the formerly rich, educated young man who had lost everything, pitied him and respected him, and granted him every kind of indulgence. And Migurski could not fail to appreciate the good nature of the lieutenant colonel with white side-whiskers on his puffy soldier's face, and, to repay him, he agreed to teach his sons, who were preparing for the cadet corps, mathematics and French.

Migurski's life in Uralsk, which had now been dragging on into its seventh month, was not only monotonous, dreary, and dull, but also hard. Of acquaintances, apart from the battalion commander, from whom he tried to keep as far away as possible, he had only one, an exiled Pole, a poorly educated, crafty, unpleasant man who was engaged here in trading in fish. But the chief hardship of Migurski's life consisted in this, that it was hard for him to get used to want. He had no means of any kind after the confiscation of his estate, and he scraped by selling the gold things he had left.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 91 --> The only and great joy of his life after his exile was his correspondence with Albina, a poetic, sweet image of whom had remained in his soul since his visit to Rożanka and was now, in banishment, growing more and more beautiful. In one of her first letters she asked him, among other things, what the words of his long-ago letter meant: “whatever my desires and dreams might have been.” He answered her that now he could confess to her that his dreams had been of calling her his wife. She answered him that she loved him. He answered that it would have been better if she had not written this, because it was terrible for him to think of what might have been and was now impossible. She answered that it was not only possible, but that it would certainly be. He answered her that he could not accept her sacrifice, that in his present position it was impossible. Soon after this letter of his he received a notice for 2000 zlotys. By the postmark on the envelope and the handwriting he knew that it had been sent by Albina, and he remembered that in one of his first letters he had described to her in a joking tone the pleasure he now felt in earning by lessons everything he needed—money for tea, tobacco, and even books. Putting the money into another envelope, he sent it back with a letter in which he asked her not to spoil their holy relations with money. He had plenty of everything, he wrote, and he was perfectly happy knowing that he had such a friend as she. With this their correspondence stopped.

In November Migurski was sitting at the lieutenant colonel's, giving the boys a lesson, when the sound of an approaching post bell was heard, and the runners of a sleigh creaked over the frozen snow and stopped at the entrance. The children jumped up to find out who had come. Migurski stayed in the room, looking at the door and waiting for the children to come back, but in through the door came the lieutenant colonel's wife herself.

— And some ladies have come to see you, “pan,” they're asking for you, — she said. — Must be from your parts, Polish women by the look of it.

If Migurski had been asked whether he thought it possible that Albina would come to him, he would have said that it was unthinkable; but in the depths of his soul he was waiting for her. The blood rushed to his heart, and, gasping for breath, he ran out into the front hall. In the front hall a stout, pockmarked woman was untying the kerchief on her head. Another woman was going in at the door of the colonel's quarters. Hearing <!-- vol. 42, p. 92 --> footsteps behind her, she looked around. From under her hood shone Albina's joyous, wide-set, shining blue eyes with frosted lashes. He stood rooted to the spot and did not know how to meet her, how to greet her. “Józio!” she cried, calling him what her father had called him and what she called him to herself, threw her arms around his neck, pressed her flushed, cold face to his face, and laughed and cried.

Having learned who Albina was and why she had come, the kind colonel's wife took her in and lodged her in her own house until the wedding.

## VI

The good-natured lieutenant colonel obtained permission from the higher authorities. A priest was sent for from Orenburg, and the Migurskis were married. The battalion commander's wife was proxy mother, one of the pupils carried the icon, and Brzozowski, the exiled Pole, was best man.

Albina, strange as this may seem, loved her husband passionately but did not know him at all. Only now was she getting to know him. It goes without saying that in the living man of flesh and blood she found much that was commonplace and unpoetic, which had not been in the image she had carried and nurtured in her imagination; but on the other hand, precisely because this was a man of flesh and blood, she found in him much that was simple and good, which had not been in that abstract image. She had heard from acquaintances and friends about his bravery in the war, and she knew of his courage in the loss of his fortune and his freedom, and she had pictured him as a hero always living an exalted, heroic life; but in reality, for all his extraordinary physical strength and bravery, he turned out to be a meek, mild lamb, the simplest of men, with good-natured jokes, with that same childlike smile of a sensual mouth framed by a little fair beard and mustache which had charmed her back at Rożanka, and with an unquenchable pipe, which was especially hard on her during her pregnancy.

Migurski too only now came to know Albina, and in Albina for the first time came to know a woman. From the women he had known before his marriage he could not know women. And what he came to know in Albina, as in a woman in general, surprised him and might rather have disappointed him in woman in general, if he had not felt <!-- vol. 42, p. 93 --> toward Albina, as Albina, an especially tender and grateful feeling. Toward Albina, as a woman in general, he felt an affectionate, somewhat ironical indulgence, but toward Albina, as Albina, not only tender love but also admiration and the consciousness of an unpayable debt for her sacrifice, which had given him undeserved happiness.

The Migurskis were happy in that, having turned all the strength of their love upon each other, they experienced among strangers the feeling of two people lost in winter, freezing and warming each other. The joyful life of the Migurskis was helped too by the share taken in their life by Nanny Ludwika, slavishly, selflessly devoted to her paniusia, good-naturedly grumbling, comical, falling in love with every man. The Migurskis were happy in their children too. A year later a boy was born. A year and a half later, a girl. The boy was a repetition of his mother: the same eyes and the same friskiness and grace. The girl was a healthy, pretty little animal.

But the Migurskis were unhappy in their distance from their homeland and, above all, in the weight of their humiliated position, to which they were unaccustomed. Albina suffered especially from this humiliation. He, her Józio, a hero, the ideal of a man, had to draw himself up stiffly before every officer, go through the rifle drill, stand guard, and obey without a murmur.

Besides this, the news received from Poland was of the saddest. Almost all their close relatives and friends had either been exiled or, having lost everything, fled abroad. For the Migurskis themselves no end of any kind to this situation was in sight. All attempts to petition for pardon, or at least for an improvement of their position, for promotion to officer, did not reach their aim. Nikolai Pavlovich held reviews, parades, drills, went to masquerades, flirted with masks, galloped needlessly about Russia from Chuguyev to Novorossiysk, Petersburg, and Moscow, frightening the people and riding the horses into the ground, and when some bold man ventured to ask for a softening of the lot of the exiled Decembrists or Poles who were suffering for that very love of the fatherland which he himself extolled, he would thrust out his chest, fix his tin eyes on whatever came his way, and say: “Let them serve. Too soon.” As if he knew when it would no longer be too soon, but would be time. And all those close to him: the generals, chamberlains, and their wives, who fed off him, were moved to tenderness by the extraordinary perspicacity and wisdom of this great man.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 94 --> On the whole, however, there was more happiness than unhappiness in the Migurskis' life.

So they lived for five years. But suddenly an unexpected, terrible grief fell upon them. First the girl fell ill; two days later the boy fell ill: he burned for three days and, without the help of doctors (none could be found), died on the fourth day. Two days after him the girl died too.

Albina did not drown herself in the Ural only because she could not imagine without horror her husband's position on hearing of her suicide. But it was hard for her to live. Always active and caring before, she now, having left all her cares to Ludwika, would sit for hours doing nothing, silently looking at whatever met her eyes, or else she would suddenly jump up and run off to her little room and there, not answering the consolations of her husband and Ludwika, cry quietly, only shaking her head, asking them to go away and leave her alone. In summer she would go to the children's grave and sit there, tearing her heart with memories of what had been and what might have been. She was especially tormented by the thought that the children might have stayed alive if they had lived in a town where medical help could have been given. “What for? what for?” she thought. “Józio and I both—we want nothing from anyone, except for him to live as he was born and as his grandfathers and great-grandfathers lived, and for me only to live with him, to love him, to love my little ones, to bring them up.” “And suddenly they torment him, exile him, and from me they take what is dearer to me than the world. Why? what for?” she put this question to people and to God. And she could not imagine the possibility of any answer.

And without this answer there was no life. And her life stopped. The poor life in exile, which she had known how to adorn with her womanly taste and grace before, now became unbearable not only to her but also to Migurski, who suffered for her and did not know how to help her.

## VII

At this time, the very hardest for the Migurskis, there arrived in Uralsk a Pole, Rosołowski, who had been implicated in a grandiose plan of revolt and escape organized at that time in Siberia by the exiled priest Sierociński.

Rosołowski, just like Migurski, just like thousands of people punished with exile to Siberia because they wanted <!-- vol. 42, p. 95 --> to be what they had been born—Poles, had been implicated in this affair, punished for it with rods, and sent as a soldier into the same battalion Migurski was in. Rosołowski, a former teacher of mathematics, was a lanky, stooping, thin man with sunken cheeks and a frowning brow.

On the very first evening of his stay, Rosołowski, sitting over tea at the Migurskis', naturally began to tell, in his slow, calm bass, about the affair for which he had suffered so cruelly. The affair consisted in this, that Sierociński had organized throughout Siberia a secret society whose aim was, with the help of the Poles enrolled in the Cossack and line regiments, to stir up the soldiers and convicts to mutiny, raise the settlers, seize the artillery in Omsk, and set everyone free.

— But was that really possible? — asked Migurski.

— Very possible, everything was ready, — said Rosołowski, frowning gloomily, and slowly, calmly told the whole plan of liberation and all the measures taken for the success of the affair and, in case of failure, for the rescue of the conspirators. Success was certain, if two villains had not turned traitor. Sierociński, in Rosołowski's words, was a man of genius and of great strength of soul. And he died a hero and a martyr. And Rosołowski, in his even, calm bass, began to tell the details of the execution, at which, by order of the authorities, he had had to be present together with all those tried in this affair.

— Two battalions of soldiers stood in two ranks, in a long street; each soldier had in his hand a flexible stick, of such an imperially approved thickness that only three could go into the muzzle of a rifle. The first they led out was Doctor Szakalski. Two soldiers led him, and those who had the sticks beat him on his bared back as he came level with them. I saw this only when he came up to the place where I was standing. Before that I heard only the roll of the drum, but then, when the whistle of the sticks and the sound of the blows on the body became audible, I knew he was coming near. And I saw how the soldiers pulled him along by the rifles, and he walked, flinching and turning his head now to one side, now to the other. And once, when they were leading him past us, I heard a Russian doctor saying to the soldiers: “Don't beat hard, have pity.” But they kept on beating; when he was led past me the second time, he was no longer walking by himself but was being dragged. It was terrible to look at his back. I <!-- vol. 42, p. 96 --> shut my eyes. He fell, and they carried him away. Then they led out the second. Then the third, then the fourth. All fell, all were carried away, some lifeless, others barely alive, and we all had to stand and watch. This went on for six hours—from early morning until two o'clock in the afternoon. The last they led out was Sierociński himself. I had not seen him for a long time and would not have recognized him, he had aged so. His shaven face, all in wrinkles, was a pale greenish color. His bared body was thin, yellow, the ribs stuck out above the drawn-in belly. He walked just like all the others, flinching at each blow and jerking up his head, but he did not groan, and loudly recited a prayer: Miserere mei Deus secundam magnam misericordiam tuam. [^3]

— I heard it myself, — Rosołowski rasped quickly and, shutting his mouth, began to breathe noisily through his nose.

Ludwika, who was sitting by the window, was sobbing, her face covered with her kerchief.

— And what makes you want to go describing it! Beasts are just beasts! — Migurski cried out and, throwing down his pipe, jumped up from his chair and with quick steps went off into the dark bedroom. Albina sat as though turned to stone, her eyes fixed on the dark corner.

## VIII

The next day Migurski, coming home from drill, was surprised by the look of his wife, who, as in the old days, met him with light steps and a radiant face and led him into the bedroom.

— Well, Józio, listen.

— I'm listening. What is it?

— I thought all night about what Rosołowski told us. And I have made up my mind: I can't live like this, can't live here. I can't! I'll die, but I won't stay here.

— But what is there to do?

— Escape.

— Escape? How?

— I've thought it all through. Listen.

And she told him the plan that she had thought up that night. The plan was this: he, Migurski, would leave the house in the evening and leave his greatcoat on the bank of the Ural, and on the greatcoat a letter in which he would write that he was taking his own life. They would take it that he <!-- vol. 42, p. 97 --> had drowned himself. They would search for the body, they would send off papers. And he would hide. She would hide him so that no one would find him. It would be possible to live like that even for a month. And when everything had settled down, they would run away.

At the first moment her scheme seemed to Migurski impossible to carry out, but by the end of the day, when she was persuading him with such passion and assurance, he began to agree with her. Besides this, he was inclined to agree also because the punishment for an unsuccessful escape, the same kind of punishment as the one Rosołowski had told about, would fall on him, Migurski, while success would free her, and he saw how hard life here had been for her after the death of the children.

Rosołowski and Ludwika were let into the scheme, and after long consultations, changes, and corrections the plan of escape was worked out. At first they meant to arrange it so that Migurski, after he had been declared drowned, would escape alone, on foot. Albina, for her part, would set out in a carriage and meet him at an agreed place. Such was the first plan. But later, when Rosołowski told about all the unsuccessful attempts at escape in Siberia in the last five years (in all that time only one lucky man had got away and saved himself), Albina proposed another plan: that Józio, hidden in the carriage, should travel with her and Ludwika as far as Saratov. In Saratov, in disguise, he was to walk down along the bank of the Volga and at an agreed place get into a boat, which she would hire in Saratov and in which he would sail together with Albina and Ludwika down the Volga to Astrakhan and across the Caspian Sea to Persia. This plan was approved by everyone and by the chief organizer, Rosołowski, but there was the difficulty of arranging in the carriage a space that would not attract the attention of the authorities and yet could hold a person. When, after a trip to the children's grave, Albina told Rosołowski how painful it was for her to leave the children's remains in a foreign land, he thought a little and said:

— Ask the authorities for permission to take the children's coffins with you; they will allow it.

— No, I don't want to, I don't want that! — said Albina.

— Ask. That is the whole thing. We won't take the coffins, but we'll make a big box for them and put Józef in the box.

At first Albina rejected this proposal, so unpleasant was it to her to connect deception with the memory of the children, <!-- vol. 42, p. 98 --> but when Migurski cheerfully approved this project, she agreed.

So the final plan that was worked out was this: Migurski would do everything that was needed to convince the authorities that he had drowned himself. When his death had been acknowledged, Albina would submit a petition asking that after her husband's death she be permitted to return to her homeland and to take the children's remains with her as well. And when she had been given this permission too, a show would be made of the graves having been dug up and the coffins taken, but the coffins would be left in place, and instead of the children's coffins Migurski would be placed in the box prepared for this. The box would be put into the tarantass, and so they would drive to Saratov. From Saratov they would take a boat. In the boat Józio would come out of the box, and they would sail to the Caspian Sea. And there, Persia or Turkey and freedom.

## IX

First of all the Migurskis bought a tarantass on the pretext of sending Ludwika back to her homeland. Then began the fitting of the tarantass with a box in which one could lie, though doubled up, without suffocating, and out of which one could quickly and unnoticed get out and climb back in again. The three of them, Albina, Rosołowski, and Migurski himself, devised and fitted the box. Especially important was the help of Rosołowski, who was a good carpenter. The box was made so that, fixed on the undercarriage bars behind the body, it fitted tightly against the body, and the side that fitted against the body could be let down, so that a person, having taken out this side, could lie partly in the box, partly on the floor of the tarantass. Besides this, holes for air were bored in the box, and on top and on the sides the box was to be covered with bast matting and tied with ropes. One could get into it and out of it through the tarantass, in which a seat had been made.

When the tarantass and the box were ready, even before her husband's disappearance, Albina, in order to prepare the authorities, went to the colonel and declared that her husband had fallen into melancholy and had attempted suicide, and that she was afraid for him and asked that he be released for a time. Her gift for dramatic art came in useful to her. The anxiety and fear for her husband that she expressed were so natural that the colonel was touched and promised to do <!-- vol. 42, p. 99 --> everything he could. After this Migurski composed a letter that was to be found under the cuff of his greatcoat on the bank of the Ural, and on the agreed day, in the evening, he went to the Ural, waited for darkness, laid his clothes on the bank, the greatcoat with the letter, and secretly returned home. A place had been prepared for him in the attic, which was locked with a padlock. At night Albina sent Ludwika to the colonel to report about her husband that, having left the house 20 hours before, he had not come back. In the morning they brought her her husband's letter, and with an expression of violent despair, in tears, she took it to the colonel. A week later Albina submitted a petition to leave for her homeland. The grief shown by Migurska struck everyone who saw her. Everyone pitied the unhappy mother and wife. When her departure had been permitted, she submitted another petition, for permission to dig up the bodies of the children and take them with her. The authorities marveled at this sentimentality, but permitted this too.

In the evening of the day after receiving this permission too, Rosołowski, with Albina and Ludwika, in a hired cart with the box in which the children's coffins were to be put, drove to the cemetery, to the children's grave. Albina, kneeling at the children's graves, prayed and soon got up and, frowning, turning to Rosołowski, said:

— Do what has to be done, but I can't, — and she walked off to one side.

Rosołowski and Ludwika moved the gravestone and dug over the top parts of the grave with a spade so that the grave had a dug-up look. When everything was done, they called Albina and returned home with the box filled with earth.

The appointed day of departure came. Rosołowski rejoiced at the success of the undertaking, which had been carried almost to its end. Ludwika baked cookies and little pies for the road and, repeating her favorite saying, “Iak mame Kocham,” said that her heart was bursting with fear and joy. Migurski rejoiced both at his release from the attic, where he had sat for more than a month, and most of all at Albina's animation and joy in life. She seemed to have forgotten all her former grief and all the dangers, and, as in her girlhood days, running up to him in the attic, she shone with rapturous joy.

At three o'clock in the morning a Cossack came to escort them, and a Cossack driver brought a troika of horses. Albina with Ludwika and the little dog sat down in the tarantass on cushions covered with a rug. The Cossack and the driver got up <!-- vol. 42, p. 100 --> on the coachman's seat. Migurski, dressed in peasant clothes, lay in the body of the tarantass.

They drove out of the town, and the good troika carried the tarantass along a beaten road, smooth as stone, between the endless, unplowed steppe overgrown with last year's silvery feather grass.

## X

Albina's heart stood still in her breast with hope and rapture. Wanting to share her feelings, she now and then, smiling slightly, pointed out to Ludwika with her head now the broad back of the Cossack sitting on the coachman's seat, now the floor of the tarantass. Ludwika, with a significant look, gazed motionlessly before her and only puckered her lips ever so slightly. The day was clear. On all sides spread the boundless, empty steppe, glittering with silvery feather grass in the slanting rays of the morning sun. Only, now on one side, now on the other side of the hard road, on which, as on asphalt, the unshod, quick hooves of the Bashkir horses rang hollowly, could be seen little mounds of earth thrown up by susliks; a sentinel animal sat on its haunches and, warning of danger, whistled piercingly and vanished into its burrow. Travelers were rarely met: a train of Cossack carts with wheat or mounted Bashkirs, with whom the Cossack briskly exchanged Tatar words. At all the stations the horses were fresh and well fed, and the fifty-kopeck pieces for vodka that Albina gave made the drivers drive, as they said, courier-fashion—at a gallop all the way.

At the very first station, while the old driver had led his horses away and the new one had not yet brought his, and the Cossack had gone into the yard, Albina, leaning over, asked her husband how he felt, whether he needed anything.

— Splendid, comfortable. I don't need anything. I can easily lie here even two days and nights.

Toward evening they came to the large village of Dergachi. So that her husband could stretch his limbs and refresh himself, Albina stopped not at the post station but at an inn, and at once, giving the Cossack money, sent him to buy her eggs and milk. The tarantass stood under a shed, it was dark in the yard, and, having set Ludwika to watch for the Cossack, Albina let her husband out, fed him, and before the Cossack came back he climbed into his hiding place again. They sent for horses again and drove on. Albina felt <!-- vol. 42, p. 101 --> an ever greater and greater lift of spirit and could not hold back her rapture and gaiety. There was no one for her to talk to but Ludwika, the Cossack, and Trezorka, and she amused herself with them.

Ludwika, who, in spite of her plainness, at every dealing with a man at once suspected that man of amorous designs on her, now suspected this very thing of the huge, good-natured Ural Cossack with unusually clear and kind blue eyes who was escorting them and who was especially pleasant to both women for his simplicity and good-natured kindliness. Besides Trezorka, whom Albina threatened, not letting him sniff under the seat, she now amused herself with Ludwika and her comical coquetry with the Cossack, who did not suspect the intentions ascribed to him and smiled good-naturedly at everything that was said to him. Albina, excited both by the danger and by the success of the affair, which was beginning to come true, and by the wonderful weather, and by the steppe air, was experiencing a feeling of childlike rapture and gaiety that she had not experienced for a long time. Migurski heard her merry talk and he too, in spite of the physical hardship of his position, which he was hiding (he was especially hot, and thirst tormented him), forgetting himself, rejoiced in her joy.

Toward evening of the second day something began to show in the mist. It was Saratov and the Volga. The Cossack with his steppe eyes saw both the Volga and a mast and pointed them out to Ludwika. Ludwika said that she saw them too. But Albina could make out nothing. And she only said, deliberately loudly—so that her husband would hear:

— Saratov, the Volga, — as though talking to Trezor, Albina told her husband all that she saw.

## XI

Without entering Saratov, Albina stopped on the left side of the Volga, in Pokrovskaya Sloboda, right opposite the town. Here she hoped during the night to manage to talk with her husband and even to let him out of the box. But the Cossack, all through the short spring night, did not move away from the tarantass and sat beside it in an empty cart standing under the shed. Ludwika, by Albina's arrangement, sat in the tarantass and, being fully convinced that it was for her sake that the Cossack did not move away from the tarantass, winked, laughed, <!-- vol. 42, p. 102 --> and covered her pockmarked face with her kerchief. But Albina no longer saw anything amusing in this and grew more and more anxious, not understanding why the Cossack kept so constantly by the tarantass.

Several times in the short May night, with the evening glow merging into the morning glow, Albina went out of the room of the inn, past the stinking gallery, onto the back porch. The Cossack was still not asleep and sat, his legs hanging down, on the empty cart standing beside the tarantass. Only before daybreak, when the cocks had already woken and were calling to one another from yard to yard, did Albina, coming down, find a moment to talk with her husband. The Cossack was snoring, sprawled out in the cart. She went up to the tarantass cautiously and pushed the box.

— Józio! — there was no answer.

— Józio, Józio! — she said more loudly, in fright.

— What is it, dear, what! — Migurski said in a sleepy voice from the box.

— Why didn't you answer?

— I was asleep, — he said, and by the sound of his voice she knew that he was smiling. — Well, shall I come out? — he asked.

— You can't, the Cossack is here, — and, having said this, she glanced at the Cossack, asleep in the cart.

And, strange to say, the Cossack was snoring, but his eyes, his kind, blue eyes, were open. He was looking at her, and only when he met her gaze did he close his eyes.

“Did I imagine it, or was he really not asleep?” Albina asked herself. “I must have imagined it,” she thought, and turned to her husband again.

— Bear it a little longer, — she said. — Do you want something to eat?

— No. I want to smoke.

Albina glanced at the Cossack again. He was asleep. “Yes, I imagined it,” she thought.

— Now I'm going to the governor.

— Well, good luck...

And Albina, taking a dress out of the suitcase, went into the room to dress.

Having changed into her best widow's dress, Albina crossed the Volga. On the embankment she took a cab and drove to the governor's. The governor received her. The pretty, sweetly smiling Polish widow, who spoke French beautifully, <!-- vol. 42, p. 103 --> very much pleased the old governor, who was trying to look young. He granted her everything and asked her to come to him again tomorrow to receive from him an order to the town governor in Tsaritsyn. Rejoicing both at the success of her petition and at the effect of her attractiveness, which she saw in the governor's manner, Albina, happy and full of hope, was driving back downhill along the unpaved street in a dolgushka to the landing. The sun had already risen above the forest and played with its slanting rays on the rippling water of the vast flood. To the right and to the left along the hillside, like white clouds, could be seen apple trees steeped in fragrant blossom. A forest of masts showed by the bank, and sails gleamed white over the flood, which played in the sun and rippled in the light wind. At the landing, having got to talking with the cabman, Albina asked whether it was possible to hire a boat to Astrakhan, and dozens of noisy, merry boatmen offered her their services and their boats. She came to terms with one of the boatmen, who pleased her more than the others, and went to look at his boat, a kosovushka, which stood crowded among other boats at the landing. The boat had a small mast that could be set up, with a sail, so that it could go before the wind. In case of calm there were oars and two strong, merry burlak oarsmen, who sat in the sun in the boat. The merry, good-natured pilot advised her not to leave the tarantass behind but to take the wheels off it and put it on the boat. “It'll fit just right, and it'll be more comfortable for you to sit in. God send us good weather, we'll run down to Astrakhan in five days or so.”

Albina struck a bargain with the boatman and told him to come to Pokrovskaya Sloboda, to Loginov's inn, to look at the tarantass and receive a deposit. Everything was going better than she had expected. In the most rapturously happy state Albina crossed the Volga and, having paid off the cabman, set off for the inn.

## XII

The Cossack Danilo Lifanov was from Streletsky Umet on the Obshchy Syrt. He was 34 years old, and he was serving out the last month of his term of Cossack service. In his family there were his 90-year-old grandfather, who still remembered Pugachev, two brothers, the wife of his elder brother who had been exiled to hard labor in Siberia for the old faith, his wife, two daughters, and two sons. His father had been killed in the war with the French. He was the senior man of the household. In their yard there were 16 horses, <!-- vol. 42, p. 104 --> two tsabans of oxen, and 15 sotenniks of their own free land had been plowed and sown with wheat. He, Danilo, had served in Orenburg, in Kazan, and was now finishing his term. He held firmly to the old faith, did not smoke, did not drink, and did not eat from the same dishes as worldly people, and he held just as strictly to his oath. In all his affairs he was slowly, firmly thorough, and to whatever he had been charged by the authorities to do he gave all his attention and did not forget it for a minute until he had carried out the whole of his assignment, as he understood it. Now he had been ordered to escort two Polish women with coffins as far as Saratov, so that nothing bad would be done to them on the road, so that they would travel quietly and get up to no mischief, and in Saratov to hand them over to the authorities in due form. And so he had delivered them to Saratov, with the little dog and with all their coffins. The women were quiet, kindly, Polish though they were, and did nothing bad. But here, in Pokrovskaya Sloboda, in the evening, as he was passing the tarantass, he saw that the little dog had jumped into the tarantass and begun to whine and wag its tail there, and from under the seat of the tarantass he seemed to hear someone's voice. One of the Polish women, the old one, seeing the little dog in the tarantass, took fright at something, seized the little dog, and carried it off.

“There's something here,” thought the Cossack, and he began to watch. When the young Polish woman came out to the tarantass in the night, he pretended to be asleep and distinctly heard a man's voice from the box. Early in the morning he went to the police and reported that the Polish women who had been put in his charge were up to no good and, instead of the dead, were carrying some living person in the box.

When Albina, in her rapturously merry mood, sure that now it was all over and that in a few days they would be free, came up to the inn, she was surprised to see at the gate a smart pair with its side horse running wide, and two Cossacks. At the gate people were crowding, looking into the yard.

She was so full of hope and energy that the thought never even entered her head that this pair and the crowding people had anything to do with her. She went into the yard and, at the same moment glancing under the shed where her tarantass stood, saw that the people were crowding precisely around her tarantass, and in that same instant heard Trezorka's desperate barking. The most terrible thing that could possibly have happened had happened. In front of the tarantass, glittering in his clean uniform, with buttons and <!-- vol. 42, p. 105 --> half-epaulets shining in the sun and with patent-leather boots, stood a portly man with black side-whiskers, saying something loudly in a hoarse, commanding voice. Before him, between two soldiers, in peasant dress, with hay in his tangled hair, stood her Józio, and, as if puzzled at what was going on around him, raised and lowered his mighty shoulders. Trezorka, not knowing that he was the cause of the whole misfortune, his hackles up, was barking uselessly and furiously at the police chief. Seeing Albina, Migurski started and wanted to go to her, but the soldiers held him back.

— It's nothing, Albina, nothing! — said Migurski, smiling his meek smile.

— And here's the little lady herself! — said the police chief. — This way, please. The coffins of your infants? Eh? — he said, winking toward Migurski.

Albina did not answer and only, clutching at her breast, her mouth open, looked at her husband in horror.

As happens in the minutes before death and in the decisive minutes of life generally, she in a single instant felt through and thought through an abyss of feelings and thoughts, and at the same time did not yet understand, did not believe in her misfortune. The first feeling was one long familiar to her—the feeling of wounded pride at the sight of her hero husband humiliated before those coarse, savage people who now held him in their power. “How dare they hold *him*, this best of all people, in their power?” The other feeling, which seized her at the same time as this one, was the consciousness of the misfortune that had happened. And the consciousness of misfortune called up in her the memory of the chief misfortune of her life, the death of the children. And at once the question arose: what for? what for were the children taken away? And the question, what for were the children taken away? called up the question: what for is the beloved, the best of people, her husband, now perishing and suffering? And at once she remembered the shameful punishment that awaited him, and that she, she alone, was to blame for this.

— Who is he to you? Is he your husband? — the police chief repeated.

— What for, what for? — she cried out and, going off into hysterical laughter, fell onto the box, which had now been taken down from the coachman's seat and stood by the tarantass. Shaking all over with sobs, her face streaming with tears, Ludwika came up to her.

— Panienka, dear panienka! Jak Boga kocham, nothing will happen, nothing, — she was saying, senselessly running her hands over her.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 106 --> They put handcuffs on Migurski and led him out of the yard. Seeing this, Albina ran after him.

— Forgive, forgive me, — she was saying. — It was all me! I alone am to blame.

— They'll sort out there who is to blame. And it will come to you too, — said the police chief, and he moved her aside with his hand.

Migurski was led to the ferry, and Albina, not knowing herself why she was doing this, followed him and did not listen to Ludwika, who was trying to persuade her.

The Cossack Danilo Lifanov all this time stood by the wheels of the tarantass and glanced gloomily now at the police chief, now at Albina, now down at his own feet.

When Migurski had been taken away, Trezorka, left alone, wagging his little tail, began to fawn on him. He had got used to him on the road. The Cossack suddenly pushed himself away from the tarantass, tore off his cap, flung it on the ground with all his strength, kicked Trezorka away from him, and went into the eating house. In the eating house he demanded vodka and drank day and night, drank away everything he had with him and on him, and only on the following night, waking up in a ditch, did he stop thinking about the question that was tormenting him: had he done right in informing the authorities about the Polish woman's husband in the box?

Migurski was tried and, for the escape, sentenced to be driven through 1000. His relatives and Wanda, who had connections in Petersburg, obtained a mitigation of the punishment for him, and he was exiled to permanent settlement in Siberia. Albina went after him.

Nikolai Pavlovich, for his part, rejoiced that he had crushed the hydra of revolution not only in Poland but in all Europe, and was proud that he had not violated the precepts of Russian autocracy and, for the good of the Russian people, had kept Poland in the power of Russia. And people in stars and gilded uniforms praised him so for this that he sincerely believed that he was a great man and that his life had been a great good for mankind and especially for Russian people, toward whose corruption and stupefaction all his powers had unconsciously been directed.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
