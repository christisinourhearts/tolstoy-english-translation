<!-- vol. 41, p. 221 -->

1 April

The sciences are countless, and every science is infinite: one can go farther and farther in it. And therefore in every science the very first and main thing is to know which subjects are the most important, which are less important, which are still less important, and which are still and still less important. One needs to find this out because, since it is impossible to study everything, one must study what is most needed.

1

> In our time an enormous quantity of knowledge worthy of study is accumulating. Soon our abilities will be too weak, and life too short, to take in even just the most useful part of this knowledge. A full abundance of riches is at our service, but having taken them in, we must again throw away much of it as useless junk. It would be better sometimes not to burden ourselves with it.

> *Kant.*

2

> With our premature and often too abundant reading, which gives us so much undigested material, our memory usually becomes the master of our feelings and tastes; that is why a great effort of thought is often necessary for us in order to give back to our feeling its primal innocence, to find *ourselves* amid the rubbish of other people's thoughts and views, so as to begin *ourselves* to feel and speak and, I am almost ready to say, so as to begin someday *ourselves* to exist.

> *Lichtenberg.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 222 -->

3

A Persian sage says: “When I was young, I said to myself: I want to know all science. And I got to the point where there were only a few things left that I did not know, but when I grew old, I saw that my life had passed and I knew nothing.”

4

> Cast out of your heart and mind the thought of knowing everything in heaven and on earth. Very little in general exists that we can ever know, either about the ways of providence or about the laws of existence. But even this little is sufficient, fully sufficient; to strive for more is not good for us. And be assured that beyond the real needs of our modest existence and of that kingdom which each of us is destined to rule in unruffled self-possession, that is, ourselves, our thoughts, words, and deeds, every increase of labor intensifies madness, every increase of knowledge intensifies sorrow.

> *John Ruskin.*

5

> The observations and calculations of astronomers have taught us much that is worthy of wonder; but the most important result of their researches is perhaps that they have revealed to us the abyss of all that we cannot know: without this human knowledge reason could never have imagined the whole immensity of this abyss, and reflection on this can produce a great change in determining the final aims of the activity of our reason.

> *Kant.*

6

> “There are grasses on the earth: we see them; from the moon they would not be visible. On these grasses there are threads, in these threads—little animals, but beyond that there is nothing more.”—What presumption! “Compound bodies consist of elements, and the elements cannot be decomposed.”—What presumption!

> *Pascal.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 223 -->

7

*Do not fear ignorance, fear false knowledge. From it comes all the evil of the world.*

Knowledge is infinite. And therefore the one who knows a great deal surpasses the one who knows very little by an infinitely small amount.
