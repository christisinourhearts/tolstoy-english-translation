## THE ESSENCE OF CHRISTIAN TEACHING

Always, from the most ancient times, people have felt the misery, precariousness, and meaninglessness of their existence and have sought salvation from this misery, precariousness, and meaninglessness in faith in a god or gods who could deliver them from the various calamities of this life and in a future life give them the good they desired and could not obtain in this life. And therefore from the most ancient times there have been among different peoples various preachers who taught people what that god is, or what those gods are, who can save people, and what must be done to please this god or these gods in order to receive a reward in this life or the next.

Some religious teachings taught that this god is the sun and is embodied in various animals; others taught that the gods are heaven and earth; still others taught that God created the world and chose from all the peoples one beloved people; a fourth taught that there are many gods and that they take part in the affairs of people; a fifth taught that God, taking the form of a man, came down to earth. And all these teachers, mixing truth with falsehood, demanded of people, besides abstaining from acts considered bad and doing deeds considered good, also sacraments, and sacrifices, and prayers, which more than anything else were to secure people their good in this world and in the next.

But the longer people lived, the less and less these creeds satisfied the demands of the human soul.

People saw, first, that the happiness in this world to which they aspired is not attained, despite the fulfillment of the demands of the god or gods.

Second, as enlightenment spread, trust in what the religious teachers preached about God, about the future life, and about its rewards, since it did not agree with the clarified conceptions of the world, grew weaker and weaker.

If formerly people could believe without hesitation or doubt that God created the world 6000 years ago, that the earth is the center of the universe, that hell lies under the earth, that God came down to earth and then flew away to heaven, and so on, now this <!-- vol. 41, p. 66 --> can no longer be believed, because people know for certain that the world has existed not 6000 years but hundreds of thousands of years, that the earth is not the center of the world but only a very small planet in comparison with other heavenly bodies; and they know that there can be nothing under the earth, since the earth is a sphere; they know that one cannot fly away to heaven, because there is no heaven, but only the apparent vault of the sky.

Third, and chiefly, trust in these various teachings was undermined by the fact that people, entering into closer intercourse with one another, learned that in every country the religious teachers preach their own particular teaching, recognizing their own alone as true, and deny all the others.

And people, knowing this, naturally drew the conclusion that none of these teachings is truer than another, and that therefore none of them can be accepted as undoubted and infallible truth.

The unattainability of happiness in this life, the ever-spreading enlightenment of humanity, and the intercourse of people with one another, through which they came to know the creeds of other peoples, caused people's trust in the creeds taught them to grow weaker and weaker. And meanwhile the need to explain the meaning of life and to resolve the contradiction between the striving for happiness and life, on the one hand, and the more and more clearly understood consciousness of the inevitability of calamities and death, on the other, became more and more urgent.

A person desires good for himself and sees in this the meaning of his life, and the longer he lives, the more he sees that this good is impossible for him; a person desires life and its continuation, and sees that he and everything that exists around him are doomed to inevitable destruction and disappearance; a person possesses reason and seeks a reasonable explanation of the phenomena of life, and finds no reasonable explanation either of his own life or of anyone else's. If in ancient times the consciousness of this contradiction between human life, which demands good and its own continuation, and the inevitability of death and suffering was accessible only to the best minds, such as Solomon, Buddha, Socrates, Laozi, and others, in later times it has become a truth accessible to all; and therefore the resolution of this contradiction has become more necessary than ever.

And precisely at the time when the resolution of the contradiction between the striving for good and for life and the consciousness of their impossibility had become agonizingly necessary for humanity, <!-- vol. 41, p. 67 --> it was given to people by Christian teaching in its true meaning.

The ancient creeds, with their assurances of the existence of a God who is creator, provider, and redeemer, tried to hide the contradiction of human life; Christian teaching, on the contrary, shows people this contradiction in all its force, shows them that it must be so, and from the recognition of the contradiction derives its resolution. The contradiction consists in the following.

Indeed, on the one hand a person is an animal and cannot cease to be an animal as long as he lives in a body; on the other hand, he is a spiritual being that denies all the animal demands of man.

In the first period of his life a person lives without knowing that he lives, so that it is not he himself who lives, but through him lives that force of life which lives in everything we know. A person begins to live himself only when he knows that he lives. And he knows that he lives when he knows that he desires good for himself and that other beings desire the same. This knowledge is given him by the reason that has awakened in him.

And having learned that he lives and desires good for himself, and that other beings desire the same, a person inevitably learns also that the good he desires for his separate being is out of his reach, and that instead of the good he desires, inevitable suffering and death await him, and that the same awaits all other beings. And a contradiction appears, for which a person seeks a resolution in which his life, such as it is, would have a reasonable meaning. He wants his life either to go on being what it was before the awakening of his reason, that is, wholly animal, or to be wholly spiritual. A person wants to be a beast or an angel, but can be neither the one nor the other.

And it is here that the resolution of this contradiction appears that Christian teaching gives. It tells a person that he is neither beast nor angel, but an angel being born from a beast—a spiritual being being born from an animal. That our whole sojourn in this world is nothing other than this birth.

As soon as a person awakens to rational consciousness, this consciousness tells him that he desires good; and since his rational consciousness has awakened in his separate being, <!-- vol. 41, p. 68 --> it seems to him that his desire for good relates to his separate being. But the very rational consciousness that showed him himself as a separate being desiring good for himself shows him also that this separate being does not correspond to the desire for good and life that he ascribes to it; he sees that this separate being can have neither good nor life.

“What, then, has true life?” he asks himself, and he sees that true life belongs neither to him nor to the beings that surround him, but only to that which desires good. And having come to know this, a person ceases to recognize as himself his bodily and mortal being, separate from others, and recognizes as himself that spiritual and therefore not mortal being, inseparable from others, which is revealed to him by his rational consciousness.

In this consists the birth in a person of a new spiritual being.

The being revealed to a person by his rational consciousness is the desire for good; it is the same desire for good that formerly made up the aim of his life, but with this difference: the desire for good of the former being related to a separate, single bodily being and was not conscious of itself, while the present desire for good is conscious of itself and therefore relates not to anything separate but to all that exists.

In the first period of the awakening of reason it seems to a person that the desire for good which he is conscious of as himself relates only to the body in which it is enclosed. But the clearer and firmer reason becomes, the clearer it becomes that a person's true being, his true “I,” as soon as he is conscious of himself, is not his body, which has no true life, but the desire for good in itself—in other words, the desire for the good of all that exists. And the desire for the good of all that exists is what gives life to all that exists, what we call God.

So that the being that is revealed to a person by his consciousness, the being that is being born, is that which gives life to all that exists—is God.

According to the former teachings, in order to know God a person had to believe what other people told him about God, about how God supposedly created the world and people and then revealed himself to people; according to Christian teaching, a person knows God directly, by his consciousness, in himself. In himself consciousness <!-- vol. 41, p. 69 --> shows a person that the essence of his life is the desire for the good of all that exists, something inexplicable and inexpressible in words, and at the same time what is nearest and most understandable to a person.

The principle of the desire for good appeared in a person first as the life of his separate animal being; then as the life of those beings he loved; then, from the time his rational consciousness awakened in him, it showed itself as the desire for the good of all that exists. And the desire for the good of all that exists is the principle of all life; it is love; it is God, as it is said in the Gospel that God is love.

*L. Tolstoy.*
