<!-- vol. 41, p. 57 -->

25 January

There is knowledge that every person needs. Until a person has made this knowledge his own, all other knowledge will do him harm.

1

> Socrates constantly pointed out to his disciples that in a rightly ordered education one should go in each science only up to a certain limit, which ought not to be crossed. In geometry, he said, it is enough to know so much that, when occasion arises, one is able to measure correctly a piece of land one is selling or buying, or to divide an inheritance into parts, or to know how to allot work to laborers. “This is so easy,” he said, “that with a little diligence you will not be at a loss over any measurement, even if you had to measure the whole earth.” But he did not approve of absorption in the great difficulties of this science, and although he himself knew them, he said that they could take up a person's whole life and draw him away from other useful sciences, whereas they are needed for nothing. In astronomy he thought it desirable to know so much as to tell from the signs of the sky <!-- vol. 41, p. 58 --> the hours of the night, the days of the month, and the seasons of the year, to know how not to lose one's way, to hold a course at sea, and to change the watch. “This science is so easy,” he added, “that it is within reach of every hunter, all seafarers, and in general of anyone who wishes to occupy himself with it at all.” But to go so far in it as to study the various orbits described by the heavenly bodies, to calculate the size of the planets and stars, their distance from the earth, their motions and changes—this he strongly condemned, because he saw no use in such occupations. He held such a low opinion of them not out of ignorance, since he had studied these sciences himself, but because he did not want the time and strength that could be spent on what is most needed by a person—on his moral perfecting—to be spent on superfluous occupations.

> *Xenophon.*

2

> Woe to the learned who gather knowledge, woe to the self-satisfied philosophers, the insatiable researchers. These wicked rich men feast every day at their intellectual banquets, while Lazarus goes hungry without ceasing. These people are filled with what is nothing, because this empty knowledge contributes neither to inner nor to social perfecting.

> *Fénelon.*

3

> Turn your gaze away from the world of deception and do not trust your senses—they lie; but in yourself, in what is beyond the personal, seek the eternal man.

> *Buddhist wisdom. Dhammapada.*

4

> The empirical sciences, when they are pursued for their own sake and worked out without guiding philosophical thought, are like a face without eyes. They are one of the occupations suited to middling abilities, which lack, however, the higher gifts that would only be a hindrance in these painstaking researches. People with such middling abilities concentrate all their strength and all their skill on one single bounded field of science, in which they can therefore <!-- vol. 41, p. 59 --> attain the fullest possible knowledge, on condition of complete ignorance in all other fields. They may be compared to workers in clockmaking shops, of whom some make only wheels, others only springs, and others only chains.

> *Schopenhauer.*

5

*It is better to know a few rules of life than to learn many useless sciences. The rules of life will keep you from evil and direct you to good; while the knowledge of useless sciences will only lead you into the temptation of pride and keep you from clearly understanding the rules of life you need.*

Fear not ignorance, but false knowledge. It is better to know nothing than to take for truth what is not true. It is better to know nothing about the sky than to think that it is solid and that God sits on it. But it is only a little better to think that what we see as the sky is infinite space: infinite space is just as inexact as a solid sky.
