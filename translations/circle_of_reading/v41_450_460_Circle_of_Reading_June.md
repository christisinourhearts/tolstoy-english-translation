## BERRIES

The June days were hot and windless. The foliage in the forest is lush, thick, and green; only here and there do yellowed birch and linden leaves break loose. The wild-rose bushes are strewn with fragrant flowers, in the forest meadows there is solid honey clover, the rye, thick and tall, darkens and waves, half filled out, in the low places corncrakes call to each other, in the oats and the rye quails now rasp, now click, the nightingale in the forest only now and then sings a phrase and falls silent, the dry heat bakes. On the roads the dry dust lies motionless a finger deep and rises in a thick cloud, carried now to the right, now to the left, by a chance faint breath of wind.

The peasants are finishing their building, hauling manure; the cattle go hungry on the dried-up fallow, waiting for the aftergrass. The cows and calves gad about with their tails raised like hooks and run off from the herdsmen, away from the noon resting place. The boys tend the horses along the roads and the field edges. The women drag sacks of grass out of the forest; girls big and small, racing one another, crawl among the bushes over the felled forest, gather berries, and carry them to sell to the summer residents.

The summer residents, in their decorated, architecturally fanciful little houses, stroll lazily under parasols in light, clean, expensive clothes along sanded paths, or sit in the shade of trees and arbors at painted little tables and, languishing from the heat, drink tea or cooling drinks.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 451 --> By Nikolai Semyonych's magnificent dacha, with a tower, a veranda, a little balcony, galleries—everything nice and fresh, nice and new, nice and clean—stands a post troika with harness bells, harnessed to a carriage, which has brought from town, for 15 “there and back,” as the coachman says, a Petersburg gentleman.

This gentleman is a well-known liberal public figure, who has taken part in all the committees, commissions, presentations of cleverly composed, seemingly loyal, but in essence most liberal addresses. He has come from the city, where he, as always a terribly busy man, will stay only twenty-four hours, to see his friend, the companion of his childhood and almost his fellow thinker.

They differ only a little in the methods of applying constitutional principles. The Petersburger is more of a European, with a slight partiality even for socialism, and receives a very large salary from the posts he occupies. Nikolai Semyonych, for his part, is a purely Russian man, Orthodox, with a shade of Slavophilism, and owns many thousands of desyatinas of land.

They dined in the garden on a dinner of five courses, but because of the heat they ate almost nothing, so that the labors of the 40-ruble cook and his assistants, who had worked especially hard for the guest, were almost wasted. They ate only the ice-cold botvinya with fresh whitefish and the many-colored ice cream in a pretty mold, decorated with various spun-sugar threads and biscuits. At dinner were the guest, a liberal doctor, the children's tutor, a student, a desperate Social Democrat, a revolutionary, whom Nikolai Semyonych knew how to keep in check, Marie, Nikolai Semyonych's wife, and three children, of whom the youngest came in only for the dessert.

The dinner was a little strained, because Marie, herself a very nervous woman, was worried about the upset stomach of Goga, as (as is the custom among decent people) the youngest boy, Nikolai, was called, and also because, as soon as a political conversation began between the guest and Nikolai Semyonych, the desperate student, wishing to show that he was not shy of stating his convictions in front of anyone, would burst into the conversation, and the guest would fall silent, while Nikolai Semyonych would calm the revolutionary.

They dined at seven o'clock. After dinner the friends sat on the veranda, cooling themselves with cold Narzan with light white wine, and talked.

Their disagreement first showed itself on the question of what the elections should be, two-stage or single-stage, <!-- vol. 41, p. 452 --> and they had begun to argue hotly when they were called to tea in the dining room, which was protected from flies by screens. Over tea there was a general conversation with Marie, whom this conversation could not interest, since she was wholly absorbed in the thought of the signs of Goga's stomach upset. The conversation was about painting, and Marie was arguing that in decadent painting there is un je ne sais quoi that cannot be denied. At that moment she was not thinking about decadent painting at all, but was saying what she had said many times. The guest had no need of this whatever, but he had heard what is said against decadence, and he said it all so plausibly that no one would have guessed that he did not care in the least about decadence or non-decadence. Nikolai Semyonych, for his part, looking at his wife, felt that she was displeased with something and that there would perhaps be some unpleasantness—besides, he found it very boring to listen to what she was saying and what he had heard, it seemed to him, more than a hundred times.

They lit the expensive bronze lamps and the lanterns in the yard and put the children to bed, having subjected the sick Goga to medical procedures.

The guest, with Nikolai Semyonych and the doctor, went out onto the veranda. The footman served candles with shades and more Narzan, and at about twelve o'clock there began a real, lively conversation now about what state measures ought to be adopted at the present time, important for Russia. Both smoked without stopping as they talked.

Outside, beyond the gates of the dacha, the coachman's horses, standing without feed, jingled their little bells now and then, and the old coachman, sitting in the carriage, also without feed, who had lived twenty years with one master and sent all his wages home to his brother, except for some three or five rubles, which he drank away, now yawned, now snored. When the roosters at the various dachas had begun calling to each other, and especially one loud, thin one at the neighboring dacha, the coachman began to doubt whether they had not forgotten him, got down from the carriage, and went into the dacha. He saw that his fare was sitting and drinking something and in between talking loudly. He grew afraid and went to look for the footman. The footman, in a little livery jacket, was asleep sitting up in the front hall. The coachman woke him. The footman, a former house serf, who with his service (the service was profitable—fifteen rubles in wages and, from the masters in tips, sometimes as much as a hundred rubles a year) fed his large family—five girls and two boys—jumped up and, straightening and brushing himself off, went to the masters to say that the coachman was worried and was asking to be let go.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 453 --> When the footman came in, the argument was at its height. The doctor, who had come over to them, was taking part in it.

— I cannot admit, — the guest was saying, — that the Russian people should have to go by some other paths of development. First of all freedom is needed—political freedom—that freedom, as everyone knows, the greatest freedom—with observance of the greatest rights of other people.

The guest felt that he had got muddled and was saying something wrong, but in the heat of the argument he could not quite remember how it ought to be said.

— That is so, — answered Nikolai Semyonych, not listening to the guest and only wishing to express his own thought, which he particularly liked. — That is so, but it is reached by another path, not by a majority of votes but by general agreement. Look at the decisions of the commune.

— Ah, that commune.

— One cannot deny, — said the doctor, — that the Slavic peoples have their own special view. For example, the Polish right of veto. I do not assert that it is better.

— Allow me to finish saying my whole thought, — began Nikolai Semyonych. — The Russian people has special qualities. These qualities...

But Ivan, who had come in with sleepy eyes, in his livery, interrupted him:

— The coachman is worried...

— Tell him (the Petersburg guest said the formal “you” to all footmen and was proud of it) that I will be going soon. And I will pay for the extra.

— Yes, sir.

Ivan went out, and Nikolai Semyonych was able to finish saying his whole thought. But both the guest and the doctor had heard it twenty times (or at least so it seemed to them) and began to refute it, especially the guest, with examples from history. He knew history excellently.

The doctor was on the guest's side and admired his erudition and was glad of the chance to make his acquaintance.

The conversation dragged on so long that it grew light beyond the forest on the other side of the road, and the nightingale woke up, but the talkers kept on smoking and talking, talking and smoking.

Perhaps the conversation would have gone on still longer, but the maid came out through the door.

This maid was an orphan who, to feed herself, had had to go into service. At first she lived with merchants, <!-- vol. 41, p. 454 --> where the clerk seduced her and she gave birth. Her child died; she went to an official's, where the son, a gymnasium student, gave her no peace; then she took a place as under-maid with Nikolai Semyonych and considered herself happy that the masters no longer pursued her with their lust and that they paid her wages properly. She came in to announce that the mistress was asking for the doctor and Nikolai Semyonych.

“Well,” thought Nikolai Semyonych, “something with Goga, most likely?”

— What is it? — he asked.

— Nikolai Nikolaevich are somewhat unwell, — said the maid. Nikolai Nikolaevich, “they,” was the overeaten Goga, possessed by diarrhea.

— Well, and it's time, — said the guest, — look how light it is. How long we've sat up, — he said, smiling as though praising himself and his companions for having talked so long and so much, and took his leave.

Ivan ran about for a long time on tired legs after the guest's hat and umbrella, which the guest himself had stuck in the most unsuitable places. Ivan hoped to get a tip, but the guest, always generous, who would not in the least have grudged giving him a ruble, carried away by the conversation, forgot all about it and remembered only on the way that he had given the footman nothing. “Well, nothing to be done.”

The coachman climbed up onto the box, gathered up the reins, sat sideways, and started off. The little bells rang. The Petersburger, swaying on the soft springs, rode along and thought about the narrow-mindedness and bias of his friend's thoughts.

Nikolai Semyonych thought the very same thing, who did not go to his wife at once. “This narrow-minded Petersburg narrowness is terrible. They cannot get out of it,” he thought.

And he was slow to go in to his wife because he now expected nothing good from this meeting. The whole matter lay in the berries. Yesterday the boys had brought berries. Nikolai Semyonych bought, without bargaining, two plates of not quite ripe berries. The children ran up, asking for some, and began eating straight from the plates. Marie had not yet come out. And when she came out and learned that Goga had been given berries, she was terribly angry, since his stomach was already upset. She began reproaching her husband, he her. And an unpleasant conversation came of it, almost a quarrel. Toward evening Goga did indeed have a bad movement. Nikolai Semyonych had thought it would end with that, but the summons for the doctor meant that the matter had taken a bad turn.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 455 --> When he came in to his wife, she, in a bright-colored silk dressing gown, which she liked very much but about which she was not thinking now, was standing in the nursery with the doctor over the chamber pot and lighting it for him with a dripping candle.

The doctor, with an attentive air, in pince-nez, was looking into it, stirring the stinking contents with a little stick.

— Yes, — he said significantly.

— It's all those damned berries.

— Well, why the berries, — Nikolai Semyonych said timidly.

— Why the berries? There, you fed him, and I don't sleep all night, and the child will die...

— Well, he won't die, — said the doctor, smiling, — a small dose of bismuth and caution. We'll give it now.

— He has fallen asleep, — said Marie.

— Well, better not to disturb him. I'll look in tomorrow.

— Please do.

The doctor left, Nikolai Semyonych was left alone and for a long time still could not calm his wife. When he fell asleep, it was already quite light.

In the neighboring village at that very time the men and the boys were coming back from the night pasture. Some were riding one horse, some had horses on a lead, and behind them ran yearlings and two-year-olds.

Taraska Rezunov, a lad of about twelve, in a short fur coat but barefoot, in a peaked cap, on a piebald mare, with a gelding on a lead and a yearling as piebald as its mother, overtook everyone and galloped uphill to the village. A black dog ran merrily ahead of the horses, looking back at them. The well-fed piebald yearling behind kicked up its white-stockinged legs now to one side, now to the other. Taraska rode up to the hut, got down, tied the horses at the gate, and went into the entryway.

— Hey, you, you've overslept, — he shouted at his sisters and brother, who were sleeping in the entryway on a piece of sacking.

Their mother, who had been sleeping beside them, had already got up to milk the cow.

Olgushka jumped up, setting straight her tousled long flaxen hair with both hands, while Fedka, who had been sleeping with her, still lay with his head buried in the fur coat and only rubbed, with a hardened heel, his slender child's leg, which stuck out from under the caftan.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 456 --> The children had been getting ready since the evening before to go for berries, and Taraska had promised to wake his sister and the little one as soon as he got back from the night pasture.

He did just that. At the night pasture, sitting under a bush, he had been dropping with sleep; but now he had become wide awake and decided not to lie down to sleep but to go with the girls for berries. His mother gave him a mug of milk. He cut himself a slice of bread and sat down at the table on the high bench and began to eat.

When, in just his shirt and trousers, he set off with quick steps along the road, leaving distinct prints of bare feet in the dust, along a road on which there already lay several such bare footprints, some bigger, some smaller, with clearly printed little toes, the girls could already be seen far ahead as red and white specks against the dark green of the grove. (They had got ready a little pot and a little mug the evening before and, without having breakfast or taking any bread with them, had crossed themselves a couple of times toward the icon corner and run out into the street.) Taraska caught up with them beyond the big forest, just as they had turned off the road.

The dew lay on the grass, on the bushes, even on the lower branches of both bushes and trees, and the little girls' bare feet at once got wet and at first grew cold and then warmed up, stepping now on soft grass, now on the bumps of the dry ground. The berry place was in the cleared forest. The girls went first into last year's cutting. The young growth was just coming up, and among the lush young bushes there were patches with low grass, in which berries, still pinkish-white and here and there red, were ripening and hiding.

The girls, bent double, picked berry after berry with their small sunburned hands and put the worse ones into their mouths, the better ones into the mug.

— Olgushka! Come here. There's an awful lot here.

— Really? You're fibbing! Halloo! — they called to each other, not going far apart when they went behind the bushes.

Taraska went farther away from them, beyond the ravine, into forest felled earlier, a year before, where the young growth, especially hazel and maple, was taller than a man. The grass was lusher and thicker, and when places with wild strawberries turned up, the berries were bigger and juicier under the shelter of the grass.

— Grushka!

— Wha-at!

— And what if there's a wolf?

<!-- vol. 41, p. 457 --> — Well, what about a wolf? What are you scaring me for? And I'm not afraid, I'll have you know, — Grushka was saying, and, forgetting herself, thinking about the wolf, she was putting berry after berry, and the very best ones, not into the mug but into her mouth.

— And our Taraska's gone off beyond the ravine. Taraska-a!

— Yo-o! — answered Taraska from beyond the ravine, — come over here.

— Sure, let's go, there's more over there.

And the girls climbed down into the ravine, holding on to the bushes, and out of the ravine by the side gullies to the other side, and there, in the full sun, they at once came upon a little clearing with short grass, strewn all over with berries. Both were silent and worked without stopping, with both their hands and their lips.

Suddenly something bolted, and amid the silence, with what seemed to them a terrible crash, there came a crackling through the grass and bushes.

Grushka fell down from fright and spilled the berries she had gathered, half a mug of them.

— Mama! — she squealed, and began to cry.

— It's a hare, a hare. Taraska! A hare. There it goes, — Olgushka was shouting, pointing at the grayish-brown little back with ears flashing between the bushes. — What's the matter with you? — Olgushka turned to Grushka when the hare had vanished.

— I thought it was a wolf, — answered Grushka, and suddenly, right after her terror and tears of despair, burst out laughing.

— What a fool.

— I got scared something awful! — Grushka was saying, pealing with laughter that rang like a little bell.

They gathered up the berries and went on. The sun had already risen and had dappled the greenery with light, bright patches and shadows, and it glittered in the drops of dew, in which the girls were now soaked right up to the waist.

The girls were already almost at the end of the forest, going farther and farther in the hope that the farther they went, the more berries there would be, when from different places came the ringing halloos of girls and women who had come out later and were also gathering berries.

By breakfast time the mug and the little pot were already half full, when the girls met up with Aunt Akulina, who had also come out for berries. Behind Aunt Akulina, on fat, crooked little legs, toddled a tiny pot-bellied boy in only a little shirt and with no cap.

— He tagged along with me, — said Akulina to the girls, taking the boy up in her arms. — And there's nobody to leave him with.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 458 --> — And just now we scared up a huge hare. How it went cra-ack—so scary...

— You don't say! — said Akulina, and put the little one down again.

Having talked this way, the little girls parted from Akulina and went on with their work.

— Guess we'll sit a bit now, — said Olgushka, sitting down in the thick shade of a hazel bush. — I'm worn out. Eh, we didn't bring any bread; it'd be good to eat some now.

— I want to too, — said Grushka.

— What's Aunt Akulina shouting so hard about? Hear? Halloo, Aunt Akulina!

— Olgushka-a! — answered Akulina.

— What!

— Isn't the little one with you? — Akulina was shouting from beyond the side gully.

— No!

But now the bushes rustled, and from beyond the side gully appeared Aunt Akulina herself, with her skirt tucked up above her knees and a basket on her arm.

— Haven't you seen the little one?

— No.

— What a sin! Mishka-a-a!

— Mishka-a-a!

No one answered.

— Oh, my grief, he'll get lost. He'll wander into the big forest.

Olgushka jumped up and went with Grushka to search in one direction, Aunt Akulina in the other. Without stopping, in ringing voices, they called Mishka, but no one answered.

— I'm worn out, — Grushka was saying, falling behind, but Olgushka kept on halloing without stopping and went now to the right, now to the left, looking about on all sides.

Akulina's desperate voice could be heard far off toward the big forest. Olgushka already wanted to give up searching and go home, when in one lush bush, near a stump with young linden shoots, she heard the persistent and angry, desperate cheeping of some bird, probably with nestlings, displeased with something. The bird was obviously afraid of something and angry at something. Olgushka glanced around at the bush, overgrown with thick, tall grass with white flowers, and right under it saw a little blue heap, not like any of the forest plants. She stopped and looked closely.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 459 --> It was Mishka. And it was him the bird was afraid of and him it was angry at.

Mishka was lying on his fat belly, with his little hands tucked under his head and his chubby, crooked little legs stretched out, and was sleeping sweetly.

Olgushka called his mother and, having woken the little one, gave him some berries.

And for a long time afterward Olgushka told everyone she met, and at home her mother, and her father, and the neighbors, how she had searched for and how she had found Akulina's little one.

The sun had now come all the way out from behind the forest and was hotly baking the earth and everything that was on it.

— Olgushka! Come bathe, — the little girls who had gathered with her invited Olga. And all of them in a big round dance went with songs to the river. Splashing, squealing, and kicking their legs, the girls did not notice how a low black cloud was coming up from the west, how the sun began to hide and come out again, and how there came a smell of flowers and birch leaves and it began to rumble with thunder. Before the girls had time to dress, the rain poured down and soaked them to the skin.

In their little shirts, stuck to their bodies and darkened, the girls ran home, ate, and carried dinner out to the field where their father was plowing over the potatoes.

When they came back and had dinner, their little shirts had already dried. Having sorted over the wild strawberries and laid them in bowls, they carried them to the dacha, to Nikolai Semyonych's, where they paid well; but this time they were refused.

Marie, who was sitting under a parasol in a big armchair, languishing from the heat, on seeing the girls with berries waved her fan at them.

— No need, no need.

But Valya, the eldest, a twelve-year-old boy, who was resting from the overstrain of the classical gymnasium and was playing croquet with the neighbors, on seeing the berries ran up to Olgushka and asked:

— How much?

She said:

— Thirty kopecks.

— Too much, — he said. He said “too much” because that was what the grown-ups always said. — Wait, just go around the corner, — he said, and ran to his nanny.

Olgushka and Grushka meanwhile were admiring the mirror ball, in which some little houses, forests, gardens could be seen.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 460 --> And this ball and much else were not surprising to them, because they expected everything most wonderful from the mysterious and, to them, incomprehensible world of gentlefolk.

Valya ran to his nanny and began asking her for thirty kopecks. The nanny said that 20 was enough and got the money out of her little trunk for him. And he, going around his father, who had only just got up after the hard night before and was smoking and reading the newspapers, gave the twenty-kopeck piece to the girls and, pouring the berries out onto a plate, fell upon them.

When she got home, Olgushka untied with her teeth the knot in her kerchief in which the twenty-kopeck piece was tied, and gave it to her mother. Her mother put the money away and gathered up the washing to take to the river.

Taraska, for his part, who since breakfast had been plowing the potatoes with his father, was asleep at that time in the shade of a thick, dark oak; his father sat right there too, keeping an eye on the hobbled, unharnessed horse, which was grazing on the boundary of someone else's land and might at any minute stray into the oats or into someone else's meadows.

Everything in Nikolai Semyonych's family was today just as usual. Everything was in good order. The three-course breakfast was ready, the flies had long been eating it, but no one came, because no one felt like eating.

Nikolai Semyonych was pleased with the justness of his judgments, which was made clear by what he had read in the newspapers today. Marie was calm, because Goga had had a good movement. The doctor was pleased that the remedies he had proposed had been of use. Valya was pleased that he had eaten a whole plate of wild strawberries.
