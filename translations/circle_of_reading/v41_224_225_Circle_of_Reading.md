<!-- vol. 41, p. 224 -->

3 April

When we die, there can be only one of two things: either what I consider myself will pass into another separate being, or I will cease to be a separate being and will merge with God. The one or the other—in both cases it will be good.

1

> If life is a sleep and death an awakening, then my seeing myself as a being separate from everything is a dream.

> *After Schopenhauer.*

2

Death is the destruction of that body by means of which I perceived the world as it presents itself in this life; it is the destruction of that glass through which I looked. Whether it will be replaced by another, or whether what looked through the window will merge with everything—we cannot know.

3

> A certain limit of life must exist, and just as with the fruits of trees and of the earth, just as with the seasons of the year, everything must begin, continue, and pass. Wise people willingly submit to this order.

> *Cicero.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 225 -->

4

> To the question of whether I—I, separate from the whole world—will live after death, to this question there is only one answer: if it is better that separate life should continue after death, it will continue; but if it is not better, then it will cease.

> Everything I know about God makes me believe that what he has done is the very best for us.

> *After Emerson.*

5

Death delivers so easily from all difficulties and calamities that people who do not believe in immortality ought to wish for it. And people who believe in immortality, who expect a new life, ought to wish for it still more. If neither the one nor the other wishes for it, it is only because people suffer in dying. Suffering holds people back from death.

6

> No one knows what death is, whether it is evil or good. Yet all people fear it as if they knew for certain that it is evil.

> *Plato.*

7

Even if a person knows that when the thunder rumbles the lightning has already struck and therefore the thunder cannot kill, he is still always frightened by the thunderclap. It is the same with death. Even if we know that bodily death destroys only the body but not the life of the spirit, we still cannot help fearing it. But an enlightened person, having overcome this fear in himself, remembers that his life is not in the body but in the spirit; while to an unenlightened person it seems that with death everything perishes, and he fears it and hides from it just as a foolish person hides from a thunderclap, whereas this clap can in no way kill him any longer.

*One must live so as neither to fear death nor to desire it.*
