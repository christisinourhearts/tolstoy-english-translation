<!-- vol. 42, p. 250 -->

13 November

Self-perfection is proper to man if only because he can never, if he is truthful, be satisfied with himself.

1

> Man must develop his predispositions to good. Providence did not place them in man fully finished; they are only predispositions. To make oneself better—this is what man must strive for, and this he must attain.

> *Kant.*

2

> “The root of evil is in not knowing the truth,” said the Buddha.

> From this same root grows the tree of error with its fruits of suffering, numbered in thousands.

> Against ignorance there is only one remedy—knowledge. But true knowledge can be attained only through personal perfecting. Consequently, the amelioration of social evil too can be attained only by people's adopting a higher world-view and, having become better, acting in accordance with the higher world-view.

> And therefore all attempts to improve the life of the world are vain until people themselves become better; the improvement of each separate person is the surest means of improving the life of the world.

> *Hartmann.*

3

> He who asks of life only the betterment of his being in the sense of inner satisfaction and religious submission is less exposed than anyone to the danger of not fulfilling the calling of his life.

> *Amiel.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 251 -->

4

> A Christian cannot be only a teacher or only a pupil; he is always both together, and therefore he always moves forward, and there is no end of perfecting for him.

> Let each one consider himself nothing other than a schoolboy and a pupil. Do not think that you are too old to learn, that you have reached real maturity and development, and that your character and soul are already such as they should be and cannot be better. For a Christian there is no finished course: he is a pupil to the very grave.

> *After Gogol.*

5

> A thinking person experiences a sorrow which may perhaps even lead to the corruption of morals, and of which a superficial person has no notion: namely, when he reflects on the misfortunes that so heavily oppress the human race and, apparently having no hope of anything better, he feels discontent with the providence that governs the order of the world. But not to condemn providence (even if it has destined for us now, in our earthly life, so hard a path) is in the highest degree important: partly so that we do not lose courage amid the burdens of life, partly so that we, laying the blame on providence, do not lose sight of our own guilt, which perhaps is the only cause of all our evils.

> *Kant.*

6

Just as you wean yourself from bad habits, so you can and must wean yourself from egoism. If you want to increase your pleasure, if you want to put yourself forward, to call forth the love of others—stop. If you do not feel like doing anything for others—do not do it, only do nothing for yourself except what is unavoidably needed.

7

> *The first rule for attaining virtue is to think only of your own self-perfection, and not to act with a view to praise from people.*

> *The Chinese Shijing.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 252 -->

8

> He whose evil deeds are covered by good ones lights up this world like the moon coming out from behind the clouds.

> Better than taking possession of the whole earth, better than ascending to heaven, better than ruling over the whole world, better than all this is the joy of the first steps toward holiness.

> *Dhammapada.*

9

Yet another said: I will follow you, Lord! but first allow me to take leave of my household.

But Jesus said to him: no one who has put his hand to the plow and looks back is fit for the kingdom of God.

Luke 9:61–62.

10

He who has placed his life in perfecting looks only forward. Only he who has stopped looks back at what he has done.

Dissatisfaction with oneself is a necessary condition of a reasonable life. Only this dissatisfaction impels one to work on oneself.
