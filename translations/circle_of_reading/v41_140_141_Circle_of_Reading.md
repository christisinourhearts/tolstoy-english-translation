<!-- vol. 41, p. 140 -->

1 March

Fear of death is not natural to a rational being. Fear of death in man is the consciousness of sin.

1

An animal does not foresee inevitable death and therefore does not know the fear of death. But man often fears death. Can it be that man's possession of reason, which reveals to him the inevitability of death, makes his position worse in comparison with the animal? It would be so if man used his reason for foreseeing death and not for improving his life. The more a man lives a spiritual life, the less terrible death is to him. If a man lives by the spiritual life alone, then death is not terrible to him at all. Death for such a man is only the liberation of the spirit from the body. He knows that what he lives by cannot be destroyed.

2

> He does not live who dreads death.

> *Seume.*

3

Nothing so confirms one in the indestructibility, the timelessness of one's life, nothing so helps toward a calm acceptance of death, as the thought that in dying we enter not into a new state but only return to the one in which <!-- vol. 41, p. 141 --> <br> we *were* before birth. One cannot even say: *were*, but rather to that state which is as natural to us as the one in which we find ourselves here and now.

4

Death is a change in the body, the greatest and the very last. We have all lived through changes in our body and are living through them now: at first we were naked little lumps of flesh, then we became nursing infants, then hair and teeth grew, then the teeth fell out, new ones grew, then we began to turn gray, to go bald. And we were not afraid of all these changes. Why then are we afraid of the last change? Because no one has told us what happened to him after this change. But no one, after all, will say of a man who has gone away from us and does not write to us that he no longer exists, but will say only that there is no news of him. The same is true of the dead: the fact that we do not know what will be with us after death, just as we do not know what was with us before this life, shows only that it is not given us to know this, because there is no need to know it. One thing we know: that our life is not in the changes of the body but in what lives in this body. And what lives in this body is a spiritual being, and for a spiritual being there is neither beginning nor end, because there is no time.

5

*Socrates said that if death is the same kind of state as that in which we are when we sleep, losing all consciousness of life, then we all know that there is nothing terrible in this state. But if death is a passage to a better life, as many think, then death is not an evil but a good.*

Death is more certain than tomorrow, than night after day, winter after summer. Why then do we prepare for tomorrow, for night, for winter, and do not prepare for death? One must prepare for it. And there is only one preparation for death—a good life. The better the life, the less terrible death is and the easier death is. For a saint there is no death.
