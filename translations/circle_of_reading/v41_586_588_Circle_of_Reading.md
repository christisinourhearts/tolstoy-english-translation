22 August

It is harmful to spread among people the thoughts that our life is a product of material forces and depends on these forces. But when such false thoughts are called sciences and passed off to mankind as holy wisdom, the harm done by such a teaching is terrible.

1

There are no people with more confused notions of the meaning of life, of good and evil, than the people of science of our time. From this it comes about that the science of our time, while achieving great <!-- vol. 41, p. 587 --> successes in the field of investigating the conditions of the material world, turns out in the life of people to be not only of no use for anything, but even especially harmful.

2

The chief error, which more than anything else hinders the true progress of our Christian humanity, consists in this: that the people of science of our time, who now sit in the seat of Moses, guided by the pagan worldview restored at the time of the Renaissance, have decided that Christianity is a state that people have already outlived, while, on the contrary, that pagan, social, ancient understanding of life which they hold to, and which humanity really has outlived, is the very highest understanding of life—the one to which humanity must now steadfastly hold.

3

> False science and false religion always express their dogmas in high-flown language, which seems to the uninitiated something mysterious, important, and attractive. The reasonings of learned men are often as little intelligible, not only to others but even to themselves, as the speeches of professional teachers of faith. A learned pedant, using Latin terms and fancy words, often makes of the simplest thing something as unintelligible as the Latin prayers of the Catholic priests are to their illiterate parishioners. Mysteriousness is not a sign of wisdom. The wiser a person is, the simpler the language in which he expresses his thoughts.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

4

> A rapid increase of knowledge, acquired at the cost of very slight personal effort, is not fruitful.

> Learning too can go all into leaves without bearing fruit.

> One often meets very superficial heads that know a surprising amount. But what a person arrives at on his own leaves behind in his mind a track along which he can go in other circumstances too.

> *Lichtenberg.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 588 -->

5

> How much better it would be to try to root out the evil of our own age than to glorify in the eyes of posterity the bad deeds of earlier ones! How much better it would be to praise the works of nature than the devastating raids of some Philip or Alexander and others, who, having become famous through the calamities of peoples, were no lesser scourges of mankind than floods devastating whole countries, or a fire devouring a multitude of living beings!

> *Seneca.*

6

> Do not look on learning as a crown to adorn yourself with, nor as an ax to earn your living with.

> *The Talmud.*

7

*Knowledge will always be false if external gain is set as the aim of knowledge. Only that learning which is called forth by inner needs is useful to oneself and to one's neighbors.*

8

Science has now become a dispenser of diplomas for idleness.

The legitimate aim of the sciences is the knowledge of truths that serve the good of people. The false aim is the justification of deceptions that bring evil into human life. Such are jurisprudence, political economy, and, especially, theology.
