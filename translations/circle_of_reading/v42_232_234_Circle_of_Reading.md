<!-- vol. 42, p. 232 -->

7 November

One can look on life as a dream, and on death as an awakening.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 233 -->

1

> I cannot rid myself of the thought that I died before I was born, and that in death I return again to the same state. To die and come to life again with the memory of one's former existence—this we call fainting; to awaken anew with new organs—this means to be born.

> *Lichtenberg.*

2

> If I have killed an animal—a dog, a little bird, a frog, even if only an insect, then, strictly speaking, it is nevertheless inconceivable that through my malicious or thoughtless act this being could turn into nothing, or rather that original force thanks to which this so wonderful phenomenon only a minute before stood before us in all its energy and joy of life. And on the other hand, the millions of animals of every kind entering into life at every moment in infinite variety, full of force and impetuosity, cannot possibly have never existed at all before the act of their birth and, having been nothing, begin to be. If, then, I observe that one thing disappears from my sight I know not where, and another appears I know not whence, and that moreover the one and the other have the same form and essence, the same character, but only not the same matter, which, however, even during their existence is unceasingly cast off and replaced by new matter—then the supposition suggests itself of its own accord that what disappears and what takes its place is one and the same being, which has undergone only a slight transformation, a renewal of the form of its existence, and that, consequently, what sleep is for the individual, death is for the species.

> *Schopenhauer.*

3

In sleep we live almost exactly as we do awake. Pascal says that if we saw ourselves in our dreams always in one and the same situation, and awake in different ones, we would take the dream for reality and reality for a dream.

Reality differs from a dream in that in real life we possess our ability to act in accordance with our moral demands. In a dream, however, we often know that we are committing repulsive, immoral acts, but we have no power to hold ourselves back. So I would say that if we did not know a life in which we would have more power to satisfy moral demands than in a dream, we would consider the dream to be life in full and would never doubt that it is not real life.

Now, is not our whole life, from birth to death, with its dreams, in its turn a dream, which we take for reality, for real life, and in whose reality we do not doubt only because we do not know a life in which our freedom to follow the moral demands of the soul would be still greater than that which we possess now.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 234 --> This is not quite right.

4

> I do not regret that I was born and have lived part of my life here, because I have lived in such a way that I have reason to think that I have been of some use. And when the end comes, I shall leave life as I would leave an inn, and not my own real home, because I think that our stay here is appointed for us as a transitional and only temporary one.

> *Cicero.*

5

> Even if I were mistaken in thinking that the soul is immortal, I would be happy and content with my mistake; and as long as I live, no person has the power to take from me this conviction, which gives me such unchanging peace, such complete satisfaction.

> *Cicero.*

*We put the question wrongly when we ask: what will be after death? Speaking of the future, we speak of time, but in dying we leave time.*
