<!-- vol. 42, p. 383 -->

28 December

What is called science is either the most important human activity, when its aim is to discover the laws of people's life, or the most trivial and stupefying occupation—the investigation of everything that can arouse the curiosity of idle people.

1

It would seem that, in order to recognize the importance of occupying oneself with what is called science, one ought to prove that these occupations are useful. But men of science usually assert that since we occupy ourselves with certain subjects, these occupations will surely someday, somewhere, and for someone be useful.

2

There exists a crude scientific superstition, which has arisen from the same source as religious superstition: from the desire to indulge human weaknesses, and which is just as harmful as the religious one, if not more so. People go astray, they live badly. It is a natural property of people, having become aware of the wrongness of their life, to try to change it; but now “science” appears: the science of state, financial, church, criminal, police, and every other law, the science of political economy, history, and the most fashionable—sociology, and it turns out that the bad life of people proceeds according to <!-- vol. 42, p. 384 --> unchangeable laws, and that the business of people consists not in struggling with their weaknesses and changing their life from worse to better, but only in being present at the course of their life according to the laws discovered by learned men. This superstition so plainly contradicts both common sense and human conscience that it could never have been accepted by people if it were not so soothing, justifying people in their bad life.

No religious superstitions have ever done, or can do, such evil to people as this one.

3

> We lack the knowledge to understand even so much as the life of the human body. Look at what must be known for this: the body needs place, time, motion, warmth, light, food, water, air, and much else. And in nature everything is so closely bound together that one cannot know one thing without having studied another. One cannot know the parts without knowing the whole. We will understand the life of our body only when we have studied everything that it needs; and for that it is necessary to study the whole universe. But the universe is infinite, and knowledge of it is unattainable for man. Consequently, we cannot fully make clear to ourselves even the life of our body.

> *Pascal.*

4

One may study sciences unnecessary for the spiritual life, such as astronomy, mathematics, physics, and the like, just as one may enjoy all sorts of pleasures, games, drives, and walks, when these occupations do not hinder the fulfillment of duties; but it is equally immoral to occupy oneself with sciences that do not contribute to the true, spiritual good of mankind as with pleasures, to the detriment of one's direct duties.

*Science is not what people may call by this name, but what constitutes the highest and most necessary object of knowledge for the good of people.*
