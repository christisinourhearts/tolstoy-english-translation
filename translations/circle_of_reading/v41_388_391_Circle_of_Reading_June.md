## MUST IT BE SO?

Amid the fields stands an iron foundry enclosed by a wall, with huge chimneys that smoke without ceasing, with clanging chains, blast furnaces, a branch railway, and the <!-- vol. 41, p. 389 --> scattered little houses of the managers and workers. In this works and in its mines working people dig about like ants: some, 100 arshins under the ground, in dark, narrow, stifling, damp passages that constantly threaten death, hew out ore from morning till night or from night till morning; others, in the darkness, bent over, haul this ore or clay to the shaft and take the empty little cars back and fill them again, and so they work twelve, fourteen hours a day all week.

So they work in the mines. At the blast furnace itself some work at the furnaces in suffocating heat, others at the tapping of the molten ore and slag. Still others—enginemen, stokers, fitters, brickmakers, carpenters—work in the workshops, also twelve, fourteen hours all week.

On Sundays all these people get their pay, wash, and sometimes even unwashed, get drunk in the taverns and pothouses that surround the works on all sides and lure the workers in, and early on Monday morning they again take up the same work.

Right there near the works peasants are plowing someone else's field with worn-out, scrawny horses. These peasants got up at dawn, if they did not spend the night at the night pasture, that is, did not spend the night by the marsh—the only place where they can feed a horse—they got up at dawn, came home, harnessed the horse, and, taking a hunk of bread, drove off to plow someone else's field.

Other peasants, right there, not far from the works, sit on the highway, having fenced off a shelter for themselves out of matting, and break stone for the highway. These people's feet are battered, their hands are calloused, their whole body is dirty, and not only their face, hair, and beard, but even their lungs are saturated with lime dust.

Taking a large unbroken stone from the unbroken heap, these people set it between their feet, shod in bast shoes and wrapped in rags, and strike the stone with a heavy hammer until the stone splits; and when it splits, they take the broken pieces and strike them until these too break into fine crushed stone; and again they take whole stones, and again from the beginning... And so these people work from the summer morning dawn until night—15, 16 hours—resting only about two hours after dinner, and twice, at breakfast and at noon, they refresh themselves with bread and water.

And so all these people live, in the mines and at the works, the plowmen and the stone breakers, from their young years until old age; and so too live <!-- vol. 41, p. 390 --> their wives and mothers, in labors beyond their strength, contracting diseases of the womb; and so too live their fathers and children, badly fed, badly clothed, in overtaxing work that ruins their health, from morning till evening, from youth until old age.

And now, past the works, past the stone breakers, past the plowing peasants, meeting and overtaking ragged men and women with beggars' bags, who trudge from place to place and feed themselves in Christ's name, there rolls along, its harness bells jingling, a carriage drawn by a matched team of four bay horses, five vershoks tall, the worst of which is worth the whole homestead of any of the peasants admiring the team. In the carriage sit two young ladies, glittering with the bright colors of their parasols and ribbons and of the feathers of their hats, each of which costs more than the horse with which a peasant plows his field; in the front seat sits an officer in a freshly washed tunic, glittering in the sun with braid and buttons; on the box sits a heavy coachman in the blue silk sleeves of his shirt and a velvet coat. He nearly ran over some women pilgrims and nearly knocked into the ditch a peasant going by with his cart empty, in his ore-stained shirt, jolting on his cart.

— Don't you see this, then? — says the coachman, showing his whip to the peasant who has not turned aside quickly enough, and the peasant jerks at the rein with one hand and with the other fearfully takes his cap off his head.

Behind the carriage, the nickel-plated parts of their machines glittering in the sun, two cyclists and one woman cyclist speed noiselessly along and laugh merrily as they overtake and frighten the pilgrim women, who cross themselves.

Off to the side of the highway ride two people on horseback: a man on an English stallion and a lady on a pacer. To say nothing of the price of the horses and the saddle, the black hat with the lilac veil alone costs two months of the stone breakers' work, and for the stick—a fashionable English whip—as much was paid as will be earned for a week of underground work by that young fellow who is walking along, pleased that he has hired on at the mines, and who steps aside, admiring the sleek figures of the horses and riders and the fat, foreign, huge dog in an expensive collar that runs behind them with its tongue hanging out.

Not far behind this company, in a cart, ride a smiling girl with curled ringlets, smartly dressed, in a white apron, and a stout, ruddy man with combed side-whiskers and a cigarette in his teeth, who is whispering something to the girl. In the cart can be seen a samovar, bundles in napkins, an ice-cream freezer.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 391 --> These are the servants of the people riding in the carriage, on horseback, and on bicycles. Today is nothing out of the ordinary for them. They live like this all summer and go on outings almost every day, and sometimes, as today, with tea, drinks, and sweets, so as to eat and drink not in one and the same place but in a new one.

These gentlefolk are three families living in the country and at their dachas. One is the family of a landowner—the owner of 2,000 desyatinas of land; another, of an official who receives 3,000 rubles in salary; the third—the richest family—the children of a factory owner.

None of these people is in the least surprised or moved by the sight of all the destitution and hard labor that surround them. They consider that all this is just as it should be. What occupies them is something else entirely.

— No, this is impossible, — says the lady on horseback, looking back at the dog, — I cannot look at this. — And she stops the carriage. They all talk at once in French, laugh, and put the dog in the carriage, and ride on, covering the stone breakers and those walking along the road with clouds of lime dust.

The carriage, and the riders, and the cyclists have flashed past like beings from another world; while the factory hands, the stone breakers, the peasant plowmen go on with their heavy, monotonous work for others, which will end together with their lives.

“Some people do live,” they think, following with their eyes those who have gone by. And their tormenting existence seems to them still more tormenting.

Must this really be so?

*Leo Tolstoy.*
