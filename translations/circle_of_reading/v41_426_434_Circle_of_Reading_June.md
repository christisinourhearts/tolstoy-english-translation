## VOLUNTARY SERVITUDE [^1]

Doctors advise against occupying oneself with incurable wounds; and I am afraid that I am acting foolishly in wishing to give advice to a people that has long since lost all understanding and that, by no longer noticing its illness, shows that its illness is mortal.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 427 --> First of all, it is beyond doubt that if we lived by the rules and teachings that nature has given us, we would be obedient to our parents, would submit to reason, and would be no one's slaves. To the obedience of each to his father and mother all people bear witness—each in himself and for himself. As for reason, I think that it is a natural property of the soul, and if people sustain it in themselves, it blossoms into virtue.

But what cannot be doubted, and what is perfectly clear and evident, is this: that nature made us all alike—as if she had poured us into one mold—so that we should regard one another as comrades or, rather, as brothers; and if in sharing out our abilities she gave some bodily and spiritual advantages, she still did not wish to sow enmity among us, did not wish the stronger and cleverer, like robbers in a forest, to fall upon the weak; but rather, one must think, by endowing some with greater abilities in comparison with others, she thereby created the possibility of brotherly love, by virtue of which the stronger would help those who need their help.

And if this good mother nature gave us one and the same outward appearance so that each might see and recognize himself in another; if she gave us all the great gift of speech so that, by making a custom of the mutual exchange of thoughts and the communion of our wills, we might come to know one another and draw closer and closer; if she tried by every means to unite and bind together our society, tying it by means of communion as if into a strong knot; and since, finally, this striving of hers toward union is confirmed also by the nature of all things in the world, then there is no doubt that we must be fellow comrades and that no one can think that nature destined some to be slaves and others to be masters.

And truly it is superfluous to examine whether freedom is natural, since no one can fail to feel suffering when he is in slavery, and there is nothing in the world so unbearable as humiliation. Consequently, it must be acknowledged that freedom is natural and that from birth there is proper to us not only freedom but also the need to defend it. But if it should happen that we came to doubt this, or had grown so coarse that we had lost the capacity to know our good and our natural <!-- vol. 41, p. 428 --> strivings, then we would have the honor of learning this very thing from the coarse animals. The animals, if people prove not too deaf, will cry out to them: “Long live freedom!” Indeed, many of them die as soon as they are deprived of freedom. Others—both large and small—when they are caught, fight back with all their strength: with beak, and claws, and horns, showing by this how they prize what they are losing; and once they are caught, they show quite unambiguously how conscious they are of their misfortune, and they go on with their life more in order to grieve over what they have lost than to be content with their slavery. We accustom a horse to service almost from its very birth; but however much we pet it, when it comes to subduing it, it will begin to bite the bit and to strain, as if to show by this at least that it serves not of its own wish but under our compulsion. So that all beings that have feelings are conscious of the evil of subjection and strive for freedom. The animals that are lower than man cannot grow used to submitting otherwise than against their will. What a strange phenomenon it is, then, that man alone has so far betrayed his nature that, born in order to live freely, he has lost even the memory of freedom and the desire to regain it!

There are three kinds of tyrants (I am speaking of evil sovereigns): some rule by the choice of the people, others by force of arms, the third by inheritance. Those who rule by the right of war conduct themselves so that one can see they rule by the right of victory. Those who are born sovereigns are no better than the first: having been brought up in the traditions of tyranny, they suck in the nature of tyrants with their mother's milk and make use of the peoples subject to them as of inherited slaves; according to their disposition—whether they are covetous or prodigal—they dispose of the state as of their inheritance. He, on the other hand, who has received his right from the people ought, it would seem, to have been more capable; and I think he would be so if he did not feel himself exalted above others, if he were not surrounded by flattery and by what is called greatness, with which he does not want to part, if he did not use his power in order to pass it on to his children. And, strange to say, these very sovereigns who rule by the choice of the people surpass all other tyrants in every kind of vice and even <!-- vol. 41, p. 429 --> in cruelty. Apart from strengthening slavery—cutting down the freedom of their subjects—that freedom of which they have so little left as it is—they see no other means of establishing their power. So that, to tell the truth, although I do see some difference between the three kinds of tyrants I have pointed out, yet in essence they are all alike, and although they attain power by different paths, yet their manner of ruling is always one and the same. As for conquerors, they consider that they have the same right over the conquered as over their booty. Their heirs, for their part, treat their subjects as their natural slaves.

But if in our time people entirely new were born, accustomed neither to subjection nor to freedom—people who knew neither the one nor the other—and if these people were offered one of the two: either to be subjects or to live freely—what would they choose? It is not hard to decide that they would prefer to obey reason alone rather than to serve one man. But this is only if they were not like those Israelites who, without compulsion and without any need, made themselves a tyrant. I never read the history of this people without a feeling of vexation, which brings me to the point where I become inhuman and rejoice at the calamities that the Israelites called down upon themselves. As for all people, insofar as they are people, one of two things is needed in order to subject them—one must either compel them or deceive them.

It is astonishing how a people, as soon as it is subjected, at once falls into such forgetfulness of freedom that it is hard for it to wake up in order to regain it. It serves so willingly that, looking at it, you would think it had lost not its freedom but its slavery. It is true that at first people are compelled and conquered by force; but the next generation, which has never seen freedom and does not know what it is, already serves without regret and voluntarily does what its predecessors did under compulsion. Because of this, people born under the yoke and therefore brought up in slavery accept as the natural state the one in which they were born; they accept it without looking ahead, content with life in the position in which they were born, and not thinking of seeking any other rights or any other goods than those they find. For however prodigal <!-- vol. 41, p. 430 --> and careless an heir may be, he will still at some time or other look over his deeds of inheritance, to learn whether he is enjoying all his rights, whether something has not been taken from him or from his predecessor. But habit, which in general has great power over us, has in nothing such force over us as in teaching us to be slaves and accustoming us to swallow with ease the bitter poison of slavery, like Mithridates, who little by little accustomed himself to poisoning himself.

In all countries and in every air slavery is repugnant and freedom is pleasant, and therefore one must pity those who were born with the yoke on their necks. But one must also excuse such people, since they have never seen even the shadow of freedom and therefore do not notice all the evil of slavery. For no one regrets what he has never had, and regret comes only after a lost pleasure.

It is natural for man to be free and to wish to be so; but at the same time his nature is such that he grows used to everything.

And so we shall say that (all the things he grows used to are natural to man. So that) the first cause of voluntary servitude is habit—that habit by which the very best horses first gnaw their bits and then play with them, first strain to get out from under the saddle, and in the end prance in their harness as if with pride. People say that they have always been subjects, that their fathers lived as such—they think they must bear bondage, make themselves believe this, and on the ground of long standing justify the power of those who tyrannize them. But among such submissive people there are also noble people who, feeling the weight of the yoke, wish to throw it off and never grow used to subjection. These people, like Ulysses, who on sea and on land wished to see the smoke of his native hearth, remember their natural rights and their free ancestors; possessing a clear understanding and a penetrating mind, they are not content, like the coarse crowd, with only what is under their feet, but have heads on their shoulders—heads brought up by education and science. And these people, if freedom were to leave the world altogether, if it were lost to people forever, would still feel it in their spirit and would love it, since slavery is always repugnant to them, however it is dressed up.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 431 --> The Turkish sultan grasped this. He decided that books and learning, more than anything, awaken in people the consciousness of themselves and hatred of tyranny. It is said that in his dominions there are only such learned men as he needs. And because of this, however numerous the people who, in spite of everything, have kept in themselves their devotion to freedom, with all their desire and zeal they have no influence whatever; and this is because, in the complete absence of freedom to speak and even to think, they cannot know one another.

And so, the chief cause for which people voluntarily give themselves into slavery is that they are born and brought up in this position. From this cause comes also that other one: that under the power of tyrants people easily become cowardly and effeminate. A tyrant never thinks that his power is secure, and therefore he tries to see that under his power there is not a single worthy person.

This cunning, by means of which tyrants stupefy their subjects, is revealed nowhere so clearly as in what Cyrus did with the Lydians after he had taken possession of their chief city, Lydia, and had taken Croesus, that rich sovereign, captive and carried him off with him. When it was announced to him that the Lydians had revolted, he soon subdued them again; but not wishing to destroy so fine a city or to keep an army there constantly in order to hold it for himself, he devised another means: he set up places of entertainment there, taverns, brothels, spectacles, and issued an order that all the inhabitants should make use of all this. And this method proved so effective that after this he no longer needed to make war on the Lydians. These unhappy people amused themselves by devising various new games; so that the Romans borrowed from them the word by which we call pastime: “ludi.”

Tyrants do not openly admit that they want to corrupt their subjects, but what Cyrus frankly applied, all of them in reality do, since the nature of the common people in cities is such that they are suspicious only of those who love them, and simple and pliable toward those who deceive them. I do not think there could be found any bird that would be caught more readily by bait, or any fish that would take the hook more readily, than all peoples are caught into slavery for the slightest feather with which, as <!-- vol. 41, p. 432 --> the saying goes, their lips are smeared (so that one must be amazed how easily they give in as soon as they are tickled). Theaters, games, shows, buffooneries, gladiators, strange animals, pictures, and other such follies were for the ancients the bait of slavery, the price of their freedom, and the weapon of tyranny. Such were the devices of bait used by the ancient tyrants for the purpose of lulling their subjects to sleep under their yoke. Thus, stupefied by these amusements, entertained by empty spectacles staged before their eyes, the peoples grew used to being slaves no worse than little children who learn to read only in order to know the content of the bright pictures in their books.

The Assyrian kings, and after them the Median ones, showed themselves to the people as seldom as possible, so that the people would think that they were something extraordinarily great and would remain in this delusion, for it is natural to people to exaggerate in their imagination what they cannot see. In this way the peoples that were under the power of Assyrian rule were accustomed to be slaves by means of this mystery, and were slaves the more willingly the less they knew their master; and sometimes they did not know at all whether he existed, and all of them feared on trust one whom no one had seen. The first kings of Egypt showed themselves only with sometimes a branch and sometimes fire on their heads, and went out in masks, and by this aroused in their subjects respect and wonder; whereas people not too servile and stupid would, it seems to me, have found them merely amusing and ridiculous. Downright pitiful and paltry are the tricks that the tyrants of antiquity used for establishing their tyranny among a people so pliable to their deceptions. There was no net into which the people would not fall; and never did tyrants deceive the people more easily, or subject it more easily, than when they themselves laughed at it. And so, was there ever a time when tyrants, for the sake of establishing their power, did not accustom the people not only to obedience and slavery, but also to adoration?

All that I have said so far about how tyrants teach people to obey them concerns the simple and coarse people.

Now, however, I come to the question that constitutes the secret and the chief weapon of tyranny. Whoever thinks that tyrants guard themselves with the weapons of guards and the guns of a fortress is much mistaken; true, they make use of these, but more for <!-- vol. 41, p. 433 --> form and for fear than because they really rely on them. Bodyguards keep out of the palaces not the people who are dangerous, but only those insignificant people who can do the tyrant no harm.

If we count the number of Roman emperors who were killed, we shall see that their bodyguards did not so much deliver them from dangers as kill them. It is not weapons and not armed men—horse and foot—that protect tyrants; but, however hard it is to believe this, three or four men support the tyrant and hold the whole country in slavery for him. The tyrant's circle of intimates has always consisted of five or six men; these men either crept into his confidence themselves or were drawn close by him in order to be the accomplices of his cruelties, the companions of his pleasures, the arrangers of his enjoyments, and the confederates of his robberies. These six make their chief wicked not only with his own malice but with theirs as well. These six have six hundred who are under their power and who stand toward the six just as the six stand toward the tyrants. And the six hundred have under them six thousand, whom they have raised up, to whom they have given the administration of provinces or of money matters—so that these should serve their covetousness and cruelty and should do evil that can continue only with them there and only by them escapes lawful punishment. After these follows a still larger retinue. And whoever cares to amuse himself—unraveling this net—will see that not only six thousand, but hundreds of thousands, millions are chained to the tyrant by this chain. For the sake of this, offices are multiplied, all of which are a support of tyranny, and all the people who hold these offices have their advantages in it, and by these advantages they are bound to the tyrants; and the people to whom tyranny is advantageous are such a multitude that there would be almost as many of them as of those to whom freedom would be a joy. And as doctors say that if there is something spoiled in our body, all the bad humors at once flow to that sick place, just so to a sovereign, as soon as he becomes a tyrant, everything bad gathers—all the dregs of the state, a heap of thieves and scoundrels, capable of nothing, but covetous and greedy—they gather in order to share in the booty, in order to be little tyrantlings under a big tyrant. So do great robbers and famous corsairs. Some go scouting, <!-- vol. 41, p. 434 --> others stop travelers; some keep watch, others lie in ambush; some kill, others rob; and—although there is a difference between them, for some are servants and others chiefs—all of them are sharers in the booty.

So the tyrant subjects some of his subjects by means of others and is guarded by those whom he would have to fear if they were not scoundrels. But, as the saying goes, “to split firewood, they make wedges of the same wood”—so too his bodyguards are the same as he. It happens that they too suffer from him; but these God-forsaken, lost people are ready to bear evil, if only they may be in a position to do it not to the one who does evil to them, but to those who bear it and cannot do otherwise.

*La Boétie.*

## Notes

[^1]: Étienne de La Boétie was born in 1530 and at the age of 16 wrote the discourse on voluntary servitude from which an extract is given here. De La Boétie was a member of the court in Bordeaux and a close friend of Montaigne. Montaigne valued him highly as a writer and as a man, and wrote an account of his illness and death. De La Boétie died in 1563. The editor of his discourse says finely of it that the reading of such discourses is nourishment for that marrow of lions' bones (moëlle de lion) of which, unfortunately, present-day generations are deprived.
