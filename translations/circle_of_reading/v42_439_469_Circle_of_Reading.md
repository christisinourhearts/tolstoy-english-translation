## \* RESERVE DAYS OF THE FIRST EDITION OF “CIRCLE OF READING”

## I WEALTH

Wealth is not essential in that needs increase with the increase of wealth, for the most part in such a way that the more wealth there is, the less wealth suffices to satisfy them.

1

It is difficult, if not impossible, to find a reasonable limit to our desires for property. In fact: the contentment of this or that person in this respect depends not on an absolute but on a certain purely relative quantity, namely on the relation between his demands and his possessions. Therefore the latter in itself means as little as a numerator without a denominator. Things that it never even entered a person's head to demand are entirely superfluous to him, and he is quite content without having them; while another, who has a hundred times more, feels unhappy because he lacks what he has come to demand.

*Schopenhauer.*

2

Whoever has less than he wishes must know that he has more than he deserves.

*Lichtenberg.*

3

*Not he is poor who has little, but he who wants more.*

*Seneca.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 440 -->

4

Nothing can be more “decent” than to have few needs and to satisfy them oneself, so as to be able to give, instead of using every occasion to grab.

It is far more “decent” to satisfy one's needs oneself than to be served in luxury; this may seem not decent nowadays and to a small number, but it is the only “decency” always and for everyone.

*Emerson.*

5

Do not gather treasures for yourselves on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves dig under and steal; but gather treasures for yourselves in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys and where thieves do not dig under and do not steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

*Matthew 6:19–21.*

6

Acquire for yourself that wealth which thieves cannot steal, on which tyrants dare not encroach, which even after death will remain with you, never diminishes and never decays.

*Indian proverb.*

Whoever, keeping what he has, reduces his needs will feel rich; and having felt rich, he will clearly see the senselessness and the heaviness of wealth.

## II WEALTH

Wealth not only does not give the good, but always deprives a person of the true good.

1

Woe to you who acquire house to house, who join field to field, until there is no room, so that you alone may live on the earth.

*Isaiah 5:7, 8.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 441 -->

2

Woe to you, the rich! For you have already received your consolation.

Woe to you who are sated now! For you will hunger. Woe to you who laugh now! For you will weep and sob.

Woe to you when all people speak well of you, for that is how their fathers treated the false prophets.

*Luke 6:24–26.*

3

If we begin to have in view only how to acquire more money, and not how to use it for our needs, then the order is already turned upside down, and it owns us, and not we it. So let us free ourselves from this cruel slavery and become free at last. Why do we ourselves invent for ourselves a multitude of various bonds?.. Are the natural bonds, the necessities of everyday life, and the accumulation of countless affairs not enough for you, and yet you weave still other nets for yourself and tie yourself up by the feet with them.

*John Chrysostom.*

4

Every day we blaspheme God by plunder and covetousness. Perhaps one of you will say: you talk about covetousness every day. Oh, if only one could speak of this every night as well! Oh, if only he too who accompanies you in the marketplace and sits with you at table, if only wives, and friends, and children, and slaves, and farmers, and neighbors, and even the floor and the walls could pour forth this speech, so that through it we might fall away from covetousness at least a little!.. This sickness has seized the whole universe, it possesses the souls of all, and truly, great is the power of Mammon!

*John Chrysostom.*

5

Heavy blankets produce nightmares, holding back and constricting the circulation of the blood. The possession of great wealth acts in a similar way, calling forth in waking life the same heavy states by its holding back and constricting of the movements of the soul.

*“The World's Advance Thought.”*

6

*It is much easier to acquire wealth than to get rid of it.*

*Thoreau.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 442 -->

7

If the rich were happy, we would know that there is no God.

*V. N.*

Better lack than excess.

## III THE DIVINE NATURE OF THE SOUL

Human nature is good.

1

Providence is the divine order. Everything in heaven assists what lives on earth.

The contemplation of good is received by us not as the rays of the sun are, whose bright brilliance blinds the eye with an excess of light; this contemplation enlightens and strengthens the power of sight, so that each person little by little receives this spiritual clarity.

This clarity also calms all the senses. Constantly shining in the mind, it enlightens the whole soul and transforms it into a divine essence.

Because the soul is made divine by contemplating beauty, the infinity of good.

He who is truly a man is a divine being and cannot be equated with any animal living on the earth.

Man is a mortal god. Without leaving the earth, he lives on high. Such is the greatness of his nature.

*The Egyptian Hermes Trismegistus.*

2

Virtue in us comes not from nature and not from teaching, but is put into us by the deity.

*Plato.*

3

It is natural for man to be good, just as it is natural for water to flow from above downward.

*Eastern wisdom.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 443 -->

4

*If you marvel, looking at the stars or the depths of the sea, enter into your own soul and marvel.*

*Isidore.*

The first step toward perfecting lies in learning to distinguish in our soul the voice of God from other voices.

## IV VEGETARIANISM

The simpler the food, the more pleasant it is—it does not pall, the healthier, and the more accessible always, everywhere.

1

“Since I have already begun to confide to you with what enthusiasm I took up the study of philosophy in my youth, I will not hide from you the reverence for the teaching of Pythagoras that Sotion (Seneca's teacher) inspired in me. Sotion set out to me the grounds on which first he himself, and later Sextius, resolved to abstain from the flesh of animals. Each of them had his own reason, but both were excellent. Sotion asserted that man is able to find sufficient nourishment for himself without the shedding of animals' blood, and that cruelty inevitably becomes inherent in man as soon as he resorts to killing to satisfy the lust of gluttony. He loves to repeat that we are urgently obliged to limit our need for luxury; that, besides, variety of food is harmful to health and alien to our nature. If these Pythagorean rules are right, he said, then abstinence from meat food must bring us nearer to blamelessness; and if they are mistaken, then keeping them will at least accustom us to moderation and simplicity of life! Besides, what loss can you suffer from the loss of your cruelty? I only want to deprive you of the food that is proper to lions and kites. Moved by these and similar arguments, I began to abstain from meat food, and after a year the habit of such abstinence was not only easy but pleasant. I firmly believed then that my mental faculties had become more active, and now I consider it unnecessary to assure myself of the truth of this. You will ask why, then, I returned to my former habits? Because, I will answer, by the will of fate I happened in my youth to live during the reign of the emperor <!-- vol. 42, p. 444 --> Tiberius, under whom certain foreign religions became an object of suspicion. Among the signs of belonging to the suspected superstitions was abstinence from meat food. Then, yielding to my father's entreaties, I returned to my original way of eating, after which it was no longer hard for him to persuade me to take part indiscriminately even in the most luxurious feasts.

I say this, — Seneca continues, — in order to prove to you how powerful the early impulses of youth toward all that is good and true are under the influence of the exhortations of virtuous mentors. If we go astray in youth, it is partly through the fault of our guides, who teach us to argue and not to live, and partly through our own fault, in that you expect from our teachers not so much the encouragement of the good inclinations of our soul as the development of the faculties of our mind. And from this it comes about that instead of a love of wisdom, only a love of words shows itself in us.”

*Seneca.*

2

If people ate only when they were very hungry, and if they fed on simple, pure, and healthy food, they would not know illnesses, and it would be easier for them to govern their souls and bodies.

3

If we want to be healthy, we must live as nature prescribes to us—feeding on fruits, nuts, bread, vegetables, and the like, and not on the remains of animals.

4

In olden times there was no need for such an increase in the number of doctors, nor for such a quantity of medical instruments and medicines. The preservation of health was simple for a simple reason. Various dishes have bred various diseases. Note what an enormous number of lives a single stomach consumes—the devastator of lands and seas.

*Seneca.*

Try not to complicate but to simplify your needs, and the most pressing of them—food. The more you simplify, the more you will gain, and you will lose nothing.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 445 -->

## V VEGETARIANISM

The hypocrisy of people who are unable to kill animals and do not give up using them for food.

1

With your own hands you would not slaughter a bull or stab a lamb, and yet you want to lay this bloody work on another. I could vouch that many will say: “I cannot kill.” Do you really think, then, that you have the right, do you really have the conscience, the spirit, to hire another to do the work which you would rather leave undone than do yourself. Believe me, you are *your brother's keeper.* Do not lower him to the level of your slave, chained to labor against which your higher instincts revolt.

*Mayor.*

2

Not one beast, even the fiercest, sheds blood as senselessly as man—this king of creation—sheds it. And to feel this, one need only look at least once in one's life at the well-ordered slaughterhouses, at the executioners called slaughtermen, and at the gluttons called gastronomes.

*M. Yu. Goldstein.*

3

*The true responsibility for the cruelties committed by butchers rests on those who make use of the services of these butchers while keeping their peace of mind.*

*Nicholson.*

4

The killing of living beings is so contrary to the nature of man that few men and women could eat the animals they would be forced to kill themselves, and yet, feasting on the remains of a slain animal, they forget, or pretend to forget, its attachment to life and its sufferings before death.

*Phillips.*

5

If you wait for a living and thinking being to be deprived of life by others, and if it is repugnant to you yourselves to tear out the heart <!-- vol. 42, p. 446 --> and shed the blood of your victim, then why, I ask you, contrary to nature and pity, do you feed on beings endowed with conscious life.

*Plutarch.*

If a person cannot or does not wish to live without using the flesh of animals as food, then at least he ought to kill them himself, but people are so inhuman that they commit yet another crime, worse than the killing itself: by corrupting them, they make other poor and benighted people kill living beings.

## VI VEGETARIANISM

All the arguments against meat-eating, however strong they may be, are insignificant before the basic argument that in animals we sense the same force of life that lives in us. We sense that by violating this life we commit something like suicide.

He who does not stifle in himself this feeling, natural to all people, will not need any other arguments.

1

We display inhuman cruelty toward the lower animals, raising them and killing them for food, and time shows that we gain nothing by this; on the contrary, we ourselves are deprived of health and good taste, and we lose in the economic respect.

*Salt.*

2

Meat-eating is contrary not only to our physical nature but in other respects as well. The mind and the thinking faculty grow dull from surfeit and fatness; meat food and wine may perhaps give solidity to the body, but this only contributes to the weakening of the mind.

*Plutarch.*

3

The consumption of alcoholic drinks does not lead to killing, but every morsel of meat consumed by us is the product of killing; its consumption calls forth a craving for alcoholic drinks, which in its turn incites to killing.

Thus sowing and reaping are harmoniously linked, and the circle of evil is closed.

*Lucy Mallory.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 447 -->

4

It is extremely important not to distort the natural taste and not to make children carnivorous, if not for the sake of their health, then *at least for the sake of their character*, because, however this may be explained, it is certain that great lovers of meat are generally hard-hearted people.

*Rousseau.*

5

We may be accused of exaggeration if we say that meat food leads to premature death; however, there is no doubt that it is the cause of premature old age, of diseases and disorders, by virtue of the habits it engenders: alcoholism, excess in the sexual instinct, and intemperance in many other respects.

*Dr. A. Kingsford.*

6

Whoever adopts vegetarianism to improve his health may easily return to meat-eating because of those same considerations of health. But the humane vegetarian will always remain a vegetarian; he will never return to meat-eating, will never, either for his taste or for his health, demand the killing and torment of animals with all the other cruelties that accompany them.

*Lucy Mallory.*

7

Whoever does harm to animals out of a desire to give himself pleasure adds nothing to his happiness in this life and in the life to come; whereas he who does no harm to animals: does not shut them up, does not kill them, but wishes good to all sentient beings, he experiences happiness without end.

*Indian Manu.*

8

Of all manner of sins which I have committed against Amshaspand-Brahman in heaven, against cattle, against animals of every kind in the world, in that I beat them, tormented them, unjustly killed them, did not give them fodder and drink in due time, castrated them; did not guard them from the robber (the beast of prey), from the wolf, did not protect them from excessive heat and frost; slaughtered young cows, working oxen, destroyed horses fit for war, slaughtered rams, goats, cocks, and hens—so that both they, the good-natured ones, and Amshaspand-Brahman were offended <!-- vol. 42, p. 448 --> by me and displeased—I repent in thought, and word, and deed, bodily and spiritual, and ask forgiveness.

*Khorda Avesta. The sacred book of the Persians.*

9

*One cannot close one's eyes to the fact that, by eating meat, I demand the killing of living beings for the satisfaction of luxury, of taste.*

What matters is not the practical arguments against meat-eating, all of them may be true, but there may be cases where they do not apply; one thing is always and for everyone true and binding: the livelier the compassion in a person for everything living (include in this whatever you like), the kinder, the better, the more a human being he is. To kill animals, however, out of curiosity, for the pleasure of hunting, or for a pleasant taste is not compassionate, but coarse and insolent, cruel.

## VII REQUITAL

Even if it happens that good and evil do not receive their requital in time, still for the most part, even in the world, life goes better for the good person than for the evil one.

1

Only the humble of heart acknowledges God. Humility does not arouse envy.

Trees are carried away by the torrent; reeds remain.

Suniti said: “My child, do not grieve that you were not valued, because no one can take away what you have done, or give you what you have not done. A reasonable person is content with the respect that he deserves.”

Be good-natured, respectful, friendly, concerned for the advantage of others—and the good will come to you as naturally as waters pour down onto low ground.

*Indian Vishnu.*

2

The doer of evil prospers as long as the fruit of his evil deeds has not ripened; but when this fruit ripens, then the doer of iniquity will see all his evil in plain sight.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 449 --> The virtuous one sees evil days—the fruits of his good deeds have still, still not ripened; but blessed he will be when his deeds bear good fruit.

*Buddhist wisdom.*

3

If with evil and inhuman people you are not meek, but cruel and inhuman, then beware lest you become just like them.

The best way to take revenge on an offender is not to follow his example.

4

The vital condition of virtue, as virtue, consists in being content with it and wishing that the reward should go to others; and the vital condition of vice likewise consists in enjoying it and wishing that the requital for its deeds should fall to the lot of others.

*John Ruskin.*

5

The requital for fulfilling a commandment is itself a commandment, that is, if someone fulfills one of the commandments, the Lord opens to him the possibility of fulfilling another as well, which is no small reward, for this leads to his own benefit.

*The Talmud.*

6

No virtue is ever solitary. It always has neighbors.

*Chinese wisdom.*

7

Do not be rude and harsh with anyone, for another may just as easily turn to you with the same; rage brings suffering in its train, a blow is answered with a blow.

*Buddhist wisdom.*

8

Anticipate every person with your greeting. It is not enough to keep on a peaceful footing only when occasion arises, namely—not to start a quarrel with one's neighbor and not to answer his greeting; no, one must prepare peace, forestall <!-- vol. 42, p. 450 --> discord and strife, making it impossible for them to arise, for when matters have already come to the need for peaceful intervention, who can then vouch for success.

*The Talmud.*

9

The path of pleasure is winding; the path of duty is straight. Go by the latter—and you will see that the former will follow you unfailingly.

10

By laws as old as the universe, which have existed since the time of the first plan of creation, truth steadily strives toward triumph. Wait for the outcome. If you live to see it, you will see that, as in every struggle, everyone will be repaid according to his deserts.

*Carlyle.*

Do not do good and do not avoid evil for the sake of worldly requital (often there is none); but when evil befalls you, know that it is that very force of evil which you brought into the world, and therefore do not murmur. And when you feel joy from the good that you have done, do not count it as your due, but accept it as a gift you have not deserved.

## VIII HEALING

**1**

It seems strange why for the labor of producing necessary, excellent objects, such as bread, fruit, glass, iron, so little is paid, while for unnecessary and often harmful labor, such as the bar, the art of healing, people pay not only dearly but insane sums. But in essence it cannot be otherwise. Bread, glass, iron are objects needed by everyone, and their quantity can be brought to the point of satisfying everyone; whereas the bar, art, medicine are the possession of a small number of rich people, because in essence no one needs them. That is why all attempts to spread the bar, art, and medicine, such as they are now, to the masses have so far remained unsuccessful. If the bar, art, and medicine are to be needed by the masses, they must be completely different.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 451 -->

2

The medical art, such as it is now, hardly brings more benefit than harm. Rather the reverse.

3

In order for the medical art to be not harmful but useful, it must learn to serve the *masses.*

4

Faith in doctors, precisely in this one and not that one (although they all know one and the same semblance of science), is exactly the same as faith in wonder-workers, icons, prophets, healers (Wunderfrau, the Zouave, Kuzmin).

The sick person does not know that his life depends on spiritual causes not subject to material laws, but instead of seeing this cause in the spiritual principle of the world and of the soul, he wants to see it in a living person: a wonder-worker, a prophet, a doctor. And just as it is hard for a monk, an elder, to whom his devotees ascribe the gift of miracles and prophecy, not to believe himself in the gift ascribed to him, so exactly it is for the doctor from whom healing is expected.

5

I would stop believing in good, in God, and would not want to go on living, if I could believe that a woman in childbirth, the wife of a rich man, for whom a doctor was sent for and paid 10,000 rubles, survived because of this, while the wife of his coachman, ill with the same illness, died because the coachman did not have 10,000.

6

No one needs more than doctors to remember the rule that true knowledge consists in knowing that we do not know what we do not know. The most careful and reasonable doctors are involuntarily carried away by the demands for help with which people turn to them, and, in order to think that they can help, they assure themselves and others that they know what they do not know.

7

In nothing is the false arrangement of our society so evident as in medicine. Medicine, occupied with treating only <!-- vol. 42, p. 452 --> the rich, has devised such methods as are applicable only among the rich; for the great masses of the people it is powerless and only burdens these masses with the taxes that are collected from them for the maintenance of medical institutions.

8

There exists a strange opinion, very widespread in our time, that medicine, a means of relieving suffering and of putting off death—a service to life—is in itself a good deed. But there are no good deeds. There are only good strivings. One can lessen suffering and serve life by thousands of means besides medicine. But to imagine that a form of activity can be good in itself is only a sign of a crude failure to understand the law of morality.

9

It is enough to begin caring for one's health for this care to swallow one up entirely and exclude the possibility of serving people.

All illnesses bring a person nearer to that passage into another state to which all people are doomed.

## IX LYING

A lie is a deliberate distortion of the truth, which can be committed only for the sake of attaining the aims of the animal personality.

1

The world does not lie. Study the physiognomy of all beings, and you will see that all say what they must. From generation to generation the expression of their inner feelings has been imprinted in their features by involuntary signs.

Neither the wolf nor the tiger flatters in order to tear you apart the better. The lion does not stop frowning in order to win you over. He cannot turn his roar into a feigned sound. The snake hides, but in its eyes you see its hatred. It does not lie. Even the anthropoid apes do not try to change the expression of their faces.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 453 --> So it is with all animals. Those that, like the spider, set traps do not caress their victims. They do not lie.

Animals utter cries, imitate sounds, but in their cries and imitation there is no intention of seeming other than what they are.

There is only one being on earth that lies. That is man. He alone is able to assume a face, a manner of speech, by means of which he seems the opposite of what he really is.

The beast of prey is able only to hide, lying in wait for its prey, but it is not able to soften its face, lower its eyelid, change its voice and smile, take on the look of a lamb. But man is able. Why? Because man has learned to master himself, and as a result animal cunning becomes a lie in him.

*Keterer.*

2

Your father is the devil, and you want to carry out the lusts of your father; he was a murderer from the beginning and did not stand in the truth, for there is no truth in him; when he speaks a lie, he speaks what is his own, for he is a liar and the father of lying.

*John 8:44.*

3

A lie is terrible in that it hides the spiritual being that is one in all, and therefore prevents the possibility of union. With a lying person there can be no relations except animal ones.

4

A lie attains only the nearest and most insignificant aims and always harms the most important, eternal tasks of man.

5

Lying is as natural to man as cunning is to an animal. Lying can be unconscious, innocent, in children and simple-minded people. But the more reason develops, the more unnatural and criminal lying becomes. The greater a person's mind and education, the more harmful his lying is to him and to others.

6

As long as the spiritual man is joined with his body, lying is inherent in him. Only a completely holy and dying person can be entirely free from lying. The perfecting of a person can be measured by his liberation from lying.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 454 -->

7

There are five most common kinds of temptations:

1) The temptation of preparation (personal). The lie of this temptation lies in this, that a person withdraws from life in the present—the one real life—and transfers it to the future, whereas the future does not belong to man.

2) The temptation of work. The lie of this temptation lies in this, that every human work may turn out to be useless, may be interrupted and not be finished. But the work of God, performed by a person, the fulfillment of the will of God, can never be useless and can be interrupted by nothing.

3) The family temptation. The lie of this temptation lies in this, that the animal feeling that draws one toward continuing the race, which is lawful only to the extent that it does not violate love for people, is taken for a virtue that justifies sin.

4) The temptation of comradeship. The lie of this temptation is that, entering into comradeship with one small number of people, people separate themselves from the natural comradeship of all people and therefore violate the most important natural duties in the name of artificial ones.

5) The state temptation. The lie of this temptation lies in this, that a person, supposedly in the name of the good of the people, may renounce the demands of his conscience and of his moral freedom.

8

*Let us say what we feel, let us feel what we say; let our words agree with our life.*

*Seneca.*

A person is often unable to free himself from the social lie in which he takes involuntary part. But a person is always able not to invent lies for the benefit of his animal being.

## X SILENCE

People learn how to speak, but the chief science is how and when to be silent.

1

*If you very much want to speak, then be sure to keep silent awhile, and best of all do not say at all what you so much want to say.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 455 -->

2

If the tongue is good, then there is nothing better than it; if it is evil—there is nothing viler than it.

*The Talmud.*

I spent my whole life among the wise and found nothing better for a man than silence.

He who speaks much will not escape sin.

If a word is worth one coin, then silence is worth two.

If silence befits the intelligent, then all the more the foolish.

*The Talmud.*

4

Speak only of what is as clear to you as the morning; otherwise be silent.

*The Talmud.*

5

A wise man is acknowledged as fully enlightened for one word he has spoken, and likewise for one word spoken by him inadvertently he is acknowledged as knowing nothing. And therefore a wise man must be very careful in his words.

*Chinese wisdom.*

6

*Accustom your tongue to the words: “I do not know.”*

*Eastern wisdom.*

7

How often silence is the wisest of answers. Give more rest to your tongue than to your hands.

8

Silence is the best answer to the ignorant. Turn your tongue over seven times before you begin to speak. One must either be silent or say things that are better than silence.

9

To what can the tongue in a man's mouth be compared? It is the key to the treasury of wisdom: when the door is locked, no one can know <!-- vol. 42, p. 456 --> what is there, whether precious stones or worthless wares. Although, by the teaching of the wise, silence is useful, free speech is still more needed in its time. Two things show poor reasoning: to be silent when one should speak, and to speak when one ought to be silent.

*The Persian Saadi.*

Rarely, if ever, will you repent of having kept silent, but how many times will you repent of what you have said, and you would repent still more often if you knew all the consequences of your word.

## XI THE PRESENT

True life, life in the present, cannot have a goal in the future: the goal of true life is in fulfilling the will of God, which, outside of time, was, is, and will be.

1

Neither you nor I have any business with what may happen there, somewhere far away, or someday in the course of time, but we ought always to respond keenly to everything that is happening here and now.

*John Ruskin.*

2

You will never bring back lost time, you will never correct evil that has been done.

*John Ruskin.*

3

Do good, living and dying.

*John Ruskin.*

4

In dying one can do as much good as in the middle of life, and even more.

5

For no reasonable man can the shortness of life be a reason for uselessly wasting the share of it that has been given to him. Neither our days nor our lives can be <!-- vol. 42, p. 457 --> noble and holy if we spend them doing nothing. The best morning prayer is the one in which we ask that not one moment of this day should pass uselessly, and the best thanksgiving before dinner consists in the consciousness that we have honestly earned our food.

*John Ruskin.*

6

How, then, can one not think about the future? Not adjust one's actions to an expected supposition? Working for others, adjust yourself to the future. For yourself, live in the present alone.

7

As soon as you have gone off into the past or the future, you have gone away from God, and because of this one is at once lonely, orphaned, not free.

8

There is no future: it is made by us.

9

The present. We never care about the present. The future seems too slow to us, and we try to bring it nearer to ourselves, or we call back the past in order to hold it: we are so unreasonable that we wander in times that do not belong to us and do not think about the one that is ours, and so frivolous that we think about those times that are no more and, without caring about it, let slip the one that is.

*Pascal.*

There is no situation in which a person would not feel himself free and a creator of life, if only he transfers his whole consciousness into the present.

## XII STUPEFACTION

There is nothing in a person more precious, more necessary to him—than the light of reason. What an astonishing thing it is, then, that people, besides all those passions that act upon the soul, weakening the light <!-- vol. 42, p. 458 --> of reason, also use means of acting directly on the brain, the organ of reason, in order to weaken its light.

1

Drunkenness, smoking, every deliberate stupefaction—this is fear of one's own conscience. The more sensitive a person's conscience, or the worse his life, the more he needs to stifle it with something stupefying.

That is exactly why the most conscientious and the most depraved people give themselves up most of all to self-stupefaction.

2

When preparing to commit a crime, people usually get drunk.

And people who live criminally get drunk in advance so as not to be conscious of the wrong, for their life as well.

3

What an urgent need to stifle their conscience there must be in people who cannot refrain for even the shortest time from poisoning the pure air with tobacco filth, be it the air of a May morning, and in spite of the presence of children and of people who have an aversion to tobacco smoke.

4

*A person who has stopped drinking and smoking acquires that mental clarity and calmness of view which lights up all the phenomena of life for him from a new, true side.*

5

Drunkenness is voluntary madness.

*Seneca.*

That it is very hard to stop drinking wine and smoking is a false notion, a suggestion to which one must not yield.

One should not think that special conditions are necessary for freeing oneself from a useless and harmful habit; one should think, on the contrary, that it is very easy to stop doing what is stupid and harmful.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 459 -->

## XIIa STUPEFACTION SUPPLEMENT

One can love only God, and therefore one can and should serve only him.

1

One's own will cannot be satisfied, even if it should receive everything it desires; but one has only to renounce it, and at once one feels satisfied.

The true and only virtue consists in hating oneself—for man is worthy of hatred for his lust—and in seeking a being truly worthy of love, in order to love it. But since we cannot love what is outside us, we must love a being that is in us but that is not what we ourselves are. And such is only the all-embracing being. The kingdom of God is in us; the universal good is in us, but it is not we.

*Pascal.*

2

For us one thing is important—to know what God wants of us. And this is expressed quite clearly in the Gospel, and therefore I consider that my task is to learn to fulfill all that is so clearly commanded me from without by words and from within by conscience, to direct all my powers to this, firmly knowing that if I devote my powers to fulfilling the will of the master, he will not abandon me, and with me—with my soul—there will be the very thing that should be.

## XIII THE POWER OF THOUGHT

Great thoughts, those that move mankind, bringing it nearer to the good, do not need the praise of people.

There is power in them, and therefore they move people whether they want this or do not want it.

1

One great thought breathed into a person's soul can transform him. The idea of freedom—in ancient and new religions, and the idea of divine truth—in religious sects: how easily they have triumphed over worldly advantages.

How many heroes and martyrs they have given. Great ideas are mightier than passions.

*Channing.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 460 -->

2

One often sees people condemning and reviling in words the very thought that is changing their life and giving them the good.

3

Not only does truth itself give certainty, but the mere seeking of it gives peace.

*Pascal.*

4

Thinkers can be divided into those who think *for themselves* and those who think *for others:* the latter are the rule, the former the exception. It is the former who are independent thinkers in the double sense of the word, and egoists in the noblest sense: only from them does the world receive instruction for itself. For only the light that someone has lit for himself shines for others as well.

*Schopenhauer.*

5

Atheists ought to say things that are perfectly clear, and yet only a person perfectly devoid of common sense can say that it is perfectly clear that the soul is mortal.

Atheism is a sign of strength of mind, but only up to a certain point.

*Pascal.*

6

The vastness of the circle of thoughts is always to the detriment of their depth, and abundance is in inverse proportion—to sincerity.

7

The last step of reason lies in recognizing that there is an infinite number of things that surpass it. Reason is very weak if it does not reach this. One must know how to doubt where necessary, to affirm where necessary, and, where necessary, to submit. And he who does not act so does not know the power of reason. There are people who sin against these three rules. Some affirm everything as proven, not knowing what can be considered proof; others doubt everything, not knowing in what one must submit; still others submit to everything, not knowing where one must have one's own judgment.

*Pascal.*

## XIV SERVICE

Nothing so lightens people's life as the recognition that their calling is in the service of God.

1

Remain simple, kind, pure, truthful, God-fearing, just, courageous, merciful, and be zealous in your duties. Try to act in everything in accordance with the precepts of reason and conscience; care for the good of everyone. Life is short. Do not let slip its most precious fruit—good deeds for the good of people.

*Marcus Aurelius.*

2

Which of you, having a slave plowing or herding, will say to him on his return from the field: go quickly, sit down at the table.

On the contrary, will he not say to him: prepare me supper, and gird yourself and serve me while I eat and drink; and afterward eat and drink yourself.

Will he thank this slave for having carried out the order—I think not.

So you too, when you have done all that you were commanded, say: we are worthless slaves; because we have done what we had to do.

*Luke 17:7–10.*

3

The son of man came not in order to be served, but to serve and to give his soul for the redemption of many.

*Matthew 20:28.*

4

The prophet said: care only for what is good for all, and do not think about the evil that people have done you: forgive others willingly and do good to all; but avoid the company of the ignorant, the self-assured, and the quarrelsome.

Blessed is the life of those who have conquered anger and forgiven their adversaries. Repay evil with good.

Let us be like the trees that give their fruits to those who throw stones at them.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 462 -->

5

*God has declared to you what is good and what the Lord requires of you. Be just, merciful, and walk humbly before God.*

*The prophet Micah.*

6

A sage was asked: “How should one serve the invisible spirits?” The sage said: “When we are not yet able to serve people, how then can we serve invisible spirits?”

He was also asked: “What is death?” He said: “When we do not yet know what life is, how then can we know what death is?”

*Chinese wisdom.*

7

Without resolve in our heart to do good as long as your right hand has not grown numb, and to do it regardless of whether it leads you to life or to death, no life worthy of the name is possible!

*John Ruskin.*

8

No one can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be zealous for the one and not care about the other. You cannot serve God and mammon.

*Matthew 6:24.*

9

One cannot care at the same time both for one's soul and for worldly goods. If you want worldly goods—give up your soul; if you want to keep your soul safe—renounce worldly goods. Otherwise you will be constantly divided in two and will get neither the one nor the other.

When you are alarmed or upset by something worldly, remember that you will have to die, and then what earlier seemed to you an important misfortune and agitated you will become in your eyes a trifling unpleasantness not even worth worrying about.

*Epictetus.*

Only recognize yourself not as master but as servant, and at once seeking, anxiety, and dissatisfaction are replaced by certainty, calm, peace, and unbroken joy.

## XV HUMILITY

Pride is the opposite end of humility. But even before it reaches pride, the absence of humility deprives a person of spiritual joys, rewarding him for this deprivation with nothing but disappointment.

1

When you are invited by anyone to a wedding, do not sit down in the first place, so that there may not happen to be among those invited by him someone more honored than you.

And he who invited you and him, coming up, would not say to you: give up your place to him; and then with shame you will have to take the last place.

But when you are invited, on coming, sit down in the last place, so that he who invited you, coming up, will say: friend! move up higher; then you will have honor before those sitting with you.

For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled: and he who humbles himself—will be exalted.

*Luke 14:8–11.*

2

A person standing on tiptoe cannot stand for long. A person who puts himself on display cannot shine. Whoever is pleased with himself cannot become renowned. Whoever boasts cannot have merit. Whoever is proud cannot rise high. Before the judgment of reason such people are like scraps of food and arouse everyone's disgust. Therefore he who has reason does not rely on himself.

*Laozi.*

3

A person who seeks wisdom is by that very fact already wise; he who imagines that he has found it—is a fool.

*Eastern wisdom.*

4

To know much and not put oneself on display as knowing is moral height. To know little and put oneself on display as knowing—is a sickness. Only by understanding this sickness can we be rid of it.

*Laozi.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 464 -->

5

A sage was asked: “What should one do to increase one's virtues, correct oneself of one's faults, and be able to see the errors of the mind?”

The sage said: “This is an excellent question. In order to increase virtue in oneself, one must put above everything one's duty to do what one should, and not think about the benefit that will come of it. In order to correct oneself of one's faults, one must not think about correcting the faults of others. In order to be able to see the errors of the mind, one must be modest and not trust oneself.

*Chinese wisdom.*

*One cannot be humble on purpose. To be humble there is one means: not to think about oneself, but to think about serving God and people.*

## XVI TEMPTATIONS

Every comparison of oneself with others, for the sake of justifying oneself, is a temptation that hinders both a good life and its chief work—perfecting.

1

It is dangerous to compare oneself with others in order to acknowledge one's own superiority. Involuntarily you will condemn others, exalt yourself, and acknowledge evil as good.

2

*We are most dissatisfied with others when we are dissatisfied with ourselves. The consciousness of our bad deeds irritates us, and our heart in its cunning attacks what is outside it in order to drown out what it feels.*

*Amiel.*

3

It is impossible for a person to look so deeply into his own heart that he could be fully sure of the purity of his moral intention and of the blamelessness of his way of thinking even with regard to any single action of his, even if he had no doubt about the lawfulness of that action. Often the weakness <!-- vol. 42, p. 465 --> that keeps a person from venturing on a crime is taken by him for virtue, which creates the notion of strength. Very many people live a long, blameless life, and yet they are only people fortunate in having escaped so many temptations. How much moral content there was in their way of thinking when they performed each action—this remains hidden even from them.

*Kant.*

4

People are inclined to believe the external signs of condemnation and exaltation. It is hard for us to recognize in a man under guard, in prison and in convict's clothing, a being as worthy of respect as a man in rich clothing riding in a rich carriage. And yet we could judge their worth only if we knew their inner motives.

Compare yourself only with the highest perfection that you can imagine, and not with people who seem to you lower than you.

## XVII JUSTICE

*Every person knows what is just and what is unjust, just as he knows whether it is level or uneven under his feet.*

1

Every person possesses not only an ideal of justice but also the capacity to be just.

Justice is revealed to us not as some distant, great good that we must revere from afar; but it is revealed to us together with the consciousness that justice is inseparably bound up with our life, that we must put it into practice.

*Channing.*

2

The most natural demand of the soul is justice, and we are least of all inclined to fulfill it. Demand of us anything you like, only not justice. “But mercy,” you will say, “is exalted above judgment.” Yes, it is greater than justice, it is its summit, it is a temple whose foundation is <!-- vol. 42, p. 466 --> justice. But you cannot reach the summit without beginning from the foundation. You can base your deeds not on mercy, but only on justice, for the simple reason that there is no mercy without justice. It is the final reward for a good deed.

*John Ruskin.*

3

It has been ordained by the creator himself that the measure of all human actions should be not advantage but justice, and, by virtue of this, all efforts to determine the degree of advantage are always fruitless. No man has ever known, knows, or can know what the final results of a given action or of a whole series of actions will be, both for himself and for other people. But every man can know which action is just and which is not. And we can all likewise know that the consequences of justice will, in the end, be the best, both for others and for us, though we are not able to say beforehand what this best will be and in what it will consist.

*John Ruskin.*

4

Madmen think that there is no real justice, but only accidental justice, because they often do not notice retribution. But if justice remains unpunished for years and even centuries, the hour of retribution will still come as surely as a man cannot escape death. God, as in the beginning, lives and speaks with us in the affairs of the universe. The great world soul is just. O brothers, must you really, after so many centuries of Christian preaching, still be reminded of *what* various pagans, Muslims, Romans, Jews, Scythians, and Greeks once understood. Reminded that justice exists on earth, and that in essence there is nothing eternal except it.

*Carlyle.*

Not acknowledging the injustice of one's actions requires lying.

## XVIII THE ARRANGEMENT OF LIFE

The chief cause of a bad social arrangement is the division of people into masters and workers, into the enlightened and the unenlightened, into the clean and the dirty, into the dark, the black, and the white.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 467 -->

1

The sovereignty of the people comes down, in essence, to whether it is possible for anyone originally to have the right to command a people against its will. It is incomprehensible how this can be asserted in a reasonable way. A people, it goes without saying, has supreme and sole power over itself; but it is a sovereign who is a minor and therefore must remain under guardianship, and it can never itself dispose of its rights without giving rise to boundless dangers, all the more so since, like all minors, it can very easily become the plaything of cunning rogues, who are therefore called demagogues.

*Schopenhauer.*

2

Have you seen how a herd of swine rushes to a bucket of slops? That is human society as it is at the present time.

Have you seen a company of well-bred people, men and women, sitting together at dinner, when no one crowds another, is greedy, or gorges himself, and when each, knowing that his hunger will be satisfied, tries to give way to others, to help them? That is human society as it could be.

*Henry George.*

3

Under existing conditions, with land considered private property, material progress develops two different tendencies, two opposite currents. On the one hand, the increasing population and the improvement in methods of production bring about the growth of enormous fortunes, the destruction of the middle, intermediate class, and the reduction of the working mass to the level of lower wages and heavier dependence. On the other hand, by bringing people into closer contact, stimulating thought, and calling forth new desires and hopes, modern progress tends to make the masses of the people dissatisfied with their position, able to feel injustice keenly. What will come of this can be predicted with the same accuracy as one can predict what will come if two trains rush toward each other on the same rails.

*Henry George.*

4

Together with steam, electricity, and a multitude of inventions of various kinds, new mighty forces have entered the world. With right <!-- vol. 42, p. 468 --> use they can be our servants, more dutiful than the genies of the Arabian tales. With wrong use they can turn into monsters of destruction. They demand, and urgently demand, great social changes. Manifesting themselves under social institutions founded on natural justice and recognizing the equal right of all people to the riches of nature, they will raise all equally, and the industrial organization of people will naturally be transformed into a vast cooperative society. Manifesting themselves under social conditions that deny natural justice by recognizing land as private property, these forces will produce inequality and will lead, in the end, only to destruction and devastation.

*Henry George.*

5

We gentlemen live by the most refined plunder. We keep peasants who work, growing stupefied with work, so that we may be fed for nothing and that feelings and thoughts may be our possession alone.

*John Ruskin.*

6

At public festivities, masts are set up for people to climb and get prizes. Such a method of entertainment—to torment a person for hours (let him ruin his health), or a race in sacks, and we will amuse ourselves watching—could have arisen only under the division of people into masters and slaves.

All the forms of our life have taken the shape they have taken only because there was this division: acrobats, tavern waiters, privies, the manufacture of mirrors, of cards, all factories—all of it could arise in the form it has only because there was the division into masters and slaves.

*We want to set up a brotherly life while keeping the slave forms of life.*

Brotherhood is natural, proper to people. Not brotherhood but division—that is what is diligently cultivated.

For the inequality of people those who acknowledge themselves to be a higher breed of people are as much to blame as those who acknowledge themselves to be people not equal to all others, but lower.

## XIX HOLY FOOLISHNESS

You can never know about yourself whether you are acting for God, for truth, or for human glory. The one means of finding out is to do what is good joyfully, in spite of the condemnation of people.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 469 -->

1

When the world reviles and slanders us, our business is not to be grieved by this, but rather to think: is there not some ground or truth in it.

2

No one shows such respect for and devotion to virtue as he who loses the reputation of a good man only in order to remain one in his own consciousness.

*Seneca.*

It is good to serve people as they wish it, but it is better still to serve them without their approval and reward.

*Conway.*

4

It would be most useful of all to compile a description of the life of those people whom the world neither thought of nor heard of, but who now carry out the chief share of all its labors and from whom we can best learn how to carry them out.

5

What can be greater than to join those people who tear the chains from human freedom and thought, who widen the field of vision of mankind?.. It is good to serve mankind as it wishes, but far greater is to serve it in ways that are neither popular nor bring reward.

*Condemnation for doing good is the only test of the sincerity of one's service to good.*
