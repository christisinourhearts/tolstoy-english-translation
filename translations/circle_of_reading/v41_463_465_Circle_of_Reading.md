<!-- vol. 41, p. 463 -->

2 July

In no field is there such abuse of words as in the appraisal of works of—especially supposed—art.

1

> We are fully satisfied with the impression of a work of art only when it leaves behind it something that we, for all the effort of thought, cannot bring to complete clarity.

> *Schopenhauer.*

2

> Art is such an action upon people that in their souls the mysterious becomes evident, the vague becomes clear, the complex—simple, the accidental—necessary. The true artist always simplifies.

> *After Amiel.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 464 -->

3

> If the whole world, as representation, is only appearance, then art is the clarification of this appearance, a camera obscura that shows objects more purely and allows one to survey and grasp them better. Art is a spectacle within a spectacle, a stage upon the stage, as in “Hamlet.”

> *Schopenhauer.*

4

> A man endowed with ordinary senses conforms his thoughts to things; the artist conforms things to his thoughts. The ordinary man considers nature invariably fixed and firm; the artist—fluid, shimmering, and he puts the stamp of his own being on it. For him the unruly world is submissive and pliant; he clothes dust and stones with human qualities and turns them into expressions of reason.

> *Emerson.*

5

> *Remember that nothing beautiful can be done out of rivalry, nothing noble out of pride.*

> *John Ruskin.*

6

Sciences and arts can be needed by the people only when those who live among the people and as the people live, claiming no rights, offer them their scientific and artistic services, which it will depend on the will of the people to accept or not to accept.

7

There are two indubitable signs of true science and true art: the first, internal—that the servant of science and art fulfills his calling not for gain but with self-renunciation; and the second, external—that his work is understandable to all people.

8

Science and art are as closely bound up with each other as the lungs and the heart, so that if one organ is perverted, the other cannot act properly either.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 465 --> True science studies and brings into people's consciousness those truths of knowledge that the people of a given time and society consider the most important. Art, for its part, carries these truths over from the realm of knowledge into the realm of feeling.

The practice of art, though not so high a matter as those who practice it usually consider it, is nevertheless a matter that is not useless, and a good one, if it unites people and evokes good feelings in them; but the practice of such art as is approved by the rich classes of our world—art that divides people and evokes ill feelings in them—is a matter not only useless but harmful.
