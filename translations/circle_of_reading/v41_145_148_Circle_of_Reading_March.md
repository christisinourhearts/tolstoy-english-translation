> POOR PEOPLE

> In a fisherman's hut Jeanne, the fisherman's wife, sits by the fire and mends an old sail. Outside the wind whistles and howls and, splashing and breaking against the shore, the waves boom... Outside it is dark and cold, there is a storm at sea, but in the fisherman's hut it is warm and snug. The earthen floor is swept clean; the fire in the stove has not yet gone out; the dishes gleam on the shelf. On the bed with its white curtain let down, five children sleep to the howling of the stormy sea. The fisherman husband went out to sea in his boat in the morning and has not come back yet. The fisherman's wife hears the booming of the waves and the roar of the wind. Jeanne is frightened.

> The old wooden clock with its hoarse chime struck ten, eleven... Still her husband is not back. Jeanne falls to thinking. Her husband does not spare himself; in cold and storm he catches fish. She sits at work from morning to evening. And what comes of it? They barely feed themselves. And the little ones still have no shoes: summer and winter they run barefoot; and the bread they eat is not wheat—it is good even that there is enough rye. The only <!-- vol. 41, p. 146 --> relish to their food is fish. “Well, thank God, the children are healthy. There's nothing to complain about,” thinks Jeanne, and again listens to the storm. “Where is he now? Keep him, Lord, save and have mercy!” she says, and crosses herself.

> It is still too early to sleep. Jeanne gets up, throws a thick shawl over her head, lights the lantern, and goes outside to see whether the sea has grown quieter, whether day is breaking, and whether the lamp is burning on the lighthouse, and whether her husband's boat can be seen. But nothing can be seen on the sea. The wind tears the shawl from her and bangs something torn loose against the door of the neighboring hut, and Jeanne remembers that since the evening she had been meaning to drop in and see how her sick neighbor was. “There's no one even to look after her,” thought Jeanne, and knocked at the door. She listened... No one answers.

> “A widow's lot is a bad one,” thinks Jeanne, standing at the threshold. “Though there aren't many children—two—still she has to think everything out alone. And now illness besides! Ah, a widow's lot is a bad one. I'll go in and see how she is.”

> Jeanne knocked again and again. No one answered.

> — Hey, neighbor! — Jeanne called out. “Could something have happened?” she thought, and pushed the door.

> Inside the hut it was damp and cold. Jeanne raised the lantern to see where the sick woman was. And the first thing that met her eyes was the bed right opposite the door, and on the bed she, the neighbor, lies on her back as quietly and motionlessly as only the dead lie. Jeanne brought the lantern still closer. Yes, it is she. The head is thrown back; on the cold, bluish face is the calm of death. A pale, dead hand, as if it had reached out for something, had fallen and hung down from the straw. And right there, not far from the dead mother, two little children, curly-haired and plump-cheeked, covered with an old dress, are sleeping, curled up and pressed close to each other with their fair little heads. Evidently the mother, dying, had still managed to wrap their little feet in an old shawl and cover them with her dress. Their breathing is even and calm. They are sleeping sweetly and soundly.

> Jeanne takes up the little cradle with the children and, wrapping them in the shawl, carries them home. Her heart is beating hard; she herself does not know how or why she did this, but she knows that she could not help doing what she did.

> At home she lays the children, who have not woken, on the bed with her own children and hurriedly draws the curtain. She is pale and agitated. It is as if her conscience were tormenting her. “What will he say?..” she says <!-- vol. 41, p. 147 --> to herself. “It's no joke, five little ones of our own—as if he hadn't enough worry with them already... Is that him?.. No, not yet!.. And why did I have to take them!.. He'll beat me! And serve me right, I deserve it. There he is! No!.. Well, so much the better!..”

> The door creaked, as if someone had come in. Jeanne started and half rose from her chair.

> “No. Again no one! Lord, and why did I do it?.. How will I look him in the eye now?..” And Jeanne falls to thinking and sits silent by the bed for a long time.

> The rain stopped; day broke, but the wind booms and the sea roars as before.

> Suddenly the door flew open, a stream of fresh sea air burst into the room, and a tall, swarthy fisherman, dragging wet, torn nets behind him, comes into the house with the words:

> — Here I am, Jeanne!

> — Ah, it's you! — says Jeanne, and stops, not daring to raise her eyes to him.

> — Well, what a night! Terrible!

> — Yes, yes, the weather was awful! Well, and how was the fishing?

> — Rotten, completely rotten! I caught nothing. Only tore the nets. Bad, bad!.. Yes, I tell you, that was some weather! I don't think I can remember such a night. What fishing could there be! Thank God I got home alive... Well, and what were you doing here without me?

> The fisherman dragged the nets into the room and sat down by the stove.

> — Me? — said Jeanne, turning pale. — Why, what would I be doing... I sat sewing... The wind howled so that it got frightening. I was afraid for you.

> — Yes, yes, — muttered her husband, — the weather's devilish bad! But what can you do!

> Both were silent for a while.

> — And do you know, — said Jeanne, — our neighbor Simon has died.

> — Really?

> — And I don't know when; probably yesterday already. Yes, it was hard for her to die. And how her heart must have ached for the children! Two children, after all—tiny ones... One doesn't talk yet, and the other is just barely beginning to crawl...

> Jeanne fell silent. The fisherman frowned; his face became serious, worried.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 148 --> — Well, here's a business! — he said, scratching the back of his head. — Well, but what's to be done! We'll have to take them, or else they'll wake up, and how will it be for them with the dead woman? Well, never mind, we'll get by somehow! Go on, quickly!

> But Jeanne did not stir from her place.

> — Well, what is it? Don't you want to? What's wrong with you, Jeanne?

> — Here they are, — said Jeanne, and drew back the curtain.

> *Victor Hugo. Retold by L. N. Tolstoy.*
