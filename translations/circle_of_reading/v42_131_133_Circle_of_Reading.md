<!-- vol. 42, p. 131 -->

8 October

Only people who have never thought about the chief and essential questions of life can think and say that everything is accessible to human reason.

1

People are of three kinds: some do not believe in anything that cannot be expressed in clear words; others believe only in those teachings in which they have been brought up; the third believe in that law which they are conscious of in their heart. Such people are the most reasonable and the most steadfast. Those people who believe in the teachings in which they were born are less reasonable and less steadfast, but all the same they are not deprived of the chief human property: to acknowledge something higher, incomprehensible, demanding of us a good life. A peasant woman calling upon God, even upon Nicholas the Wonderworker, as something higher, spiritual, demanding self-renunciation and good deeds, is nearer to the truth than people of the first kind, who acknowledge nothing that is incomprehensible to reasoning.

2

> All beginnings are mysteries; the cause of every individual or collective life is a mystery, that is, something that does not yield to reason, inexplicable, indefinite. In a word, every individuality is an insoluble riddle, and no beginning can be explained. Indeed, everything that has come to be is explained by the past, but the beginning of anything whatever <!-- vol. 42, p. 132 --> has not come to be. It always presents the original miracle of creation, because it is not the consequence of anything else: it only appears among earlier things, which make up its medium, occasion, setting, which accompany its appearance. But the appearance itself remains incomprehensible.

> *Amiel.*

3

> Thanks to your excellent upbringing, you can measure circles and squares and all the distances between the stars. Everything has become attainable for your geometry. If you are such a fine mechanic, then measure the human mind. Tell me how great it is or how small. You know what a straight line is. What use is that to you, if you do not know the straight way in life. All the liberal sciences prove incapable of teaching virtue. Even if they are fit for something else, for virtue they are worth nothing. They do not lead the mind to virtue: they only clear the way.

> *Seneca.*

4

> The mystery of a plant's life is the same as the mystery of our life, and the physiologist hopes in vain to explain it by a mechanical law, just as he explains a machine that he himself has made. We must not feel with our fingers the holy of holies of animal or even vegetable life. Doing this, we will discover nothing but the exterior.

> *Thoreau.*

5

> A thing that we see through a microscope and a telescope becomes insignificant.

> *Thoreau.*

6

> A large library distracts the reader rather than instructs him. It is much better to confine oneself to a few authors than to read many without thought.

> *Seneca.*

*It is better not to know much of what can be known than to try to know what cannot be known.*

*Nothing so corrupts and weakens the power of the mind, and nothing so excites self-conceit, as hovering in the realms of the unknowable. Worst of all is to pretend that you understand what you do not understand.*
