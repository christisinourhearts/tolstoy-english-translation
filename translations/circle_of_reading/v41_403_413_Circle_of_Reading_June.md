> FIRST SORROW

> When Grisha went out onto the balcony, he had only to narrow his big blue eyes to see, through the open gates of the stable, the round, light-colored rump of Lovky in his stall, the row of bridles on the partition, and the coachman Ignat in his old sleeveless jacket, with his never-extinguished pipe in his teeth. Usually Grisha did not resist the temptation for long: he would thrust both hands into the pockets of his short little trousers, go down the balcony steps, and walk across the big overgrown yard straight to the stable.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 404 --> — Well, how is it? — he would ask Ignat, looking around the coach house, its surroundings so familiar and dear to him. — Is the left one still lame?

> — Still lame, still lame! — Ignat would answer, fully ready to keep the conversation going.

> — And did you mend the collar?

> — I'm mending it now.

> — Mind: today my Korolyok isn't to be given to anyone!

> — Is it up to me? They'll say: “We have to go to the station or to the village, harness Korolyok”... And I'll harness him.

> — What is this, really! Always my horse, always mine... — the boy would remark grumblingly. — And did you pour it some oats?

> — Where am I to get them, if I've not been told to? — Ignat would answer, and his bearded, usually sullen face would take on a sly expression. — Your papa didn't order it.

> — Without oats! — Grisha would cry out in despair, and angry tears would well up in his eyes.

> Ignat would laugh merrily and kindly.

> — Just look, what gunpowder! Gunpowder, really, — he would say soothingly. — But you can rest easy: I won't wrong your Korolyok. I'll take from the others, but with me Korolyok is always well content.

> He would look kindly into the boy's eyes and pass his gnarled, rough hand over his head. Grisha would calm down and begin his usual round. He would sit in each of the carriages in turn, climb up onto the box, and make his remarks as he went.

> — A fi-ine little cart! — he would say in the tone of a connoisseur.

> — There's nothing bad about it! — Ignat would respond sympathetically.

> — And sturdy?

> — You'll smear yourself with tar, you scamp! — the coachman would warn. — Nanny will scold.

> Ignat was in his first year of service on the estate, but he had very quickly become close to the little master, and a strange but sincere friendship had sprung up between them.

> — Now when I lived with the Lukhovsky masters, — Ignat would begin, — they had a horse...

> — Did you live with them before us?

> — No. Before you I lived here with a certain merchant... Need, of course... If it weren't for need I wouldn't have lasted a day with him!.. And to court, too!.. And what would they take me to court for? Did I take what wasn't mine?

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 405 --> — Did the merchant really want to have you tried?

> — Want to, nothing! He went straight ahead, I mean, and filed a complaint. Saying I made off with his horse and cart. He hadn't paid wages for a whole year, and he won't let you go either. Stay and live! My woman and I tried this way and that. He takes advantage, I mean, that there was no passport. What are you going to do? So Matryona, my woman, and I hitched the horse to the cart at night and... home. We weren't going to walk, and with a small child too; it's a good sixty versts home. The merchant missed us, and our trail had gone cold. I'd have returned the horse to him. Would I really have kept it? But he, you see, flew into a rage because his free workman had left, and off to court, and a complaint: this and that, he says, I've been robbed.

> — And did they try you?

> — They say they did.

> — Well, and how was it?

> — Why, just like that! — Ignat would answer vaguely, and his thick brows would knit in worry, and his whole face would take on for a long while a gloomy, almost suffering expression.

> — You should have said you weren't guilty, — Grisha would advise seriously.

> — But did anyone ask me? What sort of courts do we have? Where is it, my little falcon, the truth? They tried and they tried, and made me a thief. That's how!

> — How did they make you one? — the boy would question eagerly.

> — Just like that! — Ignat would answer, frowning and smiling bitterly.

> Sometimes the conversation took another direction.

> — Is Matryona really your wife? — Grisha would ask.

> — Whose else, then! — Ignat would respond good-naturedly.

> — Then why isn't she with you, but always baking bread in the dugout?

> Ignat would smile.

> — And what should she be doing here with me? Telling me tales, or what?

> — Why tales? — the boy would object hotly. — Mama doesn't tell Papa tales, she just lives... And Polka, then, is your daughter?

> — A daughter, then.

> — And did you have other children?

> — No, that's all there is.

> — Why didn't you have more?

> Ignat would laugh and shake his head.

> — Well, what a child! — he would say.

> — Why are you laughing? — Grisha would go on, a little offended, and explaining his <!-- vol. 41, p. 406 --> thought. — Now Papa and Mama have three children... Ignat! — he would ask tenderly right then, looking into his friend's eyes, — when we go away to town, you take good care of my Korolyok.

> — I'll take care of him! I'll take care of him! — Ignat would promise. — Only, my dear, I might have to go away before you do.

> — Go where? — the boy would ask in surprise.

> — Why... there! — Ignat would answer in his usual mysterious manner.

> Often the friends' heart-to-heart talk was interrupted by the old nanny.

> — Grishenka! Here, are you? — she would ask, looking into the shed. — And what is this, really, — she would go on grumblingly, — a gentleman's child, and he just about lives in the stable. I'll complain to your mama! Would you believe it: he's found himself a friend. Come right now, come! And you, you good-for-nothing, — she would turn to Ignat, — instead of bringing the child to his senses, you lure him on all the more.

> — But what have I done, Anna Gerasimovna? I'm doing nothing, — Ignat would excuse himself, abashed. — If I were teaching him anything bad...

> — That's all we need, you for a teacher! — the nanny would remark contemptuously. — Go, you scamp, go!

> Grisha saw his father and mother mostly only at table. His father was always busy; his mother sat whole days in her bedroom and was considered unwell. When her head did not ache, something else ached that did not allow her to bear the noisy company of children or even the bright light of day. When it came into Grisha's head to run in to her, she would caress him, kiss him impulsively countless times, and at once ask him to go away and not disturb her.

> Sometimes Grisha resisted.

> — Mama, — he would say, — I'll sit quietly, very quietly.

> He would sit down in an armchair and fold his hands on his knees.

> — Are you well? — his mother would ask anxiously.

> — Yes, — he would answer absently, occupied with some extraneous thought, and at once go on to the question that interested him.

> He spoke in a whisper so as not to disturb the general mood of quiet and calm.

> — Mama, — he would whisper, — why is it that when it's hot, you're sure to sweat?

> — Are you hot, then? — his mother would ask.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 407 --> — Hot... And you think I'm in two shirts?

> — Only one, then?

> — Of course, in one! There! — Grisha would cry out ringingly, and, unbuttoning the collar of his calico side-buttoned shirt, he would show his bare chest.

> His mother would wince painfully.

> — Why are you shouting? — she would reproach him.

> — Oh, I forgot! — the boy would say guiltily, and fall silent. — Mama! — he would whisper again a minute later, — tell me: what is a tail for?

> — What tail?

> — Why, horses', dogs'?

> — What do you mean, what for? It's simply a tail. That's just how it's made.

> — But not simply! It's for swishing flies. What else would they swish flies with?

> The boy's chatter would begin to irritate the nervous woman, but she still endured it in silence, fully confident that Grisha himself would tire of the half-darkness of the room and go away. But Grisha would slide down the back of the armchair, lie with his back on the seat, and stick his legs up, crossing one over the other.

> — Mama! — he would say again, — and do you know where fleas breed?

> His mother would frown squeamishly and close her eyes.

> — Well, really, Grisha! What sort of talk is that!

> — In the tugs. If fleas breed there, you have to throw out the tugs and then new ones...

> — That's what comes of your always being around the stables! In the fall I'll hire you a governess. I'm ashamed of you!

> — Ashamed of what? — the boy would ask.

> — Well, all right. Well, go! Go to Nanny and your sisters. You're always either alone or with the peasants.

> Grisha would sigh deeply, reluctantly get up from the armchair, and sigh again: he did not want to leave the cool room yet, to leave his sad, sick, but still tenderly loved mama.

> — Kiss me! — his mother would say quietly.

> He would kiss her, rub his face against her face, and she would feel his sharp little shoulders under his shirt and fall into a plaintive tone:

> — You're so thin, my little one! So pale! Grisha, why are you like this?

> — I get into mischief! — the boy would answer out of habit, but his mother's compassionate tenderness worked on his nerves and made him feel sorry for himself.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 408 --> — You're my poor frail one! And it isn't easy for you either! And your little soul is often cheerless, my boy!

> And it would happen that, touched by her pity and by words he did not yet understand, Grisha would suddenly begin to sob on her shoulder.

> — What is it? What are you crying about? — his mother would question him, frightened, and touch his head to find out whether he had a fever.

> But Grisha would calm down at once and go away. And before he had reached the door he had already forgotten his causeless tears, taken up with some new and interesting thought. Something still quivered and sobbed in his chest, but he was already joyfully feeling in his pocket for a forgotten piece of string and working out what best use to make of it.

> And meanwhile the first serious sorrow was already hanging over his head.

> One morning his father, without lifting his eyes from the newspaper, said to Mama across the table:

> — Yes... do you know? They've come for Ignat!

> — They've come? Already? — Mama asked back, frightened, and, as though thinking something over, she put her unfinished cup down on the table.

> — Was there really nothing that could be done? They have children, after all, — she said quietly.

> — What would you have me do? — said the father, shrugging his shoulders. — I can't very well get mixed up with that scoundrel... Well, what's his name? With that merchant... I know him a little: a kulak and a swindler.

> — Well, there, you see, all the more reason, — said Mama.

> — All the more reason for what? He made off with a horse, and the lock was broken besides; well, that means theft with breaking in... The case is clear.

> — But what were they to do? — asked Mama. — After all, this man took advantage of some delay with the passport, didn't pay wages, extorted free work... Ignat simply ran away from slavery...

> — All the same, he shouldn't have made off with the horse! Well, that's enough; what's the use of talking about it now! — the father answered with annoyance and buried himself in the newspaper again.

> Grisha listened eagerly and understood nothing.

> — Mama, where are they taking Ignat? — he asked, opening his eyes wide.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 409 --> His mother glanced at him absently, but suddenly remembered the boy's friendship with the coachman, frowned ever so slightly, and looked away.

> — Who has come for Ignat, Mama? — Grisha went on asking.

> — Why not tell him? — the father began in a displeased tone. — What is this everlasting fear of upsetting him, of affecting his nerves? And what will come of it is some sort of wet hen, a rag, and not a man.

> — My God, then tell him yourself; am I stopping you! — Mama cried out with tears in her eyes, raised her hands to her temples, and left the table.

> — Everlasting scenes! Everlasting scenes! — the father shouted after her.

> — Your Ignat is being taken to prison for burglary. Do you understand? — he said harshly. Grisha turned pale. — Ignat for the theft, and his wife Matryona for complicity. Him for three years, and her for a year and a half.

> — And Polka? — asked Grisha.

> — And Polka... Well, what about Polka? Of course, she won't go to prison... I really don't know where to put her... Polka.

> Grisha was looking straight at his father, and his eyes were growing shiny and angry. He was turning paler and paler, but he was afraid of his father and held himself back as much as he could.

> — And what is that for? — he asked in a challenging tone.

> — He stole, I'm telling you. Or it's all the same as if he stole.

> — It's not all the same at all!.. And you said yourself that the merchant is a swindler.

> — Well, I did.

> — Then what is this? How can this be? Is this allowed?

> His father suddenly grew angry.

> — Please, please, no fuss! You've been so spoiled there's no bearing it at all.

> Holding himself back as much as he could, Grisha got up and went out of the room. But as soon as he found himself outside the door, anger and resentment against someone seemed to squeeze his throat. He ran down the corridor and burst out onto the balcony. His first thought was to see Ignat, but the gates of the stable were locked, and that meant Ignat was not there. Grisha ran to the maids' room. There Nanny was sitting at the table drinking tea, and opposite her sat a man in military uniform whom Grisha did not know. The military man, sticking out his elbow in a mannered way, was taking jam out of a jar and eating it, washing it down with tea. Grisha <!-- vol. 41, p. 410 --> at once recognized Nanny's jar and understood that Nanny was treating the military man, but he was so taken up with the unexpected news of Ignat's going away that he paid no attention to the presence of Nanny's guest.

> — Nanny, who has come for Ignat? — he asked in a trembling voice.

> Nanny did not answer at once.

> — Yes, now they'll take your dear fellow away; you won't be running away from your nanny anymore.

> — Who has come, Nanny?

> — Now he won't wriggle out of it... Who has come? Why, this is who has come.

> Grisha did not understand at once. The one who was to take Ignat and Matryona to prison he had pictured as huge, terrible, and repulsive to look at, but what was looking at him was the sunburned, good-natured face of Nanny's guest, smiling a smile that was half embarrassed, half simply foolish. Besides him and Nanny there was no one else in the room. At last Grisha understood.

> — You? — he asked in surprise and disbelief, looking straight at the military man.

> — Me, sir! — the other answered, spreading his lips in a broad grin and evidently hesitating whether he ought to stand up before the young master or go on sitting.

> — You? You... you wretch!.. I'll... I'll smash you to bits! — he shrieked, and rushed forward.

> But suddenly his face twitched, the corners of his mouth trembled, and he burst into tears, loudly and plaintively, the way helpless, grieved children cry. The constable laughed in embarrassment and looked around on all sides, spreading his hands...

> Grisha ran away to the nursery, squeezed himself into the corner by his bed, and pressed himself against the wall, holding his chest with both hands. Helpless indignation was still seething in him and seeking a way out. He saw his sister's doll on the floor, began to trample it with his feet, and finally flung it to the other end of the room. His own picture hung on the wall; he tore it down and threw it on the floor. After such strenuous activity his nervous tension eased somewhat: he sat down, leaned his forehead against the iron of his little bed, grew quiet, and began to dream... He dreamed of power...

> He needed power in order to take revenge, in order to punish all these cruel and guilty people: the judges who had condemned <!-- vol. 41, p. 411 --> Ignat; the constable who was to take him away; Nanny, for treating the constable to jam; and even his father... Grisha was indignant with his father for his evident indifference to Ignat's fate. He ought to have stood up for him, ought to have driven the constable away, but he stayed calm, read his newspapers, and even said that Ignat was “all the same as a thief.”

> Grisha wanted to take revenge on all these people who were wronging his friend so cruelly. He thought about how he would punish his father, Nanny, the constable, and, while thinking up punishments, he picked with his fingernail at the peeling paint on the iron. Suddenly he was on the alert: he seemed to hear his father's loud talk and, answering it, Ignat's timid voice. In a flash he jumped up and ran out to the maids' room. In the middle of the room, their heads hanging low, stood Ignat and Matryona, shifting from foot to foot. Beside Matryona, her nose buried in the gathers of her dress, stood Polka, and her mother looked down at her from above, and on her face there was more dull bewilderment than fear and grief. Behind them, the curious faces of the household servants peered in from behind the doors.

> — Well, all right, — Grisha's father was saying loudly, — it's too late now, and nothing can be done. Don't worry about Polka. She won't fare badly, and in life and death God alone has power. We promise to take care of her. Go with God, Ignat! What's to be done?!

> The father waved his hand, as if to make it understood that the farewell was over, but no one moved from the spot. Ignat was silent and stared dully at the floor under his feet.

> — Yes, we promise, — Mama added in a trembling voice, stretched out her hand toward Polka, but at once let it drop and turned away.

> — There's no mending the matter now! — the father began again, evidently beginning to find the mute scene of these people's despair a burden. — You'll just have to somehow... The term isn't so long; you'll live through it. What's to be done?

> Matryona quietly moved Polka aside, took a step forward, and silently dropped down at the mistress's feet, touching the floor with her forehead.

> — Matryona! — the mistress cried out, and tears at once spurted from her eyes. — Don't bow to me, Matryona! Believe me: I'll take care of your little girl... Don't bow down at my feet!

> She bent down, touched Matryona's shoulder with a trembling hand, and herself sank to the floor beside her.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 412 --> — We must bear it... Everyone must bear it! — she whispered hurriedly. — Everyone must...

> — Well, enough, enough! — the father began, not hiding his impatience. — I'm very sorry. I was pleased with you, Ignat. When you've served your term, come again. I'll take you on. And don't worry about your daughter. Go with God now!

> He took his wife by the hand and meant to lead her away with him, but she freed her hand and once more embraced Matryona tightly.

> — We must bear it! — she whispered once more.

> Matryona stood up. She passed a bewildered look around the room and stopped it on Grisha. For one moment the woman and the boy looked each other in the eyes; then Grisha timidly lowered his lashes and moved forward.

> — Goodbye! — he said very quietly and very tenderly. But Matryona went on looking at him in silence, still puzzling over something. Then Grisha went toward Ignat. He held out his hand; Ignat took it and suddenly bent down right to the child's face.

> — Polka... will you be kind to her? — he asked.

> — I will! — Grisha answered seriously and solemnly, and looked with a bold, shining gaze into the sad eyes of his friend. Ignat passed his hand over the boy's head, crossed himself devoutly toward the icon, and went to the door.

> — Matryona! — someone from the household servants called. — Matryona! Ignat's gone out. They're waiting for you, go on! The cart's at the porch.

> The young woman started; the dull expression of bewilderment gave way to fright. Beside her, her face buried in the folds of her dress as before, stood Polka, trembling all over. She slowly turned and went out.

> The boy, holding back his sobs, first at a walk, then at a run, came running into the nursery and sat down again behind the bed, looking gloomily in front of him. His father's steps were heard in the corridor. He came into the nursery and stopped in front of Grisha.

> — What are you sitting here for? Go to your nanny, — he said.

> The boy was silent and did not move from the spot.

> — Grisha! — the father shouted sternly, — am I talking to you or not?

> The child raised his head and fixed on him a serious, hostile, and intent gaze.

> — Listen, — the father began, involuntarily softening, — you seem to be angry with me? What have I got to do with it? Is it my fault? <!-- vol. 41, p. 413 --> It's I who ought to give you a good scolding: how dare you shout at the constable? Come, speak! — he shouted impatiently, feeling that his son's stubborn gaze irritated him and somehow made him ill at ease.

> — Go on... — Grisha said quietly and calmly.

> — Go on and what?

> — Go on and scold me. It's all the same to me now.

> His father was a little at a loss.

> — Well, splendid! — he said. — And now I don't even want to talk to you.

> He turned and went toward the door.

> — So you think, — Grisha shouted after him, — so you think he should be fed jam, the way Nanny does?

> His father stopped.

> — Everyone does his own work, — he remarked, — carries out his duty. The constable was ordered to go for Ignat, and he went. He is a good, kind man, and you have wronged him. And you have wronged me, and Nanny... What for?

> Grisha slowly lowered his eyes, and bewilderment and pain were clearly expressed on his face.

> — That's not good, brother! — the father concluded reproachfully, and went out of the room.

> Grisha sat motionless.

> “That's not good, brother! — his father's reproachful, almost tender voice came back to him. — Not good?.. Wronged?.. — the boy pondered agonizingly, — I wronged... And they all... Ignat... what for?”

> Grisha lowered his head and frowned like a child.

> “Everyone does his own work... But then how did such a bad, evil work come of it?..”

> He raised his eyes, and in his fixed gaze there froze an agonizingly heavy question.

> *L. Avilova.*
