## THE UNBELIEVER

> Two things fill the soul with ever new and constantly increasing wonder and reverence, the more often and the more steadily reflection occupies itself with them: the starry sky above me and the law of morality within me.

> *Kant*.

At the beginning of 1852, when I was living in Brussels, a young man unknown to me came in to see me. The face of the newcomer was pleasant, with a sincere smile and an equally sincere and lively gaze. He was dressed with a certain refinement: he had on a velvet vest with carved buttons, yellow gloves, a flower in his buttonhole, and a little cane in his hand, and a great deal of very white linen was showing. To the question of who he was, he answered that he was a priest.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 517 --> — Or, rather, I was one, — he said. — I have left the false for the true. Now I am the same as you: an exile.

I asked the exile to sit down.

— My name is Anatole Leray, — he said.

We fell into conversation. He told me his life. It turned out that he had been brought up in such a way that, without knowing himself how or why, at 25 he found himself a priest. This awakened him. The dream of a long mystical upbringing dissolved for him on the day when he saw the impassable wall of darkness, that is, of the priesthood, which had risen between nature and him. His first mass was as heavy for him as the last hour of life; as he walked away from the altar, he seemed to himself a ghost.

He looked with horror at what awaited him. He was 25 years old. He felt the full force of life in his veins; all nature through him demanded satisfaction for itself. And meanwhile these demands of nature appeared to him only as a seething of sins.

In short, he had no vocation, and he was horrified that he had understood this so late.

This struggle of the priest against what had been laid on him as a duty, growing stronger and stronger, went on in him for several years. He was strict, faithful, and honest in carrying out the duties he had taken upon himself.

But all the same it ended with his coming out of the struggle, after much suffering, defeated, that is, victorious. The man triumphed over the priest. Leray gave himself up to youth, to life, and to holy, invincible nature. These are his own expressions, when he talked to me about it. He preferred to be an apostate before Rome rather than a hypocrite before his conscience. He left the priesthood. For those who leave the church there is only one open door: democracy. All Leray's inclinations drew him to it. Before he was a cleric, he was a child of the people. He came from a poor Breton family; so he returned to the people as naturally as a drop of water returns to the ocean. And it was good for him.

He told me all this simply, with naive and strong eloquence. His return to the people had given him maturity. There was a political thinker in him; he wrote for several newspapers. He was a revolutionary, ardent and extreme in his convictions.

Having told the story of his life, he went on to set out his thoughts. I listened attentively.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 518 --> In the middle of this account he flared up.

— Yes, my dear sir, — he exclaimed, — let this be a lesson to us. Democracy must take measures. Man must be remade, the people renewed in the children. Only in education will we show the logic of the revolution.

— I think so too, — I said.

He grew still more animated.

— For me, — he said, — all education is in one thing: to free the human mind from everything supernatural.

— What do you mean by the supernatural? — I asked.

— I mean that man is perishing from these religious phantasmagorias. Superstitions are strangling the future. As long as peoples go on breathing in the prevailing fanaticism, human reason cannot be counted on. Yes! this old human reason is perishing under veils and drowning in sacred chimeras. Its boat leaks on all sides. Let us hold to the one undoubted reality. Two times two is four: outside of this there is no salvation. Philosophy must be built only on facts, admitting nothing that cannot be verified by reason. Only what is seen and felt is real. All beliefs must be in my ten fingers. Yes, war, war to the death with everything miraculous. The people must believe only in themselves. They must understand that in the cradle there is nothing but what we see—nothing but an embryo, and in the coffin nothing but annihilation. Away with all phantoms! There is nothing but the earth and life. There is no other heaven than the one in which we already live; our earth turns in it. We must reason soundly and clearly, and away with all dreams! Whoever does not want the fruit cuts down the tree: religion must be deprived of every pretext for its existence.

— What is your religion? — I asked.

— But I told you that I was brought up in a seminary.

— Well?

— Consequently, I am an atheist.

— I cannot agree with such a conclusion, — I said. — The schools of the Jesuits do not necessarily produce Voltaires. However, I am listening to you. Go on.

— I think I have said everything, — he answered. — To be rid of hypotheses, to get out of the prison of chimeras, and to help the human race be rid of them—that is what is needed.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 519 --> — I am no fonder than you of hypotheses that turn into superstitions, or of those chimeras that stand in the way of human reason, — I said. — So it may seem that you and I think alike, and yet we hardly agree. However, I would like you to state your opinion more precisely.

— Very well, — he said, — here it is: a complete end to what the spiritualists call the ideal. The ideal is supernaturalness, and the supernatural must be driven out of the world, and so out of man too. The supernatural in the world is God—let us destroy God; the supernatural in man is the soul—therefore, let us destroy the soul; there is nothing either eternal or immortal. And let us make these truths the foundation of education. I have finished.

— No, you have only just begun, — I answered. — In your view, what then is the world?

— Matter alone.

— And man?

— Matter alone.

— But do you make a distinction between one kind of matter and another?

— I would be mad if I did. Matter is always equal to matter. In this is the chief foundation of equality.

— Well, and organisms? — I said.

— Organisms are only forms. And these forms, inevitably appearing and blind in themselves, produce those mirages which make up a ladder, as it were, the first step of which you call the mind, the next conscience, the next soul, the next God. This ladder is set up by all religions. It is this ladder that must be destroyed. All its steps must be smashed: the step God, the step soul, the step conscience, the step mind, and even the step organism. Down with the organism, if it is made miraculous, if from the difference of organisms people mean to deduce the superiority of one form of matter over another! Down with the aristocracy of organisms: the forms, which disappear, are nothing other than an image of nothing. Everything becomes the atom, the indivisible and unconscious atom. An atom that was higher than the others would be God. Whoever says matter says equality. Matter is always equal to itself.

I looked at him intently.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 520 --> — So then the gnat that flies, and the burdock that grows, and the stone that rolls are equal to man?

He thought for a moment, but then, with complete honesty, which seemed stronger than his will, he answered:

— Although your syllogism is cruel, it is correct.

— Straightforward thinkers are rare, — I said. — You reason entirely consistently, with unfailing good faith. I do not want to abuse it, and so I give up this cruelty of the extreme syllogism. Let us speak only of man, applying your axioms to him: there is no soul, there is no God, there is no supernatural, there is no ideal, matter is equal to itself. I will speak only of one of the countless sides of the question.

— I am listening, — he said.

— What, in your opinion, — I said, — is the aim of man's life on earth?

— Happiness.

— But I think it is duty, obligation, — I said. — But the point is not my thought, but yours. I set aside all sentimental arguments. On the scales of the equality of matter, by how much does the happiness of one man exceed in weight and in value the happiness of another man?

— By zero.

— Before we go further, do you agree that, by logic, every action necessarily requires motives that determine it?

— Undoubtedly.

— I continue. Consequently, if a case presents itself in which the happiness of one man must be sacrificed for the happiness of another—on the scales on which these two happinesses are to be weighed, what quantity of weight will determine the necessity or lawfulness of sacrificing one for the other?

— Zero.

— Therefore, — I said, — if one observes only the material fact, in which, according to you, the only wisdom lies, a man has no reason at all to sacrifice himself, his good, for the good of another man?

Every hesitation seemed to have vanished from his thought. He answered me calmly:

— None at all.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 521 --> — And therefore, — I objected, — no reason at all either to sacrifice one's happiness for the happiness of the human race?

Here Leray started.

— If it concerns the human race, that is another matter.

— Why? — I said. — A sum of zeros will still always be zero.

He fell silent for a moment, then, with a certain effort, agreed with me.

— Truth is always truth, — he said, — you are harsh, but your syllogism is correct.

I went on:

— I am not discussing your principles; I am only drawing out what follows from them. And you yourself, step by step, are drawing this conclusion. You think in a logically correct way, and that is enough for me. And so: man is matter; he comes from nothing and returns to nothing. He has only life; only this life belongs to him. All his reason, all his common sense, all his philosophy consists in making use of this life and making it last as long as possible. The only morality is hygiene; the aim of life is happiness. The aim of life is enjoyment of it; the aim of life is to live. Many conclusions could be drawn from this, but I do not want to draw them now; I will limit myself to asking you: can it be that you truly think so?

— Yes, I truly think so.

— So that if a young man, a healthy one, gives his life for one or for many people, his equals, his neighbors, the same kind of atoms and the same kind of matter as he himself, then what will you call such a man?

— A blockhead.

We parted coldly.

Anatole Leray, having left Brussels, traveled through England, then sailed to Australia. The voyage lasted five months.

On the day the steamer was nearing the harbor, a storm arose. The ship was thrown ashore; the passengers and sailors were almost all saved, some in boats, some by swimming. Anatole Leray was one of those who managed to save themselves. But in that terrible turmoil of the shipwreck, where the terror of people answers the chaos of the waves and where <!-- vol. 41, p. 522 --> each thinks only of himself, he saw a broken boat that was being tossed about on the waves, now showing, now disappearing. In the boat were three women. The sea was still terribly stormy. Not one of the boldest sailors dared to throw himself into the sea to help these perishing women.

But Anatole Leray threw himself into the water, swam to the boat, and with the greatest efforts pulled out one of the women. But two still remained in the boat. He threw himself in a second time and pulled out another. They shouted to him: “Enough! enough!” But, tired and battered, he threw himself in a third time—and did not appear again.

*Victor Hugo (translated by L. N. Tolstoy).*
