## A GRAIN AS BIG AS A HEN'S EGG

Once some children found in a ravine a little thing the size of a hen's egg, with a groove down the middle and looking like a grain. A passerby saw the little thing with the children, bought it for a five-kopeck piece, took it to the town, and sold it to the tsar as a rarity.

The tsar called his wise men and ordered them to find out what sort of thing it was—an egg or a grain? The wise men thought and thought—they could not give an answer. This little thing was lying on the window sill; a hen flew in, began pecking at it, and pecked a hole in it; and everyone saw that it was a grain. The wise men came and said to the tsar: “This is a grain of rye.”

The tsar was astonished. He ordered the wise men to find out where and when this grain had grown. The wise men thought and thought, searched in their books—they found nothing. They came to the tsar and say: “We cannot give an answer. Nothing is written about this in our books; we must ask the peasants whether anyone has heard from the old people when and where such grain was sown?”

The tsar sent; he ordered an old peasant to be brought to him. They searched out an old, old man and brought him to the tsar. The old man came, green and toothless, barely getting in on two crutches.

The tsar showed him the grain, but the old man no longer sees; somehow he made out half of it, and half he felt with his hands.

The tsar began to ask him: “Don't you know, grandfather, where such grain grew? Didn't you yourself ever sow such grain in your field? Or in your lifetime didn't you ever buy such grain anywhere?

The old man was deaf; barely, barely did he hear, barely, barely did he understand. He began to make his answer: “No, he says, such grain in my field sow it I never sowed, and reap it I never reaped, and buy it I never bought. When we bought grain, it was all just as small a grain as now. But, he says, you must ask my father; maybe he has heard where such grain used to grow?”

The tsar sent for the old man's father and ordered him to be brought. They found the old man's father too and brought him to the tsar. An old, old man came on <!-- vol. 41, p. 289 --> one crutch. The tsar began showing him the grain. The old man can still see with his eyes; he made it out well. The tsar began to ask him: “Don't you know, old fellow, where such grain grew? Didn't you yourself ever sow such grain in your field? Or in your lifetime didn't you ever buy such grain anywhere?”

Though the old man was rather hard of hearing, he heard better than his son. “No, he says, such grain in my field sow it I never sowed, and reap it I never reaped. And buy it I never bought, because in my time money hadn't even come into use yet. Everyone fed on his own grain, and in need—they shared with one another. I don't know where such grain grew. Though our grain was bigger than today's and threshed out more, the like of this I never did see. I heard from my father that in his time grain used to grow better than ours and threshed out more and was bigger. He is the one to ask.”

The tsar sent for the old man's father. They found the grandfather too and brought him to the tsar. The old man came in to the tsar without crutches; he came in lightly—his eyes are bright, he hears well, and he speaks clearly. The tsar showed the grandfather the grain. The grandfather looked at it and turned it over. “It's a long time, — he says, — since I've seen the old-time grain.” The grandfather bit off some of the grain and chewed a morsel.

— That's the very one, — he says.

— Then tell me, grandfather, where did such grain grow? Didn't you ever sow such grain in your field? Or in your lifetime didn't you ever buy it from people somewhere?

And the old man said: “Grain like this grew everywhere in my lifetime. On this grain, — he says, — I fed myself all my life and fed people.”

And the tsar asked: “Then tell me, grandfather, did you buy such grain anywhere, or did you sow it yourself in your own field?”

The old man smiled.

— In my time, — he says, — no one could even have thought of such a sin as selling and buying grain. And of money we didn't even know; everyone had plenty of grain of his own. I myself sowed such grain, and reaped it, and threshed it.

And the tsar asked: “Then tell me, grandfather, where did you sow such grain, and where was your field?”

And the grandfather said: “My field was—God's land. Wherever one plowed, there was the field. The land was free. No one knew of land as his own. Only their labors did people call their own.”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 290 --> — Tell me, — says the tsar, — two more things: one thing—why did such grain grow before, and now it does not? And the other thing—why did your grandson walk on two crutches, your son came on one crutch, and you here came in quite lightly, your eyes are bright, and your teeth strong, and your speech clear and kindly? Why, tell me, grandfather, did these two things come about?

And the old man said: “Both things came about because people stopped living by their own labors—they began to covet what is other people's. In the old days they did not live so: in the old days they lived in God's way; they held what was their own and did not seek gain from what was others'.”

*Leo Tolstoy.*
