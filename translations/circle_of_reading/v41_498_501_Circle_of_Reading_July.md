## II THE ATTITUDE OF THE FIRST CHRISTIANS TO WAR

“The world raves in mutual bloodshed, and murder, which is considered a crime when people commit it singly, is called a virtue if it is done by a multitude.”—<!-- vol. 41, p. 499 --> So wrote the famous Cyprian in the third century, speaking of the military.

The whole Christian community of the first centuries, up to the fifth century, regarded war in the same way. The Christian community, in the persons of its leaders, definitely acknowledged that all killing is forbidden to Christians, and therefore killing in war too.

The philosopher Tatian, who converted to Christianity in the second century, considers killing in war as inadmissible for Christians as any killing, and considers the honorary military wreath unseemly for a Christian. In the same century Athenagoras of Athens says that Christians not only never kill anyone themselves, but even avoid being present at killings.

In the third century Clement of Alexandria sets the “peaceful tribe of Christians” against the pagan “warlike” peoples. But the famous Origen expressed the Christians' aversion to war most clearly of all. Applying to Christians the words of Isaiah that the time will come when people will beat their swords into sickles and their spears into plows, he says quite definitely: “We do not raise arms against any people, we do not learn the art of war, for through Jesus Christ we have become children of peace.” Answering Celsus's accusation against the Christians that they evade military service, so that, in Celsus's opinion, if only the Roman Empire becomes Christian, it will perish, Origen says that Christians fight more than others for the good of the emperor, fight for him with good deeds, with prayer, and with good influence on people. As for fighting with weapons, it is perfectly true, says Origen, that Christians do not fight alongside the imperial troops and would not go even if the emperor compelled them to.

Tertullian, a contemporary of Origen, speaks just as decisively about the impossibility of a Christian's being a soldier. “It is not fitting to serve the sign of Christ and the sign of the devil,” he says of military service, “the fortress of light and the fortress of darkness; one soul cannot serve two masters. And how is one to make war without the sword, which the Lord himself has taken away? Can one really train with the sword when the Lord said that everyone who takes up the sword will perish by the sword? And how will a son of peace take part in battle?”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 500 --> In the fourth century Lactantius says the same. “There must be no exception to the commandment of God that to kill a person is always a sin,” he says. “Christians are not permitted to bear arms, for their weapon is truth alone.” In the rules of the Egyptian church of the third century and in the so-called “Testament of Our Lord Jesus Christ,” every Christian is unconditionally forbidden to enter military service, on pain of excommunication from the church.

In the “Acts of the Saints” there are many examples of Christian martyrs of the first centuries who suffered for refusing to continue service in the Roman legions.

Thus Maximilian, brought before the conscription board, at the proconsul's first question, what his name was, answered: “My name is Christian, and therefore I cannot fight.” In spite of this declaration, he was enrolled as a soldier, but he refused to serve. He was told that he must choose between performing his military duty and death. He said: “I would sooner die, but I cannot fight.” He was handed over to the executioners.

Marcellus was a centurion in the Trojan legion. Having come to believe in the teaching of Christ and having become convinced that war is an un-Christian thing, he took off his military armor in view of the whole legion, threw it on the ground, and declared that, having become a Christian, he could serve no longer. He was put in prison, but there too he said: “A Christian must not bear arms.” He was executed.

Following Marcellus, Cassian, who served in the same legion, refused military service. He too was executed.

Under Julian the Apostate, Martin, who had been brought up and had grown up among the military, refused to continue military service. At the interrogation conducted by the emperor, he said only: “I am a Christian and therefore cannot fight.”

The First Ecumenical Council (325) clearly laid down a strict penance for re-entering the army on the part of Christians who had left the service. The authentic words of this decree, in the translation recognized by the Orthodox Church, are these:

“Those who were called by grace to the confession of the faith and showed the first impulse of zeal and put off their military belts, but afterward, as dogs, returned to their own vomit... let such for 10 years fall down before the church, asking forgiveness, after three years' hearing of the Scripture in the porch.”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 501 --> It was laid as a duty on the Christians who remained in the army not to kill enemies in time of war. Still in the fourth century Basil the Great recommends that soldiers guilty of violating this rule not be admitted to communion for three years.

Thus not only in the first three centuries of Christianity, at the time of the persecutions of Christians, but also in the first period of the triumph of Christianity over paganism, when Christianity had been recognized as the dominant, state religion, the conviction still held among Christians that war is incompatible with Christianity. Ferrutius expressed this definitely and decisively (and was executed for it):

“Christians are not permitted to shed blood, even in a just war and at the command of Christian sovereigns.”

In the fourth century Lucifer, bishop of Cagliari, teaches that even the good most precious to Christians—their faith—they must defend “not by the killing of others, but by their own death.” Paulinus, bishop of Nola, who died in 431, still threatened eternal torment for serving Caesar with weapons in hand.

Such was the view of the Christians of the first four centuries on the relation of Christianity to military service.
