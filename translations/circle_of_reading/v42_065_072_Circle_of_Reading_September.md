## THE DEATH OF SOCRATES

*From Plato's “Dialogues”*

Soon after the death of Socrates, one of his disciples, Echecrates, meeting Phaedo, another disciple of Socrates, who had been present at the death of the teacher, asked him to tell him in detail everything that had happened on that day, what those around Socrates had said, what he himself had said and done, and how he had died.

And Phaedo told the following:

— On that day too we came, as we had usually come on the preceding days as well, to the court building next to the prison. The doorkeeper who usually let us into the prison came out to us and told us to wait a little, because the judges were with Socrates now: they were taking off his fetters and announcing to him the order to drink the poison that very day. A little time passed, and the doorkeeper came out to us and said that we could go in. When we went in, his wife Xanthippe was with Socrates, with a child in her arms. She was sitting beside him on his bed.

As soon as Xanthippe saw us, she began to weep and to utter the pitiful words that women usually say on such occasions: “Here are your friends who will talk with you for the last time, and you with them,” and the like.

Socrates tried to calm her and asked her to leave us alone with him for a while. When Xanthippe had gone, Socrates, bending his leg, began to rub it with his hand and, turning to us, said: “Here, my friends, is an astonishing thing: how pleasure is bound up with suffering! I was in pain from the fetters, and now, when they have been taken off, I feel a special pleasure. Probably the gods, wishing to reconcile two opposites—suffering and pleasure—bound them together with a chain, so that one cannot experience the one without the other.” Socrates wanted to say something more, but, noticing that Crito was talking quietly with someone outside the door, he asked what he was talking about.

— Why, the one who is to give you the poison, — said Crito, — says that you must talk as little as possible. He says that those who talk before taking the poison get heated, and then the poison works weakly, and they have to drink twice and three times as much.

— Well, what of it! — said Socrates, — we will drink twice and three times as much, if need be, and I think that I must not miss the chance <!-- vol. 42, p. 66 --> to talk with you precisely now and to show that a man who throughout his life has striven for wisdom not only does not grieve but rejoices at the approach of death.

— But how can you rejoice that you are leaving us? — said one of us.

— It is true, — said Socrates, — that this seems not good on my part, but if you enter into my position, you will surely understand that a man who all his life has striven to subdue his passions, in which his body hindered him, cannot help rejoicing at his release from it. And death, after all, is only release. For that perfecting of which we have spoken more than once consists in separating the soul from the body as far as possible and accustoming it to gather and concentrate itself outside the body, within itself; and death gives this very release. So would it not be strange that a man should prepare all his life to live so as to be as close as possible to existence in death, and when this approach is ready to be accomplished, should be displeased with it? And therefore, however sorry I am to part from you and to grieve you, I cannot help welcoming death as the fulfillment of what I was striving to attain throughout my life. So here, friends, is my defense for not grieving at leaving you. I shall be glad if this defense of mine is more convincing than the one I delivered at the trial, — he said, smiling.

— But for this to be so, — Cebes said to this, — one must be certain that the soul, on leaving the body, is not destroyed and does not perish like some vapor or smoke; it would be good to believe or to know that this is so. But the trouble is that one cannot be certain of it.

— That is true, — said Socrates. — One cannot be completely certain, but there is great likelihood that it is so. Tradition says that the souls of dead people go to Hades and go on existing there until they return to the world again and are born again from the dead. One may believe the tradition or not believe it, but there is great likelihood that people are born from the dead, because not only people but all animals, plants—everything is reborn from what has died. And if this is so, then life cannot fear death, and death is only rebirth into a new life. This is confirmed also by the fact that all of us, living in this world, carry in ourselves memories, as it were, of a former <!-- vol. 42, p. 67 --> life of the soul. And there could be no memories if the soul had not lived before this life. So that, although the body of man is mortal, the soul with its capacity for knowledge, for memory, cannot die together with the body. But it is not only that all our knowledge is nothing but memories of the soul's former life: the chief proof of the presence in us of a soul independent of the body and undying is that the eternal ideas of beauty, good, justice, and truth are not only proper to our soul, but these ideas make up the very essence of our soul. And since these ideas are not subject to death, our soul too is not subject to death.

Socrates finished, and we were all silent; only Cebes and Simmias were saying something quietly to each other.

— What is it you are talking about? — asked Socrates. — If you are talking about what was just being said, then tell me what you think. If you do not agree or know a better explanation, then say so plainly.

— I will tell the truth, — said Simmias: — I do not entirely agree with what you said, and I want to ask you, but I am afraid that with a question I may cause you something unpleasant in your position.

— How hard it is, though, — said Socrates, smiling, — to convince people that I do not consider what has happened to me a misfortune. If I cannot convince even you, then how am I to convince other people? You are wrong to think that I am now in a different frame of mind than usual. So tell me, what is your doubt?

— If so, — said Simmias, — then I will say plainly what I doubt. It seems to me, Socrates, that what you said about the soul is not fully proved.

— In what? — asked Socrates.

— In this, — said Simmias, — that what you said about the soul could be said about the tuning of a lyre. It could be said that although the lyre itself with its strings is something bodily, earthly, and transient, the tuning of the lyre and the sounds it gives out are something bodiless and not subject to death, and that therefore, even if the lyre breaks and its strings snap, still the tuning and the sounds it produced cannot die and certainly remain somewhere even after the lyre is destroyed. And yet we know that, just as the harmony of a lyre is the consequence of the combination of strings stretched to a certain tension, <!-- vol. 42, p. 68 --> so our soul is the union and interaction of various elements of the body standing in a certain relation, and that therefore, just as the harmony of the lyre is destroyed with the destruction of its component parts, so the soul is destroyed in consequence of the disturbance of the relations that make up our body; and these disturbances take place in consequence of various illnesses, or of excessive slackening or tension of the component parts of the body.

When Simmias had finished, we all had an unpleasant feeling, as we told one another afterward. We had barely been convinced by Socrates' words of the immortality of the soul when strong arguments to the contrary confused us again and gave rise to distrust not only of what had been said but, as it seemed to us, of everything that could be said on this subject.

I had often marveled at Socrates, but never more than this time. That he was not at a loss for an answer is nothing surprising yet, but what I marveled at most of all was the good nature and calm with which he listened kindly and approvingly to Simmias's speech, and the way he then, noticing the impression this speech had made on us, skillfully helped us out of our doubt.

At that time I was sitting on his right, by his bed, on a low chair, and he, sitting on the bed, was higher than I. He had a habit of playing with my hair. So now too, stroking my head with his hand and gathering my hair at the back of my neck, he said:

— Tomorrow, Phaedo, you will cut off this beautiful hair.

— Yes, — I said.

— But hold off cutting it, and do as I do.

— What, then? — I asked.

— This. Let us promise to cut off our hair, you yours tomorrow, and I mine this very day, but only if we do not manage to defend our arguments.

I said jokingly that I agreed, and then Socrates turned to Simmias.

— Very well, Simmias, — he said. — The soul is like a harmony. And just as harmony arises from the right relation of the lyre and the strings, so the soul also arises from a certain relation of the elements of the body. But if this is so, then how is this to be reconciled with what we were saying just now, and what you agreed with, that all our knowledge is memories of what we knew in preceding existences? And if the soul existed before the body in which it now is, then how <!-- vol. 42, p. 69 --> can it be the consequence of a certain proportion of the parts of the body? So that if we acknowledge that all our knowledge is memories of former existences, then we must also acknowledge that our soul has an existence independent of the conditions in which the body finds itself. Besides this, the difference between a harmony and the soul is also that a harmony is not conscious of itself, while the soul is conscious of its life, and not only conscious of it but also governs it. A harmony cannot change the position of the lyre and depends on it, while the soul is independent of the body and can entirely change its state. Thus, for example, right now all the elements of my body are in a right proportion, the same as yesterday, and yet my soul has decided something that will very soon disturb this right relation of the elements, because, as you know, if I had agreed to Crito's proposal to escape from prison, I would now be far from here and would not be sitting here talking with you while awaiting execution. And I did not agree to Crito's proposal because I considered it more just to submit to the decision of the republic than to evade it.

So it turns out that the harmony has sentenced the lyre to destruction, that is, that there is something in me that is conscious of its undying principle.

And therefore, although I cannot prove this with complete evidence, being conscious in myself of a reasonable and free principle that surpasses the bodily shell in which it is found, I cannot help believing that my soul is immortal.

If the soul is immortal, — Socrates went on, — then we are obliged to take care of it not only for this life but also for the one into which it passes at the death of the body.

Because if the soul is immortal and carries away with it into other lives what it has acquired here, then how can one not try to make it as good and wise as possible!

And, after a short silence, he added:

— But, my friends, it seems to me it is already time to see to the washing, because it is better to drink the poison washed, so as not to give the women the trouble of washing a dead body.

When he had said this, Crito asked him what he charged us to carry out with regard to his children?

— What I have always said, Crito, — he said, — nothing new. By taking care of yourselves, of your soul, you will do <!-- vol. 42, p. 70 --> the very best both for me, and for my sons, and for yourselves, even if you did not promise me this.

— We will try to act so, — answered Crito. — But how are we to bury you?

— As you like, — he answered, and, smiling, added: — All the same, my friends, I cannot convince Crito that Socrates is only the I who is now talking with you, and not the one whom in a little while he will see motionless and cold.

Having said this, he got up and went into a room to wash. Crito went after him; and us he ordered to wait. And so we waited, talking among ourselves about what had been said and about the misfortune that had befallen us, depriving us of a friend, teacher, and guide.

When Socrates had finished washing and his children had been brought to him—he had two little sons and one grown son—and when the women of his household had come in, he, after talking with them, sent the women and children away and came out to us again. It was already near sunset when Socrates came out to us. Soon after him the servant of the Eleven came in and, going up to Socrates, said:

— Socrates, you surely will not blame me, be angry with me, and abuse me, as the condemned are angry and abuse me when, by order of the Eleven, I require them to drink the poison. I have come to know you during this time and consider you the noblest, gentlest, and best man of all those who have come in here, and therefore I hope that now too you are indignant not against me—for you know who is to blame for this—but against them. I have come to tell you that it is time to drink the poison—farewell, and try to bear as easily as possible what is inevitable.

Having said this, the servant began to weep—turned aside and went out.

— Farewell to you too, — said Socrates, — and we will do our part. And then, turning to us, he added: — What a good man! All this time he has been visiting me, talking with me, and I came to know him as a very good man. And now how touchingly he pities me! Well, Crito, let us carry out his demand; let them bring me the poison, if it is ready.

— I think, Socrates, — objected Crito, — that the sun is still high, and besides, many take the poison only <!-- vol. 42, p. 71 --> very late, and feast all evening, and some even enjoy the pleasures of love as well. There is no need to hurry. There is still time.

— Those you speak of, dear Crito, — said Socrates, — had reason to act as they did, thinking, probably, that it was good for them, but I think otherwise. I think that by drinking the poison a little later I shall gain nothing except to become ridiculous in my own eyes. Go and tell them to bring the poison.

Crito, having heard this, made a sign to the servant standing outside the door. The servant went out and soon returned, bringing with him the man who was to give Socrates the poison.

— These things are familiar to you, — Socrates said to him, — teach me what must be done.

— You need only, — the man answered, — after drinking, walk about until your legs grow heavy; and when they grow heavy, lie down, and the poison will do its work.

Having said this, he handed Socrates the cup. Socrates took it and, with a cheerful look, without the slightest fear, with no change at all either in his face or in his gaze, but, looking intently at the jailer, as was his custom, asked:

— What do you think about pouring a libation from this drink in honor of some deity: is it allowed or not?

— We have prepared just so much, Socrates, — the man answered, — as we considered necessary.

— Very well, — said Socrates. — But still one must pray to the gods that my migration from here to there may be accomplished safely: that is what I am praying for now.

Having said this, he raised the cup to his mouth and, without stopping, without fear or hesitation, drank all that was in it. Up to that moment we had held ourselves back and had not wept, but when we saw that he was drinking and had already drunk, we could no longer hold back: tears poured from me against my will; wrapping my head in my cloak, I wept for myself: it was not him but my own misfortune that I lamented, losing such a friend in him. Crito, who even before me had been unable to hold back his tears, went out. Apollodorus had not stopped weeping even before, but now he broke into sobs.

— What are you doing, you astonishing people? — said Socrates. — I sent the women away so that they would not do anything <!-- vol. 42, p. 72 --> of the kind. One should die in reverent silence. Calm yourselves and be brave.

Making an effort over ourselves, we stopped weeping. And he walked about in silence for some time, went up to the bed and, saying that his legs were growing heavy, lay down on his back, as the servant who had brought the poison had advised him to lie. He lay motionless; and the servant from time to time touched his feet and shins. Squeezing one of his feet, the servant asked whether he felt it? Socrates answered: “no.” Then again, pressing his hands on the shins and thighs, he showed us that Socrates was growing cold and stiff.

— As soon as the cold reaches the heart, — he said, — then it is the end.

The cold had already reached the lower part of the belly when Socrates, suddenly uncovering himself—for he had been covered—said his last word:

— Do not forget to offer a rooster in sacrifice to Asclepius.

Evidently he meant by this that he was grateful to the god of medical science, who by means of the remedy he had invented had cured him of life.

— We will do it, — answered Crito. — But do you not have something more to say?

To this question Socrates no longer answered anything, and after a little while he made a convulsive movement, after which the servant uncovered him. His gaze was already motionless. Crito went up to him and closed the lids over his open, fixed eyes.
