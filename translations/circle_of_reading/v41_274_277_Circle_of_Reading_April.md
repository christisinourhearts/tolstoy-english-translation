## FROM A LETTER

Before man is the world, which was before him and will remain after him, and he knows that this world is eternal and that he would like to take part in this eternity. Once man has been called to life, he demands his share in that eternal life which surrounds him, excites him, mocks him, and destroys him. He knows that he has begun, and he does not want to end. He loudly calls out, he begs in a quiet voice for certainty, which constantly slips away from him for his own happiness, because certain knowledge would be immobility and death for him, since the strongest mover of human energy is the unknown. Man cannot settle himself in certainty and is borne about in indefinite strivings toward perfection, and, however far he may stray into skepticism, into denial out of pride, curiosity, malice, fashion—he always returns to hope, without which he cannot live.

So that there is sometimes an eclipse, but never a complete disappearance of man's striving toward perfection. Philosophical mists pass across it, like clouds before the moon, but the white luminary continues its procession and suddenly appears from behind them untouched and shining. This irrepressible need of perfection in man explains why man has thrown himself with such trust, with such rapture, without rational control, into various religious teachings, which, promising him the infinite, offered it to him in accordance with his nature and placed him within certain bounds, always necessary even for perfection.

But now for a long time already, at every station of humanity's movement, new people have been coming out of the darkness in greater and greater <!-- vol. 41, p. 275 --> numbers, especially in the last 100 years, and these people, in the name of reason, science, observation, deny what was considered truths, declare them relative, and want to destroy those teachings which contain them.

And yet that force, whatever it may be, which created the world, since the world, as it seems to me, could not after all have created itself, having made us its instruments, has kept for itself the right to know why it made us and where it is leading us. This force, despite all the intentions that have been ascribed to it and all the demands that have been made of it—this force, it seems, wishes to keep its secret, and therefore (I will say here all that I think) it seems to me that humanity is beginning to give up the desire to penetrate it. Humanity turned to religions, which proved nothing to it, because they were different; it turned to philosophies, which explained no more to it, because they were contradictory; it will now try to manage alone with its simple instinct and its common sense, and, since it lives on the earth without knowing why and how, it will try to be as happy as is possible by the means that our planet affords it.

There are people who propose, as a remedy against all difficulties in life, labor. The medicine is well known, and it is not the less good for that, but it always was and continues to be insufficient. Let a man work with his muscles or with his mind, still the obtaining of food, the making of a fortune, or the acquiring of fame can never be his only concern. All those who limit themselves to these aims feel, even when they have reached them, that they still lack something: the point is that, whatever a man produces, whatever he says, whatever is said to him, he consists not only of a body which must be fed and a mind which must be educated and developed—he undoubtedly also has a soul, which also makes its demands. It is this soul that is in unceasing labor, in constant development and striving toward light and truth. Until it receives all the light and conquers all the truth, it will torment man.

And never has it so occupied, never has it imposed its power with such force on man, as in our time. It is, so to speak, diffused through all the air that the world breathes.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 276 --> Those few individual souls which separately desired social regeneration little by little sought out and called one another, drew close, united, understood themselves, and formed a group, a center of attraction, toward which others are now striving, souls from the four ends of the world, as larks fly to a mirror: they have thus formed a common soul, so that people should henceforth bring about together, consciously and irresistibly, the coming unity and the orderly forward movement of peoples until recently hostile to one another. This new soul I find and recognize in phenomena that seem most of all to deny it.

These armaments of all peoples, these threats that their rulers make to one another, these renewals of the persecution of certain nationalities, these hostilities between fellow countrymen are phenomena of bad appearance, but not of bad omen. These are the last convulsions of what must disappear. Illness in this case is only the energetic effort of a living being to free itself from a deadly principle.

Those who have profited, and hoped to go on profiting long and always, from the errors of the past are uniting with the aim of hindering every change. As a consequence of this—these armaments, these threats, these persecutions, but, if you look more closely, you will see that all this is only external. All this is enormous, but empty.

In all this there is no longer any soul: it has passed to another place. All these millions of armed people, who exercise every day with a view to a universal war of extermination, no longer hate those with whom they are to fight; not one of their chiefs dares to declare war. As for the reproaches, even contagious ones, which are heard from below, from above there already begins to answer them a great and true compassion that recognizes their justice.

Mutual understanding will inevitably come at a definite time, and one nearer than we suppose. I do not know whether this comes from the fact that I will soon leave this world and that the light issuing from beneath the horizon, which illumines me, is already darkening my sight, but I think that our world is entering the epoch of the fulfillment of the words: “love one another,” without reasoning about who said these words: God or man.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 277 --> The spiritualist movement, noticeable on all sides, which so many vain and naive people think to direct, will be unconditionally humane. People who do nothing with moderation will be seized with madness, with a frenzy to love one another. This, obviously, will not at first come about by itself. There will be misunderstandings, perhaps bloody ones too: so thoroughly have we been brought up and trained to hate one another, often by those very people who are called to teach us love. But since it is obvious that this great law of brotherhood must some day be fulfilled, I am convinced that times are coming in which we will irresistibly desire that it be fulfilled.

*Alexandre Dumas.*
