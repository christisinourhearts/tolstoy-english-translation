<!-- vol. 41, p. 472 -->

6 July

Neither descriptions nor the sight of the horrors of war stop people from taking part in war. One of the reasons for this is that, contemplating the horrors of war, everyone involuntarily comes to an unexpressed, vague consideration that, if so terrible a business exists and is permitted, then there probably also exist reasons for this terrible business that are hidden from him. This consideration has the effect that people, often not evil ones, defend war, seeking out its beneficent sides, as people seek out the beneficence of elemental phenomena, forgetting that they themselves make it.

1

> Thought stops in horror before the catastrophe that inevitably awaits us at the end of the century, and we must prepare for it. For 20 years (now more than 50) all the efforts of knowledge have been exhausted in inventing instruments of destruction, and soon a few cannon shots will be enough to annihilate a whole army. Under arms now are no longer, as <!-- vol. 41, p. 473 --> before, a few thousand venal poor men, but peoples, whole peoples, are preparing to kill one another. To prepare them for killing, their hatred is kindled by assuring them that they are hated, and meek people believe it, and any moment now crowds of peaceful citizens, having received the absurd order to kill one another, God knows over what ridiculous distribution of borders or what commercial, colonial interests, will throw themselves on one another with the cruelty of wild beasts.

> And they will go like sheep to the slaughter, knowing what they are going to, knowing that they are leaving their wives, that their children will go hungry, but they will go, so intoxicated by sonorous and lying words, so deceived that, imagining that the slaughter is their duty, they will ask God to bless their bloody deeds. And they will go, trampling the harvests they sowed, burning the towns they built, with rapturous singing, shouts of joy, festive music; they will go without indignation, submissive and humble, in spite of the fact that the strength is in them, and that if they could agree among themselves, they would establish common sense and brotherhood in place of the savage wiles of diplomats.

> *Rod.*

2

> An eyewitness tells what he saw in the Russo-Japanese war on coming onto the deck of the “Varyag.” The sight was terrible. Everywhere blood, scraps of flesh, headless trunks, torn-off arms, a smell of blood that sickened the most hardened. The battle tower suffered most of all. A shell burst at its top and killed a young officer who was directing the aiming. Of the unfortunate man there remained only a clenched hand holding an instrument. Of the four men who were with the commander, two were torn to pieces, the other two badly wounded (both their legs were cut off, and then they had to be cut off once more); the commander got off with a blow from a splinter to the temple.

> And that is not all. Neutrals cannot take the wounded onto their steamers, because gangrene and fever are contagious.

> Gangrene and purulent, hospital infections, together with hunger, fire, devastation, diseases, typhus, smallpox, also form part of military glory. Such is war.

> And meanwhile Joseph de Maistre extolled the benefactions of war thus:

> But the poor men out of whom cannon fodder is made have the right to disagree with this.

> Unfortunately, they do not have the courage of their convictions. From this comes all the evil. Having long been accustomed to letting themselves be killed over questions they do not understand, they go on doing it, imagining that all is going very well.

> That is why there now lie there corpses that lobsters devour under the water.

> At the time when grapeshot was smashing everything around them, they were hardly glad to think that all this was being done for their good, to restore the soul of their contemporaries, which had lost its resilience from an excess of civilization.

> The unfortunates had probably not read Joseph de Maistre. I advise the wounded to read him between two dressings. They will learn that war is as necessary as the executioner, because, like him, it is a manifestation of the justice of God.

> And this great thought will serve them as a consolation while the surgeon's saw is sawing through their bones.

> *Harduin.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 474 --> “When the human soul, through effeminacy, loses its resilience, becomes unbelieving, and takes on the putrid vices that follow excesses of civilization, it can be restored only in blood.”

3

> War is more terrible than ever. The skillful artist in this business, the murderer of genius Mr. Moltke, answered the delegates of peace with these strange words:

> “War is holy, a divine institution, one of the sacred laws of the world. It sustains in people all the great and noble feelings: honor, disinterestedness, virtue, courage—in a word, it saves people from disgusting materialism.”

> So, to gather in herds of four hundred thousand men, to march day and night without rest, to think of nothing, to study nothing, to learn nothing, to read nothing, to be of use to no one, to rot in filth, to sleep in mud, to live like cattle in constant stupefaction, to plunder towns, burn villages, ruin peoples, then, meeting another such mass of human meat, to throw oneself on it, to shed <!-- vol. 41, p. 475 --> lakes of blood, to cover the fields with torn meat and strew the earth with heaps of corpses, to be crippled, to be smashed without benefit to anyone whatsoever, and finally to croak somewhere in a foreign field, while your parents, your wife, and children are dying of hunger at home—this is called saving people from disgusting materialism.

> *Guy de Maupassant.*

4

*The time for reasoning about the harm of war is past. Everything has already been said about it. Now one thing remains, with which every person should have begun: not to do what he considers he ought not to do.*

It is not true that the existence of war proves its necessity. The conscience of humanity says that this is not true and that there must be no war.
