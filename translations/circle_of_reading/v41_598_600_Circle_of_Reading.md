<!-- vol. 41, p. 598 -->

28 August

Faith determines life.

1

Religious knowledge is that on which every other rests. And therefore it precedes all knowledge.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 599 -->

2

In order to understand that all people are equal, that it is better for a man to give his life to the service of others than to compel other people to serve him, one must determine one's relation to the world, and such a determination of a man's relation to the world is given only by religion.

3

Attempts to found morality apart from religion are like what children do who, wanting to transplant a plant they like, tear off from it the root that they do not like and that seems to them superfluous, and stick the plant into the ground without a root. Without a religious foundation there can be no real, unfeigned morality, just as without a root there can be no real plant.

4

To a kind peasant, who lived a good life, the priest at confession put the usual question: did he believe in God?

“I am a sinner, I do not believe,” answered the peasant.

“What? You do not believe in God?”

“I do not believe, Father. If I believed, would I live like this? You think only about yourself: you eat, you drink, and you forget God and your brothers.”

What would there be if everyone understood faith as this peasant did and believed in the law of Christ?

5

There are two faiths: the faith of trust in what people say—this is faith in a man or in people, and there are many different faiths of this kind; and faith in one's dependence on him who sent me into the world. This is faith in God, and such faith is one for all people.

6

Faith is an inevitable property of the soul.

Man inevitably believes in something. He inevitably believes because, besides the objects that he knows, he also <!-- vol. 41, p. 600 --> enters into relation with what he cannot know, and yet knows that it is. The relation to this incomprehensible is faith.

7

Every person always feels alike the insignificance of all that is comprehensible, as well as the greatness of something incomprehensible and most important.

8

*Whatever position a man may be in, the ideal given by Christ in his teaching is always sufficient to obtain the surest indication of the actions which ought and which ought not to be done. But one must believe this teaching fully, this one teaching, and stop believing in all others, just as a seafarer must believe in the compass and stop peering at and being guided by what one sees on either side.*

9

There are people who assert that they have no faith. This is untrue. They only do not know their faith, or cannot or do not want to express it, but they have it. And they renounce their faith only because they feel that it is not good.

10

> A man's “religion” consists not of the many things that he doubts and tries to believe, but of the few things that he is sure of and belief in which requires no effort from him.

> *Carlyle.*

Try to become conscious of your faith, of that in the name of which you live. This will help you to correct it if it is mistaken, and to become firm in it if it is true.
