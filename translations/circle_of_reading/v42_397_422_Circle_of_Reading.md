## MONTHLY READINGS

## January

## February

The most ancient, deep-rooted, and enormous error of our life is the error of worship. There is no greater sanctity on earth—the sanctity of man, the living bearer of divinity, and yet we see that what comes from man, human products, fabrications, and deeds, are exalted above man, the creation is set above the creator.

But it is time for all to see and understand that undoubted, obvious fact that all the efforts of the life of the best, righteous, and wise people, from Moses to our time, with Jesus Christ at their head, have always been directed to the struggle with deceptions, with these very human fabrications exalted above man, to the struggle with these idols, in order to show people this fanaticism, in order to make clear before people the truth worthy of faith, in order to bring man to the consciousness of the source of life and of all deeds within himself, in order to return man to the will of God and thereby to set him free.

Moses rejected material idols, but kept circumcision and created the Sabbath, and the later teachers of Judaism created new idols: rituals and the traditions of the elders. The Christian teachers rejected the Sabbath, and circumcision, and the Jewish rituals, but created baptism and the breaking of bread, and then gods poured out as from a horn of plenty: the holy spirit, the savior, the mother of God, angels, prelates and saints, God-pleasers and wonder-workers, icons and crosses and countless material images and objects; and, what is most astonishing of all, even the remains of dead men, these new Egyptian mummies.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 398 --> And as in religion, so in social life: the work of man is set above man. Monarchy, oligarchy, constitution, republic—the names and forms change, but always something is set up to which man must sacrifice his spiritual freedom; the so-called public good is bought at the price of personal enslavement. Every religion, every form of social life, as the outward expression of the laws of life, must serve him who bears within himself the essence of life and is the embodiment of divinity, man, and not enslave him. It is not the humiliation and rejection of the dignity of the rational being in man that should reign among people, but its liberation and exaltation. It is not external power, not the fear of suffering and death coming from outside, that should bind the passions and the evil will of man (and they do not bind them) and give people assurance of tranquility (and they do not give it), but the consciousness of the eternal law of life, the consciousness of the spiritual unity of people, the power that comes from above, the power of God, the power of reason, the power of conscience—that inner spiritual power which lies in the nature of man and which now shows itself only weakly under the concerted and systematic oppression of church and state; once set free, it will become a mighty bridle on all evil and the natural and reliable stronghold of personal and social tranquility and well-being. It is this very inner force, this universal light of reason, that man should place at the head of his own strictly personal and social life. And as the dusk of night melts before the light of the rising dawn, as ignorance and superstition, miracles and sorcery perish of themselves before the light of science, as wood goblins, water sprites, and house spirits have vanished before the light of knowledge and reason, so before the face of that same fire and light all the social institutions that are now so majestic, deceptive, and violent will come to nothing, and all malice, violence, and slavery among people will vanish of themselves.

*Buka.*

## March

We know that in the old days people worshipped idols. There would be, for example, a crop failure; people think that there is a special god of the harvest, that he has for some reason taken offense at man, that he must therefore be appeased, and people made an idol out of wood, called it the god of the harvest, bowed down to it, and offered sacrifices. And when after that a good harvest came, people thought that this was because they had prayed to the idol and offered it sacrifices. But now we know that the people of old were deceived; now we know that there is a God who reveals himself to man in reason by the laws of life, but that there is no special god of the harvest, that from their bowing down and offering sacrifices to their own human product no gain could come, that if a good harvest did come afterward, then that was how it was bound to be <!-- vol. 42, p. 399 --> in the order of the world's life, of which those people had no notion. This we now know, but we do not know that even now we are deceived still more, bow down with still greater humiliation, and offer still immeasurably greater sacrifices, and all as before to the same human product. The only difference is that the people of old worshipped a material product, while we worship an invention. We, the people of today, have made ourselves various churches, various faiths, and all of them the most orthodox and right-believing; have made ourselves many states and planted tsars in them; have invented governments, governorships, zemstvos, and planted in them ministers, governors, chairmen, members, and various officials, and their name, like that of the Gadarene demons, is legion. People have made themselves so many objects of worship and sacrifice that one cannot take a step without running into some idol. And people believe that all these institutions, all these idols, are needed, and to the last possible degree they humiliate themselves with worship and offer in sacrifice not only all that they have, but even themselves, imagining that some gain will come to them from this. But in reality there is no gain at all; and if we are still somehow breathing, then this is, of course, not because we have many social, sacred institutions of every kind, but in spite of this—not all the laws of life, then, have yet been replaced by substitutes; the true God, then, still lives in people.

The deception consists in this: that the inner force of our own and immediate understanding and relation to God is forcibly diminished in our life from outside and concealed by substitution with every possible social institution; or in this: that some people, being in reality the same as everyone else, have thought up exalting their sinful, anti-human, bestial business of violence into a holy right and, making use of this invention, have appropriated to themselves a position of exceptional rationality and a supposed representation before God.

This worldly deception, from which we simply cannot come to our senses, began long ago, back in the Old Testament. From the Old Testament we know that the Jews, among whom Christ appeared (whose faith, we boast, we supposedly profess)—the Jews differed from all other peoples in that they worshipped the true God, that the worship of the true God was expressed not in some humiliating and ruinous actions, such as bowing the head down to the ground, renouncing one's will, conscience, human dignity, all one's own life to the last drop of blood, as we do now before our gods, but that the worship of the true God consisted in people's fulfilling the laws of life, expressed for the Jew in the ten commandments, the first of which says that people should know the true God and not make themselves any other gods; in this consisted the first, distinguishing property of the people of God.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 400 --> But the Jews, as a people of their time, could not constantly keep to that height of life on which they had been so inimitably placed by Moses; little by little they were tempted, fell, worshipped foreign gods, and through this their life became disordered, became unbearably calamitous, and they repented and returned to the true God; but they ruined their Jewish good completely, beyond return, by making themselves an idol—an invention.

The eighth chapter of the First Book of Samuel tells how the elders of the Jews gathered to the prophet Samuel and demanded that he give them a king. Scripture tells that these words of the people displeased the prophet Samuel, and he inquired of the Lord, and the Lord answered him with a great, eternal word. He said: “It is not you they have rejected, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them. So, what is to be done, if they have rejected me; they are doing this not for the first time. Listen to their voices, give them a king, only set forth and declare to them the rights of the king who will reign over them.” And Samuel said to the people: “Both your sons and your daughters and your best lands the king will take and give to his servants, and you yourselves will be his slaves, and you will cry out then because of your king, and the Lord will not answer you then. The Lord your God is your king!” But the people did not listen, and Samuel gave them a king. Thus Israel rejected and lost the freedom given to it by God through Moses, for which he had led it out of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.

Before this, in the book of Judges and in the book of Ruth, it was written: “In those days there was no king in Israel; each one did what seemed just to him.” But under a king, it follows, this order ceased: each one stopped doing what he found just, and did not try to find out what is just and what is unjust, because for that there is the king. Perhaps it does seem that this is better, but however wise kings may be and however perfect their laws, they can never replace the living reason in man, and one cannot allow both the one and the other, because the one displaces and destroys the other, and because “no one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be zealous for the one and neglect the other.”

So it was with a king that there began in the Old Testament the worldly deception that tormented the Jewish people for thousands of years and from which we now, for other thousands of years, cannot wake up.

*Buka.*

## April

What are governments in our time, without which it seems to people impossible to exist?

If there was a time when governments were a necessary and lesser evil than the one that came from defenselessness <!-- vol. 42, p. 401 --> against organized neighbors, now governments have become an unnecessary and far greater evil than all that with which they frighten their peoples.

Governments, not only military ones but governments in general, could be, I will not say—useful, but harmless, only if they consisted of infallible, holy people, as is in fact supposed among the Chinese. But governments, after all, by their very activity, which consists in committing violence, always consist of the elements most opposite to holiness, of the most insolent, coarse, and depraved people.

Every government, therefore, and all the more a government to which military power is entrusted, is a terrible institution, the most dangerous in the world.

Government, in the broadest sense, including in it both capitalists and the press, is nothing other than an organization in which the greater part of people is in the power of a smaller part standing above them; and this smaller part submits to the power of a still smaller part, and that to a still smaller one, and so on, reaching at last a few people or one man, who by means of military violence obtain power over all the rest. So that this whole structure is like a cone, all the parts of which are in the full power of those persons or that person who is at its apex.

And the apex of this cone is seized by those people or that man who is more cunning, insolent, and unscrupulous than the others, or by a chance heir of those who were more insolent and unscrupulous.

Today Boris Godunov, tomorrow Grigory Otrepyev, today the dissolute Catherine, who with her lovers strangled her husband, tomorrow Pugachev, the day after tomorrow the mad Paul, Nicholas I, Alexander II, today Nicholas II with the Chinese-Japanese war. Today Napoleon, tomorrow a Bourbon or an Orléans, Boulanger or the company of Panamists, today Gladstone, tomorrow Salisbury, Chamberlain, Rhodes.

And it is to such governments that full power is given, not only over property and life, but also over the spiritual and moral development, over the upbringing, the religious guidance of all people.

People set up for themselves such a terrible machine—power—leaving it to whoever comes along to seize this power (and all the odds are that it will be seized by the most morally worthless man), and they slavishly submit and are surprised that things are bad for them... They fear mines, anarchists, and do not fear this terrible structure, which threatens them every minute with the greatest calamities.

People have found that, in order to defend themselves against enemies, it is useful for them to bind themselves, as the Circassians do when they defend themselves. But there is no danger at all, and people go on binding themselves.

They carefully bind themselves so that one end can do with all of them whatever it wants; then they throw the end of the rope that binds <!-- vol. 42, p. 402 --> them to dangle loose, leaving it to the first scoundrel or fool to seize it and do with them whatever they need.

For what else but this very thing are peoples doing when they submit to, establish, and maintain a government organized with military power?

*L. Tolstoy.*

## May

## VOLUNTARY SERVITUDE, A WORK OF THE FRENCH WRITER LA BOÉTIE, OF THE MIDDLE OF THE 16TH CENTURY

It is reasonable to love virtue, to respect feats, to acknowledge good from wherever we have received it, and even to give up one's own comfort for the glory and advantage of one whom one loves and who deserves it: thus, if the inhabitants of a country have found such a person, who has shown them great wisdom in guarding them, great courage in defending them, and great care in governing them; and if as a result they have grown used to obeying him so far as to grant him certain advantages, then I do not think that this was unreasonable.

But, my God! What shall we call it when we see that a great number of people not only obey but serve, not only submit but grovel before one man, and grovel so that they have nothing of their own: neither property, nor children, nor even life itself, which they could consider their own, and they endure robberies and cruelties, not from an army, not from barbarians, but from one man, and not from a Hercules or a Samson, but from a man who is for the most part the most cowardly and effeminate of the whole people. What shall we call this? Shall we say that such people are cowards? If two, three, four did not defend themselves against one, that would be strange, but still possible, and one could say that it comes from a lack of courage; but if a hundred thousand people, a hundred thousand villages, a million people do not attack that one man from whom all suffer, being his slaves, what shall we call this? Is this cowardice?

In all vices there is a certain limit: two may fear one, and even ten; but a thousand, but a million, but a thousand villages, if they do not defend themselves against one, then this is not cowardice, it cannot go that far; just as courage too cannot go so far that one man should take a fortress, attack an army, and conquer a state. So what monstrous vice is this, then, not deserving even the name of cowardice, a vice for which one cannot find a vile enough name, which is contrary to nature and which language refuses to name.

We marvel at the courage that freedom inspires in those who defend it. But what is done in all countries with all people every day, namely that one man <!-- vol. 42, p. 403 --> rules over a hundred thousand villages and deprives them of freedom; who would believe this if he only heard it and did not see it. And if this could be seen only in foreign and distant lands, who would not think that it was rather an invention than the truth. For that one man who oppresses all need not be conquered, there is no need to defend oneself against him; he is always conquered, if only the people do not consent to slavery. There is no need to take anything from him; one need only give him nothing. A country need not do anything, if only it does nothing against itself, and the people will be free. So it is the peoples themselves who give themselves into the power of sovereigns; they need only stop being slaves, and they will be free. The peoples themselves give themselves into slavery, cut their own throats. A people that can be free itself gives up its freedom, itself puts the yoke on its neck, itself not only consents to its oppression but seeks it. If regaining its freedom cost it something, and it did not seek it, this natural right, the most precious to man, which distinguishes man from the animal, then I understand that it might prefer safety and the comfort of life to the struggle for freedom. But if, in order to obtain freedom, it need only wish for it, can there really be a people in the world that would consider it bought at too dear a price, if it can be acquired by a wish alone? Man can, by means of a wish alone, regain a good that could be bought back at the price of his blood and which, if it is lost, then life becomes a torment and death a salvation, and he does not wish this. As a fire from a single spark becomes great and grows ever stronger the more firewood it finds, goes out by itself if only no more firewood is put on, destroys itself and loses its form and ceases to be fire; in the same way rulers too, the more they rob, the more they demand, the more they ruin and destroy, the more they are given and served, the more they gain in strength, become stronger and greedier for the destruction of everything; whereas if they are given nothing, if they are not obeyed, then without struggle, without battle, they become naked and insignificant, become nothing, just as a tree that has no sap and no nourishment becomes a dry and dead branch.

Bold people do not fear danger, so as to acquire the good they desire; intelligent ones do not refuse labor. If cowardly people do not know how to bear suffering and acquire the good, they do not strive for it because of their cowardice, but the desire to have it remains in them. This desire is proper to the wise and to the unreasonable, to the brave and to cowards alike. All of them desire to acquire those things that will make them happy and content; only one of them, I do not know why, people do not desire, namely—freedom, which is such a great good that as soon as it is lost all other calamities <!-- vol. 42, p. 404 --> follow after it, and even those goods which remain after it lose their taste and their charm. And it is this great good, which people would receive as soon as they wished for it, that they do not wish to acquire, as if only because it is too easy.

Poor, unhappy people, senseless peoples, stubborn in your evil, blind to your good, you allow the best part of your income to be taken from you, your fields and your houses to be plundered; you live in such a way that you can say that nothing belongs to you. And all these calamities and this ruin come not from enemies, but from an enemy whom you make for yourselves, for whom you courageously go to war, for whose greatness you do not refuse to go to your death. He who rules over you so has only two eyes, two hands, one body, and has nothing that an insignificant man among the countless number of your brothers does not have; the advantage he has over you is only that right which you give him: to destroy you. Where would he get so many eyes to watch you if you did not give them to him? How would he have so many hands to beat you if he did not take them from you? Or where would he get the feet with which he tramples your villages—where does he get them, if they are not yours? Where would he get power over you if you did not give it to him? How could he attack you if you were not in league with him? What could he do to you if you were not harborers of the thief who robs you, accomplices of the murderer who kills you, if you were not traitors to yourselves? You sow so that he may destroy your crops; you fill and furnish your houses for his plunderings; you bring up your children so that he may lead them to his wars, to the slaughter, so that he may make them the executors of his lusts, of his vengeances; you wear yourselves out with labor so that he may enjoy pleasures and wallow in dirty and vile pleasures; you weaken yourselves in order to make him stronger and so that he can keep you in the bridle. And from these horrors, which animals would not endure, you can free yourselves if you want not even to free yourselves, but only to wish for it. Resolve to serve no more, and you are free. I do not want you to fight with him, to attack him, but only to stop supporting him, and you will see that he, like a huge statue from beneath which the base has been taken away, will fall of his own weight and shatter to pieces.

## June

On 27 January 1894, in the hospital of the Voronezh prison, a certain Drozhzhin, a former village schoolteacher of Kursk province, died of pneumonia. His body was thrown into a grave in the prison <!-- vol. 42, p. 405 --> cemetery, as the bodies of all criminals who die in prison are thrown there. And yet this was one of the most holy, pure, and truthful people that there are in life.

In August 1891 he was called up to perform his military duty, but, considering all people brothers and recognizing killing and violence as the greatest sin, contrary to conscience and to the will of God, he refused to be a soldier and to bear arms. In just the same way, recognizing it as a sin to give up his will into the power of other people, who might demand bad actions of him, he also refused to take the oath. People whose life is based on violence and killing first confined him for a year in solitary confinement in Kharkov, and then transferred him to the Voronezh disciplinary battalion, where for 15 months they tormented him with cold, hunger, and solitary confinement. At last, when from continual sufferings and privations he developed consumption and was declared unfit for military service, they decided to transfer him to a civil prison, where he was to serve another 9 years of imprisonment. But while he was being taken from the battalion to the prison, on a day of hard frost, the police officers through their negligence took him without warm clothing, stood a long time in the street outside the police station, and so chilled him that he developed pneumonia, of which he died 23 days later.

The day before his death Drozhzhin said to the doctor: “Although I have not lived long, I die with the consciousness that I acted according to my convictions, in accordance with my conscience. Of course, others can judge of this better. Perhaps... no, I think that I am right,” he said affirmatively.

The next day he died.

*Ye. I. Popov.*

## LETTER OF THE MILITARY DOCTOR A. ŠKARVAN, WHO REFUSED MILITARY SERVICE IN 1894.

Mr. Senior Doctor!

I ought to have told you orally what I am writing, but I am making use of the pen, since I fear that in person I might not do it clearly and calmly enough.

I have decided not to return any more to my military duties, I have decided to stop being a soldier, and consequently I will neither wear the military uniform nor perform hospital service, which is in essence the same military service.

I refuse this because it contradicts my convictions, my way of thinking, my knowledge, my religious feeling. I am a Christian and, as such, cannot further militarism either by word or by deed. Until now I have done this because I did not have enough spiritual strength <!-- vol. 42, p. 406 --> to stand alone against so mighty a force as military organization represents. Now my decision has grown firm, and this did not happen in some impassioned moment, but it is the consistent result of my thoughts and strivings over the course of several years.

I can clearly picture how stupid, sinful, and ridiculous my intention must seem to the military court. I also know that for this I shall have to undergo heavy punishment—that the authorities will keep me in prison confinement as long as they please.

But I give myself over to a power that is higher than all mighty Europe. I want to bring my life into accord with the demands of truth alone, that is, of the eternal, one, divine truth. This truth commands me no longer to bend my neck under that universal slavish yoke of soldiering which all governments at the present time lay upon mankind.

That the military doctor must pursue, as is said and written about this, more humane and noble aims, I consider untrue, because he, like other military men, is nothing other than an instrument deprived of will, existing in order to do correctly, well, and consistently what the regulations require, namely to take care that the army may be able as long as possible to carry out its crude, inhuman work.

That is all I have to say. I ask that this letter be kept so that it can be handed over to the court, since there too I shall hardly have anything to add to what is said in it.

I shall await your orders at my quarters in the “Kronen Kaserne.”

*Dr. A. Škarvan.*

Besides this letter, A. Škarvan set out in the following article the reasons for which he found it impossible to serve as a military doctor.

## WHY ONE CANNOT SERVE AS A MILITARY DOCTOR

Many are struck by the fact that I refused to continue military service as a doctor. Many allow that a refusal of service in the ranks is still understandable, since the purpose of a soldier in the ranks undoubtedly consists in learning to kill and, if his superiors demand it, in committing killings as well.

“But,” people ask, “how can a military doctor, whose calling and duty consist not at all in killing people but, on the contrary, in giving help to the sick and suffering and, consequently, in doing humane deeds, deeds of mercy, refuse his service?”

<!-- vol. 42, p. 407 --> “The activity of a doctor,” such people add further, “is in itself a Christian activity, and therefore whoever abandons this activity deserves, even from the moral point of view, condemnation rather than sympathy.”

And people, with the general inclination proper to them not to look into the real state of things, readily accept reasonings of this kind, consider the question settled, and lay it aside so as not to think about it any more. I have had to hear such objections not only from military men, but equally from civilians, not only from materialists, but even from undoubtedly religious people. This very thing was expressed to me even by some Nazarenes, people who have understood the sinfulness of military service, who refuse it, and who for this conviction have spent their whole youth sitting in prisons and are dying there.

The question arises: how can the opinion of the Nazarenes agree with the opinion of people with quite other views on war? And another question: is this opinion right?

For me it is beyond doubt that the Nazarenes resorted to a subtler, but in essence the very same, self-deception when they decided among themselves to enter—if the authorities demand it—the medical companies, as they now do. In this lies the whole explanation.

But to assert that the service of a military doctor, and with it the service of a medical orderly, is not contrary to the spirit of Christ, that such service constitutes a kind of virtue, is a very crude error. The error lies in this: that any work and occupation can be made a work of the devil (as the practice of many doctors proves); everything depends only on how the one doing a given work relates to it. Therefore the assertion is also untrue that a doctor's occupation in general is in itself a noble occupation. Besides this, the question is further confused by the circumstance that the vast majority of people regard medical science superstitiously, not even suspecting how true Faust's saying is: “Der Sinn der Medizin ist leicht zu fassen; du durchstudirst die grosse und die kleine Welt, um es am Ende gehn zu lassen, wie's Gott gefällt.” (The essence of medicine is easy to grasp; you study the great and the small world only in order in the end to leave everything to God's will.)

But the main thing that makes the service of a military doctor criminal is that close connection which exists between his activity and the killing of people—the real purpose of armies. This connection is hypocritically covered with the cloak of humaneness and is therefore not so evident to people. Nevertheless it exists, and anyone who wishes to see it can see it, for it is very easy to lift this cloak, under which the same robber is hiding.

The military doctor examines soldiers, that is, decides which of the men are fit for cannon fodder and which are not; examines those soldiers whom their superiors punish, that is, decides whom one may shut up in a dungeon, whom one may put in fetters, <!-- vol. 42, p. 408 --> whom one may deprive of food, and the like; consequently, he constantly assists inhuman, bestial violence against people.

But let us suppose even that he will not be compelled to do any of this and that, apart from conscientiously treating sick soldiers, he will occupy himself with nothing else—even this would not in the least do away with the sinfulness of his activity, *for one cannot help asking oneself, one cannot help seeing, what aim is pursued by this treatment. The military doctor in any case represents a hireling for money, hired by an organized gang of murderers solely in order to watch over the health of people destined for slaughter and for the commission of murder.*

And in this case one cannot help being conscious of what everyone knows—that to be an accomplice, in any form whatever, of an immoral, savage institution is shameful and humiliating, whatever good name may be given to this work, however handsome a uniform one may put on for it, however many little gold crosses may be bestowed for such service. Surely no honest woman would agree for any money to become a cook for a gang of robbers, although the preparation of food not only does not constitute a sin in itself, but is a needful and necessary condition of people's life. And what, then, is the difference between robbers and armies? Solely the scale of the robbery.

It is time for all of us to understand that it is shameful and humiliating to sell one's knowledge to those who need it for the easier attainment of their evil intentions!

It is time to understand that every least assistance in the affairs of government that are founded on violence constitutes, for a person who wishes the good for himself and for others—a humiliation of his own dignity and a great crime against the most elementary demands not only of love, but even of simple humaneness!

## August

Recently a police inspector comes to me with a constable and two attesting witnesses and says: “Mr. District Police Chief, in commission with the military commander, sent me to invite you to the police administration in the matter of your failure to appear for the checking of the lists of those called up for military duty.” I was a little taken aback and faltered in my words, and he added: “They are <!-- vol. 42, p. 409 --> waiting for you in the police administration now.” I say: “Why are they waiting, then? I did not promise to come.”

— But they are asking you—you understand: the district police chief and the military commander sent me.

— True, — I say, — but I for my part have no need of them at all.

— So you won't go?

— Ye-es! I won't go.

— So you do not recognize the authorities, you are going against authority?

— I am not going against the authorities; let the authorities stand, perhaps someone or other does need them, but I for my part have no need of them at all. Here you say so menacingly: “the district police chief, the military commander” and “against the authorities,” but I understand that these too are people, born like me from a mother's womb, drinking, eating, sinning, and dying, in a word, like all people. And agree for yourself: why should I go to a person whom I have no need of at all? But if, as you say, they need me, well, then let them come to me.

— Well, yes, — said the policeman: — but if this is allowed, then no one will go, and through this the authorities will be abolished—so, you are going against the authorities?

— But understand what I am saying: I do not need them. If I do some work and people do not need it, then how can I make them need my work. It is the same with authority. I do not need it. If I am mistaken, then of course the authorities will not be abolished because I, some insignificant man, have made a mistake. But if, as you fear, the authorities in this way will be needed by no one and through this will be abolished, then what is to be done; such, then, will be the will of God.

— So you won't go, then?

— Well, of course!

— In that case, goodbye!

— Be well.

The evil of the authorities is that they are violent. To be rid of violence, one must learn to get along with one another in a good way, without needing violence. And this can be achieved only by conscious faith, by the understanding of life, in fulfilling the will of our father, who art in heaven, that is, of the eternal law. Let every person, about every deed that lies before him, first make sure by his reason whether it accords with the will of God or does not accord. And if it accords, then let him do it; but if it does not accord, then let him not only not do it, but not stir a finger, not utter a single sound for the benefit of such a deed. And then what is not needed will be abolished of itself by the will of God. What will be untied on earth will come untied in heaven too. The wisdom of life consists in keeping the will of God, in being able to repay evil with good.

*Buka.*

## REVELATION AND REASON

See the weekly reading after 27 October, pp. 176–180.

## October

The horrors of all the religious persecutions before Christ and after Christ laid open before us by history, from Nero's torches to the Inquisition, St. Bartholomew's Night, the two-edged sword of Islam, and down to the persecutions, tortures, and killings of our Russian sectarians being committed now—all these horrors testify so convincingly to the madness of violence and resistance for the purposes of spiritual unification that it is hard to add anything to these bloody testimonies.

But the most important, the highest understanding, which covers all other reasonings, consists in this: that people live on earth not in order to do their own will, but in order to fulfill the will of God, which, besides being expressed in what we recognize as sacred tradition, is laid in every person's reason and heart. All these kingdoms, governments, courts, police forces, and laws are expressions of the deceptive human will that people should all live as certain people imagine to be desirable, but it always turns out not that way, according to the human timetable, but the way that pleases God. Insofar as human ordinances do not hinder people from fulfilling the will of God, to that extent they bring good, happiness, and order into people's life, and the rule-makers flatter themselves that it is all so good as if everything came from them. Insofar as human ordinances are contrary to the will of God, to that extent they bring evil, unhappiness, and disorder into people's life, and the stupidity and ungodliness of the rule-makers come to light. Is it not better and is it not simpler to take care of one thing: to make the will of God itself the object of universal knowledge, clarification, and exaltation in place of all the numerous, intricate, and often incomprehensible and senseless human institutions and laws? People who wish to make their laws binding on people only muddy human life by this and draw them away from fulfilling the will of God. “Seek first the kingdom of God and his justice, and all the rest will be added,” said Christ. After all, it was Moses who told his stiff-necked people that God himself, with his own finger, inscribed the commandments on the stone tablets, and our priests, too, still fool the children with their holy spirit, but we have long since stopped believing this; and about our own lawmakers we know for certain that they do not climb Sinai and get their inspiration for laws no farther away than their own studies, departments, and taverns. And if so, then what are <!-- vol. 42, p. 411 --> they guided by in composing laws?—at best, of course, by nothing other than the reason inherent in every person. So would it not be better, instead of big, complicated inventions—social institutions—to use reason itself directly as the instrument for attaining every social good? Reason has been given us by God not in order to deceive us; it consists only in this, it has been given us only for this: to lead us out of error and to guide us to the knowledge of the will of God. Do us this kindness, gentlemen rulers and lawmakers: understand that we all walk under God, we are all the same people, and we all have reason, and your violence is both unnecessary, and senseless, and outrageous, and, above all, infectious and ruinous for us by its ruling example.

*A. Buka.*

## November

## INDUSTRY AND IDLENESS, OR THE TRIUMPH OF THE FARMER A WORK BY THE PEASANT T. BONDAREV

## PREFACE

How strange and wild it would have seemed to the refinedly educated Romans of the first half of the 1st century if someone had told them that the unclear, confused, often absurd letters of a wandering Jew to his friends and disciples would be read a hundred, a thousand, hundreds of thousands of times more, be more widely spread, and have more influence on people than all the poems, odes, elegies, and elegant epistles of the writers of that time so loved by refined people. And yet this is what happened with the epistles of Paul.

In just the same way my present assertion must seem strange and wild to people: that the work of Bondarev, at whose naïveté we smile condescendingly from the height of our intellectual grandeur, will outlive all those works described in the histories of Russian literature and will produce more influence on people than all of them taken together. And yet I am sure that it will be so. And I am sure of this because, just as there is a countless number of false paths, leading nowhere and therefore unnecessary, but only one true path, leading to the goal and therefore necessary, so there is a countless number of false thoughts, necessary for nothing, but only one true, necessary thought, or rather one true and necessary course of thought, and this one true and necessary course of thought is set forth in our time by Bondarev in his work with such extraordinary force, clarity, and conviction as no one has yet set it forth with. And therefore everything that seems so important and necessary now will vanish without a trace and be forgotten, but what Bondarev says and what <!-- vol. 42, p. 412 --> he calls people to will not be forgotten, because people will be brought by life itself more and more to what he says.

The discovery of all abstract scientific and applied scientific, and philosophical, and moral, and economic truths is always accomplished in this way: people walk in narrower and narrower circles around these truths, coming nearer and nearer to them, and sometimes only slightly catching hold of them, until a bold, free, and gifted man points out the very center of this truth and sets it at that height from which it is visible to all.

And this very thing Bondarev has done with regard to the moral-economic truth that was due to be discovered and clarified in our time.

Many have said and are saying the same thing. Some consider physical labor necessary for health, others—for a proper economic order, a third group—for the normal development of the all-round faculties of man, a fourth consider it a necessary condition for the moral perfection of man. Thus, for example, one of the greatest writers of England and of our time, almost as unappreciated by the cultured crowd of our time as our Bondarev, even though Ruskin is the most educated and most refined man of his time, that is, one standing at the pole opposite to Bondarev—this Ruskin says: “It is physically impossible that true religious knowledge or pure morality should exist among a class of the people that does not earn its bread with its own hands.”

Many walk around this truth and utter it with various reservations, as Ruskin does, but no one does what Bondarev does, recognizing bread labor as the fundamental religious law of life. And he does this not because (as we like to think) he is an ignorant and stupid peasant who does not know all that we know, but because he is a man of genius who knows that truth is truth only when it is expressed not with cuts and reservations and coverings, but when it is expressed completely.

Just as the truth that the sum of the angles of a triangle is equal to two right angles, expressed so that the sum of the angles of a triangle is sometimes approximately equal to two right angles, loses all meaning and significance, so the truth that a man *must* work with his own hands, expressed in the form of advice, of desirability, of an assertion that this may be useful in certain respects, and the like, loses all its meaning and its significance. This truth receives meaning and significance only when it is expressed as an immutable law, departure from which brings inevitable calamities and sufferings in its train, and the fulfillment of which is required of us by God or by reason, as Bondarev has expressed it.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 413 --> Bondarev does not demand that everyone without fail put on bast shoes and go walking behind the plow, though he does say that this would be desirable and would free people sunk in luxury from the errors that torment them (and indeed nothing but good would come even from the exact fulfillment of even this demand), but Bondarev says that every person must consider the duty of physical labor, of direct participation in those labors whose fruits he uses, his first, chief, unquestionable sacred duty, and that people must be brought up in such a consciousness of this duty. And I cannot imagine how an honest and thinking person can fail to agree with this.

*Leo Tolstoy.*

## INDUSTRY AND IDLENESS

> “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread, until thou return into the earth, from which thou wast taken.”

> (Genesis 3:19).

I write in the name of all farmers, and to all of you, however many of you there are in the world, who do not work bread for yourselves.

You, the upper class, have written thousands of books. Is there not plenty in them that is out of place and even harmful? And in spite of that, they have all been accepted, approved, and published.

But we, the lower class, for our part have written one short, genuine tale—this in all the ages and eternities—in our own defense, and you will reject it for nothing more than a lack of eloquence and for bad handwriting—so many people have assured me. This will be an insult of the highest degree for us—and also, it seems to me, for God.

My whole story consists in only two words: first, why do you not, according to the original commandment, work bread for yourselves with your own hands, but eat up other people's labors? Second, why in your writings, neither theological, nor civil, nor any other, are bread labor and the one who toils at it not approved, but humiliated to the utmost?

I divide the whole world into two circles: one of them exalted and respected, and the other humiliated and rejected. The first, richly dressed and sitting majestically in a place of honor at a table filled with sweets—this is the rich man; and the second—in rags, worn out by dry fare and the heaviest work, standing at the threshold before him with humiliation and a pitiful look—these are the poor farmers. The truth of my word is confirmed by the Gospel parable (Luke 16:20).

<!-- vol. 42, p. 414 --> Adam, for transgressing the commandment given him by God not to taste the fruits of the forbidden tree, not only lost bliss himself, but subjected all his future race until the end of the age to the same calamity. From this it is clear that he committed the greatest iniquity of all iniquities, and in no way literally ate an apple.

Then he began to hide in the bushes of that garden, as the holy scripture tells us: “And Adam and his wife hid themselves in the midst of the tree of paradise.”

And from whom was he hiding?—there were no people then. From God, of course.

There, you see what madness sin casts a man into. Why, can one really hide from God?

From this it is clear that he, having weighed his crime, expected to receive from God a punishment beyond all measure; but beyond his expectation he received this divine sentence: “for transgressing the commandment I gave you, here is your punishment: ‘in the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread, until thou return into the earth, from which thou wast taken.’”

Did not Adam then, in the course of his life (930 years), work bread for himself with his own hands to the point of bloody sweat, carrying out the penance laid on him?

And you, the upper class, are a branch of that same root; why then do you not want, in all your life, to come even near this penance, but eat many times a day? It would be one thing if you were as neglected as I and farmers like me. No, you are this much (pointing with my hand above my head) cleverer and more educated, and yet what a great crime you commit before God and people.

God said to Eve: “Multiplying I will multiply thy sorrows and thy sighings (what a terrible saying), in pains shalt thou bring forth thy children.”

Now I ask why in the woman's penance there are no secret evasions and allegories, but just as God said, so everything literally comes to pass.

For the woman living in a wretched hut, as for the queen sitting on a throne and having a crown on her head, it is one and the same lot: “in pains shalt thou bring forth thy children.” There is not the slightest difference.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 415 --> Yes! In pains to such a degree that she lies half-dead for days at a time, and sometimes dies altogether.

But now this eminent woman might say this: “I have no time to give birth; I am busy with needed and necessary affairs of state, and by giving birth I will bring the state more loss than benefit. And also for this reason: is it fitting for me to put myself on a level with the lowest peasant woman, with a common peasant woman? So I had better hire another woman for money to give birth to a child for me, or buy a ready-made child for money, and he will be my own, just like the one I would give birth to myself.” Could she reason so and do so? No, the ordinance of God cannot be changed.

Gather treasures from the whole world and give them for a child, and it will not be yours; as it was someone else's, so it will remain someone else's to you. Whose is it, then? Why, the mother's, the one who gave birth to it. So too with the man: he too can refuse bread work and buy one pound of bread with money; but as it was someone else's, so it will stay someone else's. Whose is it, then? Why, his who worked it. Because, just as God has laid it down that a woman must not shield herself with money or any evasions from bearing children, so a man must work bread with his own hands for himself, and for his wife, and for his children, and not shield himself with money or other evasions, regardless of any rank.

To woman it was said by God himself: “Not to work bread, but to bear children in pains.” Why then do our wives work? Rather than wait for your answer, reader, I make it myself.

You who eat up the bread of our labors will be found in Russia to number up to 30 million, and if, besides, according to the commandments, your wives are removed from this labor, what will come of it then? why, only one thing: the whole world must perish by starvation. So now it has been revealed to us clearly and lawfully that our wives work bread for your share and for you, white-hands, against the commandment. You eat up their labors.

A woman who has killed the fruit of her womb repents all her life, sighs from the depths of her soul and asks God for forgiveness, and in old age lays fasts and prayers on herself, by which, one may think, she will win from God by prayer forgiveness for destroying her commandment.

Do you repent, reader, of eating the bread of other people's labors all your life! Do you ask God and people for forgiveness? Never and not in the least; why, it does not even enter your mind; you have boldly relied on money, and you live at your ease all your days and consider yourself fully in the right before God.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 416 --> If this original commandment, uttered by God himself, which is the mother and parent of all virtues and the giver of temporal and eternal, earthly and heavenly goods, were accepted and respected by you, then bread labor would be loved so much that many fathers would leave their children such a testament: “When I draw near to death, take me out to the grain field, so that there my soul may part from my body; and in it bury my dust.”

But now what? Those who work expect no reward for themselves from God, and those who devour other people's labors expect no punishment.

If, I repeat again, this commandment were accepted and respected by you, what great encouragement to bread labor you would give the farmers by yourselves! They would apply such care that one desyatina would bring in as much as five do now.

If we all shirked it, then you have the right to compel us to it by force; but if you have gone away from it, or, to put it more truly, from him who commanded it, like the prodigal son of the Gospel from his father, then who will compel you to it?

And why such lowliness upon us?

Was God not able to choose another way of bringing bread into the world, and yet he laid the penance in it for our sins, that is, since a man cannot live out his life in the world without sin, and cannot live without bread, he as it were compelled us willy-nilly to be rid of our sins?

And you have thrown such a precious medicine under your feet, as was said above, laid it in the coffin, so that no one living on earth could find it; and instead you have laid it down that by faith alone in the one God, without bearing labors, one can be saved.

Why, the devil too believes that there is one God, and obeys him, as we see in Job, chapter II.

Why, if only you had counted bread labor among the little virtues—you did not grant it even that; you might at least have made it the tail instead of the head, you did not vouchsafe it even that.

You will be punished by God heavily and without the slightest mercy for having laid this commandment for so many thousands of years under heavy oppression and made a dead thing out of a living being.

However many different evil deeds and great crimes there are in the world, such as theft, murder, robberies, frauds, bribes, and extortions of every kind, the cause of it all is that this commandment is hidden from people.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 417 --> The rich man does all this with the aim of not coming near this vile occupation, and the poor man in order to be rid of it. So set this commandment before the eyes of the whole world in all its force and dignity, then in a short time every evil deed will cease and people will be delivered from heavy poverty and unbearable destitution.

Not in vain did God in the beginning appoint no virtues except bread labor, and command keeping away from no vices except running away from it.

From this it is clear that this labor has gathered all the virtues into itself. On the contrary, laziness and idleness have appropriated all the vices. And if there is now an evildoer among the farmers, it is because he does not know this law. But at the same time one must not lose sight of the fact that other labors too are a virtue, but only together with bread, that is, after eating one's fill of the bread of one's own labors.

Bread labor is a sacred duty for each and every one, and no excuses should be taken into consideration: the higher a man is, the more he must show himself an example to others in this labor, and not shield himself with some evasions, or hide from it behind various corners.

I draw my proofs here from theology for this reason, that apart from theology I have nothing from which to take proofs about this labor.

And second, because people of our class believe strongly in God, in the future life, in holy scripture. Having heard all this, they, like those who hunger for bread and like those who thirst for water, will strive toward this labor, and then toward all labors.

Then for them dark night will be bright day, rain—fair weather, mud—dry, frost—warm, snowstorm—calm, decrepit old age—blooming youth, infirmity—full health.

Bread may not be sold, and bought, and traded in, and riches made from it, because its value goes beyond the limits of human reason. In extreme and valid cases it must be given free, namely: to hospitals, to orphanages, to those sitting in prisons, to regions exhausted by crop failure, to those ruined by fire, to widows, orphans, and cripples, to the decrepit and the homeless.

The farmer is moved to great mercifulness with bread by the voice of nature and the aforementioned commandment.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 418 --> But if, on top of this mercifulness, he could penetrate into the depth of its mysteries, then everything said in the preceding question would be fulfilled. Then one would not ask another: give me bread, but would ask: accept bread from me; and he would hardly find anyone willing to eat other people's labors.

How many thousands of poods of wheat, how many grains, how many silver rubles are collected from us each year in taxes, excises, duties, and levies of every kind.

Besides that, the gentlemen landowners, the merchants, and all the rich have uncountable millions; but money is not given for nothing; it must be worked out with bloody calluses, according to the said commandment, with hands, and not with tongue and pen.

I ask: whose hands toiled over this money? Ours, of course. And into whose does it go? Into yours, of course—the white ones—for your luxuries. In a word, the whole world rests on our hands.

This is an insult of the highest degree for us, and for you a humiliation.

I know that you are a hundred times cleverer and more educated than I, and that is why you take money and bread from me. But if you are clever, then you must have mercy on me, the weak one, as it is said: “love your neighbor as yourself,” and I, after all, am your neighbor, and you are mine.

Why are we poor and stupid?—because we ourselves eat bread from our own labors and feed you. Do we have time to study and get educated? Just like our bread, so together with it our reason too you have either secretly stolen, or brazenly carried off, or craftily appropriated.

Before dinner you ought to ask a blessing not of God but of us farmers. And after dinner give thanks not to God but to us.

If God sent you manna from heaven, as to Israel in the wilderness, then you would have to give thanks to him; but if it comes through our hands, then to us, because we feed you like little children or cripples.

Can it be that God gave this heavy commandment to us alone, and ordered you to shield yourselves from it with money?

— “For me, — says the rich man, — money works bread.”

You lie—money has not sinned before God, that is why no commandment has been laid on it. And it does not eat bread either, so it is not obliged to work. So how can you say: “for me money works.”

<!-- vol. 42, p. 419 --> You also wave off bread labor by saying that there is little land as it is, and if everyone works, where is it to be found (all the more since, once you start working, you will plow up all the land).

You also recognize as a lawful defense for yourselves this excuse from bread labor: I have one task, and I cannot tear myself in two to manage both here and there; take up farming—then there is no time to think about the other task.

I ask: I have, besides bread, many other tasks; how then do I manage to think and to carry them out in deed, I, an uneducated peasant? And if I had as much mind and education as you, I would then carry out a thousand tasks. Why then do you have such an abyss of reason, and yet cannot even think about another task besides one?

They say: another man toils twenty times more than a farmer—can he be called an idler?

*For 335 days a year work at what you want and occupy yourself with what you know, but for 30 days in different seasons of the year every man must work bread.*

If God had laid on you for your sins such a commandment: Take a stone of a hundred poods and carry it—you would say: I cannot do this, you did not give me that much strength; and again: fly through the air like a bird—you did not give me wings, so I cannot do this, and so on.

Such a justification is valid.

But why can you not work bread? Of course, only for this reason: “Who am I? My hands are white and tender, and bread is prickly.”

You also shield yourself from bread labor with this: that whatever anyone is occupied with, it all comes under that commandment: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread.”

One of you says: “Today I wrote a few lines, so I eat bread in the sweat of my face.”

Another: “Today I gave people several spoken orders, so that they would work diligently for me, therefore I eat bread in the sweat of my face.”

A third: “Today I took a drive around the city in a rich carriage, and I too eat bread in the sweat of my face.”

A fourth: “Today I sold rotten goods for good, cheap for dear, and cheated inexperienced people, therefore I too eat bread in the sweat of my face.”

And the thief in his turn says: “I did not sleep all night, I worked with my own hands, I eat bread in the sweat of my face more than all of you” and so on.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 420 --> If not by truth, then by eloquence and cunning everyone comes out in the right, as Krylov says: “All those beasts that are rich in claws and teeth, all of them came out in the right, almost saints, but against the meek ox they raised talk, the tigers and wolves shout, and they strangled him and heaved him onto the pyre.”

From all the preceding questions it is clear that there is no more loathsome uncleanness in the world than the bread of other people's labors. On the contrary, there is no holy thing more soul-saving than the bread of one's own labors. This I say not by guesswork, but according to the fundamental divine laws, with which our natural law also agrees.

O have mercy on yourself, high-society class. Do not give this matter over to destruction. And if there is anything here gravely against the law, then better destroy me alone, and put this matter away for all time in the archive where the most important state documents are kept. It may be that in later generations a man will be found just enough to publish it in its whole present form. Let me alone perish, and let millions of farmers receive the greatest joy and relief in their labors.

In the whole world the complaint against God is this: If there is infinite mercy in God, then where do the countless calamities come from to which poor people are subjected.

If God is the just ruler of the world, then where does the inequality among people come from? Why is vice happy and virtue unhappy? But is the mirror to blame when our own mug is crooked, that is, is God the cause of our having rejected his original law, which leads to the equality of all?

Establish such a law that no person would dare to eat a single crumb of bread of other people's labors without valid reasons. Then people, even if they do not become equal, will still become much closer to one another—bread labor will clip the wings of pride.

After all, we are poor from your riches, and you are rich from our poverty.

It would be one thing if this insult were temporary; no—it is eternal. Our past generations, like our present ones, have drunk, are drinking, and will drink this bitter cup. They will never see a protector and defender. And all because you have laid our parent, that is, the living commandment, in the coffin.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 421 --> Though I have seen all my life, it was blindly, but now, after my study of this commandment, I see clearly the following: people have spread out over all the fields of the whole world and work bread hard; even little children and nursing babies, who have not yet eaten it, also suffer for the sake of bread. Are they not true bees, which fly over the fields and gather honey into their hive? And the upper class, I imagined to myself, is it not the drones, which only sing and do nothing, but know one thing: they eat up other people's labors.

Many thieves are caught in the world, but those are not thieves, they are pranksters. Now I have caught a thief, and what a thief! He has robbed the church and the living God and carried off the original law that belonged to us farmers; so one must point personally at this thief. Whoever does not work bread for himself with his own hands, but devours other people's labors, is a thief—take him and hand him over to the court.

Bees clip the drones' wings so that they do not eat the honey of their labors. Now our turn too has come to you drones—and we too have clipped your wings, so that you do not eat the bread of our labors. I know that you will eat even after this, but this eating will be like this: you put bread in your throat, and conscience takes you by the throat—you will not get rid of it by any means.

If bread were like other things wrongfully acquired, you put them away somewhere far off—they lie there quite peacefully. No, bread has to be put in the mouth! This is worth thinking about.

I often hear that they want to unite the whole universe in one faith. Whether this rumor is true—I do not know. If it is true, then, instead of uniting, it will be divided into as many sects again as there are now, and the result will be a labor not only not useful but even harmful.

It was well to unite in ancient times, when the people were wild—wherever you like, there you lead them, they will not break a thread. But now you will not drag them with a triple rope—first, from habit of their own custom, and second, from pride, so as not to submit one to another. But establish faith on the one original law without admixture of extraneous rules, then in a short time the whole universe will flow together into one faith in God, because bread will bow down, soften, and set on the path of virtue even the most hardened.

O! Blind you are, blind, learned man! You look with all your eyes into holy scripture, and you do not see those doors through which you could go out and lead the flock entrusted to you by God out from under the oppression of sin, and you do not see the path leading straight into eternal life; your blindness <!-- vol. 42, p. 422 --> is like that of the Sodomites, who were struck with blindness, “seeking the doors of Lot.”

But they saw their blindness in themselves, while you, being blind, think that you look brightly and see everything clearly, and know everything yourself without interpreters, and that no one should point anything out to you. Your blindness is also like Balaam's, who rode on a donkey, and the angel of the Lord stood in the way with a drawn sword; the donkey that was under Balaam sees the angel, but Balaam does not. I am the donkey, and you are Balaam, and you have been riding on me from my youth.

The whole universe has split into a thousand creeds, whereas there should be one faith, as God too is one.

The original law “in the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread” would gather all creeds into one, and, if only they came to know the whole power of its goodness, they would press it to their heart. And in one century, or even sooner, it would unite all people, from east to west, from north to south, into one faith, into one church and one love.
