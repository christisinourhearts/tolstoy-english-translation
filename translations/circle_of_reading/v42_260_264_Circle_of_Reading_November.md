## THE DEMANDS OF LOVE

Let us imagine people, a man and a woman—husband, wife, brother, sister, father, daughter, mother, son—of the wealthy class, who have vividly understood the sins of a luxurious and idle life amid the destitution of the people and their being crushed by labor, and have left the city, given to someone, in one way or another rid themselves of their surplus, kept for themselves in securities, say, 150 rubles a year for the two of them, or even kept nothing, but earn this by some craft—let us say, painting on porcelain, translating good books—and live in the country, in the middle of a Russian village, having rented or bought a hut, and working their own vegetable garden and orchard with their own hands, tending the bees, and at the same time giving the villagers help, medical, as far as they know how, and educational—they teach the children, write letters, petitions, and the like.

It would seem, what could be better than such a life? But this life will very soon cease to be joyful if these people do not play the hypocrite and lie, if they are sincere. For if these people have renounced all the advantages and joys, the adornments of life that the city and money gave them, they did so only because they recognize people as brothers, equal before the father—not equal in abilities, in merits, if you like, but equal in their rights to life and to all that it can give them.

If doubt about the equality of people is possible when we look at them as adults, each with his separate past, this doubt can no longer exist when we see children. Why will this child have all the care, all the help of knowledge for his physical and mental development, while this charming child with the same or even better aptitudes will become <!-- vol. 42, p. 261 --> a rickety, degenerate half-dwarf from lack of milk and will remain an illiterate, wild person bound by superstitions—only crude labor power?

For if these people have left the city and settled to live as they live in the village, it is only because they believe in the brotherhood of people not in word but in deed, and want, if not to accomplish it, then to be accomplishing it in their life. And this very attempt at accomplishing it must, if only they are sincere—must bring them into a terrible, hopeless position.

With their habits, acquired from childhood, of order, comfort, and above all cleanliness, they moved to the village and, having rented or bought a hut, cleaned it of insects, perhaps even papered it themselves, brought what was left of their furniture, not luxurious but necessary: an iron bed, a wardrobe, a small writing table. And so they live. At first the people are shy of them: they expect from them, as from all the rich, the guarding of their advantages by violence, and therefore do not approach them with requests and demands. But little by little the disposition of the new inhabitants becomes clear: they themselves offer to serve without payment, and the boldest, most importunate of the common people learn by experience that these new people do not refuse, and that one can get something out of them.

And so begin the declarations of demands of every kind, which become greater and greater.

There begin not only beggings, but also natural demands to share whatever one has in excess compared with others; and not only demands, but the people who have settled in the village, always in close contact with the people, themselves feel the inevitable necessity of giving away their surplus where there is extreme need. But it is not only that they feel the necessity of giving away their surplus until they have left what everyone ought to have, that is, the average—of this average—of what everyone ought to have—there is no definition whatever; and they cannot stop, because around them there is always crying need, and they have a surplus compared with this need: it would seem one must keep a glass of milk for oneself; but Matryona has two children: a nursing infant who finds no milk in his mother's breast, and a two-year-old who is beginning to waste away. It would seem one could keep a pillow and a blanket so as to fall asleep in accustomed conditions after a day of labor, but a sick man lies on a lice-ridden caftan and freezes at night, covering himself with a bit of sacking. It would seem one could <!-- vol. 42, p. 262 --> keep tea and food, but it had to be given to wanderers, weak and old. It would seem one could keep at least cleanliness in the house, but beggar boys came and were let stay the night, and they brought in lice.

It is impossible to stop, and where is one to stop?

Only those who do not know at all that feeling of consciousness of the brotherhood of people because of which these people came to the village, or who are so used to lying that they do not even notice the difference between a lie and the truth, will say that there is a limit at which one can and should stop. The whole point is that there is no such limit, that the feeling in the name of which this work is done is such that there is no limit to it, that if there is a limit to it, this only means that this feeling never existed at all, and there was only hypocrisy.

I go on imagining these people. They have worked the whole day, they have come home, they no longer have a bed, they have no pillow, they sleep on straw they have gotten hold of, and now, having eaten some bread, they have lain down to sleep; it is autumn, rain and snow are falling. Someone knocks at their door. Can they not open it? A man comes in, wet and in a fever. What is to be done? Let him lie on the dry straw? There is no more dry straw. And so one must either drive the sick man away, or lay him, wet, on the floor, or give up one's own straw and, since one must sleep somewhere, lie down with him oneself. But even this is not all: there comes a man whom you know to be a drunkard and a debauchee, whom you have helped several times and who each time drank away what you gave him—he comes now with trembling jaws and asks you to give him 3 rubles, which he stole and drank away and for which, if he does not return them, he will be put in prison. You say that you have only 4 rubles and that you need them tomorrow for a payment. Then the man who has come says: “Yes, so it's only talk, and when it comes to the point, you are just like everyone else: let him perish whom we call a brother in words, so long as we are unharmed.”

How should one act here? What should one do? Lay the feverish sick man on the damp floor and lie down oneself on the dry straw—you will sleep even less. Lay him on your own bed and lie down with him: you will catch both lice and typhus. To give the last three rubles to the one who asks means to be left without bread tomorrow. Not to give means, as he says, to renounce that in the name of which you live. If one can stop here, then why not have stopped <!-- vol. 42, p. 263 --> earlier? Why help people at all? Why give away one's fortune, leave the city? Where is the limit? If there is a limit to the work you are doing, then the whole work has no meaning, or has only one terrible meaning, that of hypocrisy.

What is one to do, then? What is to be done? Not to stop means to ruin one's life, become lice-ridden, waste away, die, and—to no purpose, as it seems. To stop means to renounce everything in the name of which what was done was done, in the name of which anything good was done. And one cannot renounce it, because it was not invented by me or by Christ that we are brothers and must serve one another; it is so, and one cannot tear this consciousness out of a person's heart once it has entered it. What is one to do? Is there not some other way out?

And so let us imagine that these people, not frightened by the position in which the necessity of a sacrifice leading to inevitable death had placed them, decided that their position came from the fact that the means with which they had come to help the people were too small, and that this would not have happened, and they would have done greater good, if they had had a great deal of money. Let us imagine that these people found sources of help, collected large, enormous sums of money, and began to help. And not a week would pass before the same thing happened. Very soon all the means, however great they might be, would pour away into those hollows which poverty has formed, and the position would remain the same.

But perhaps there is yet a third way out? And there are people who say that there is, and that it consists in promoting the enlightenment of people, and then this inequality will be destroyed.

But this way out is too obviously hypocritical; one cannot enlighten a population that is every minute on the brink of perishing from hunger. And, above all, the insincerity of the people who preach this way out is seen already from the fact that a person striving to establish equality, even if by means of science, cannot support this inequality by his whole life.

But there is still a fourth way out: to help destroy those causes which produce inequality—to help destroy the violence which produces it.

And this way out cannot fail to occur to those sincere people who will try to realize in their life their consciousness of the brotherhood of people.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 264 --> “If we cannot live here, among these people, in the village, — those people whom I imagine will have to say, — if we are placed in such a terrible position that we must inevitably waste away, become lice-ridden, and die a slow death, or renounce the only moral foundation of our life, then this comes from the fact that some are rich and others destitute; and this inequality comes from violence; and therefore, since the foundation of everything is violence, we must fight against it.” Only the destruction of this violence and of the slavery that flows from it can make possible such a service to people in which there would be no inevitability of sacrificing one's life.

But how is one to destroy this violence? Where is it? It is in the soldier, in the policeman, in the village elder, in the lock that locks my door. How then am I to fight this violence? Where, in what?

In the way people fight who live by violence and who fight violence with violence too?

But for a sincere person this is impossible. To fight violence with violence means to put new violence in the place of the old. To help by enlightenment based on violence means to do the same thing. To collect money acquired by violence and use it to help people whom violence has deprived means to heal with violence the wounds produced by violence.

But even if one fights violence not with violence but with the preaching of nonviolence, with the denunciation of violence, and, above all, with the example of nonviolence and sacrifice, still for a person living a Christian life amid a life of violence there is no other way out than sacrifice—and sacrifice to the end.

A person may not find in himself the strength to throw himself into this abyss, but a sincere person who wishes to fulfill the law of God of which he has become conscious cannot fail to see his duty. One may not go to this sacrifice, but if you want to follow the demands of love, then you must know it and say it so, consider yourself guilty if you have not given everything and your whole life, and not deceive yourself.

And is the sacrifice to the end as terrible as it seems. For the bottom of need is not deep, and we often—like that boy who hung by his hands all night in terror in the well into which he had fallen, fearing an imagined depth, while half an arshin below the boy was the dry bottom.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
