<!-- vol. 41, p. 413 -->

17 June

The calamities of wars and military preparations not only do not correspond to the causes that are put forward in their justification, but their causes are for the most part so trivial that they are not worth discussing, and are completely unknown to those who perish in wars. <!-- vol. 41, p. 414 -->

1

> The madness of modern wars is justified by dynastic interest, nationality, the European balance, honor. The justification of wars by honor is the strangest, because there is not one people that has not defiled itself in the name of honor with every crime and shameful act. There is not one that has not undergone every possible humiliation in the name of honor. And if honor does exist in peoples, then what a strange way to uphold it by war, that is, by all those crimes by which a private man dishonors himself: arson, robberies, murder.

> *Anatole France.*

2

> You ask: is war between civilized peoples still necessary? I answer: not only is it “no longer” necessary, but it was never necessary. Not sometimes but always it has disrupted the proper historical development of humanity, violated justice, held back progress.

> If the consequences of wars were sometimes advantageous for civilization in general, the harmful consequences were far more numerous. We do not see them because only a part of the harmful consequences is immediately evident. The greater part of them, and the most important, are imperceptible to us. And therefore we cannot admit the word “still.” Admitting this word gives the defenders of war the right to assert that the dispute between us is a matter only of temporary appropriateness and personal assessment, and our disagreement will then come down to this, that we consider war useless while they consider it still useful. They will willingly agree with us, with such a formulation of the question, and will say that war may indeed become useless and even harmful, only tomorrow, not today. But today they consider it necessary to perform upon the people those terrible bloodlettings called wars, which are carried out only to satisfy the personal ambitions of the very smallest minority.

> Because such was and such is now the only cause of wars: the power, honors, wealth of a small number of people <!-- vol. 41, p. 415 --> to the detriment of the masses, whose natural credulity and the prejudices aroused and sustained by this minority make wars possible.

> *Gaston Moch.*

3

> It is astonishing to what degree the most trivial disagreement can turn into a holy war. When England and France declared war on Russia in 1855, this happened over so trivial a circumstance that one has to dig for a long time in the diplomatic archives to understand this cause. And at the same time the consequences of this strange misunderstanding were the death of 500 thousand good people and the expenditure of from 5 to 6 billion.

> In essence there were causes, but such as are not confessed. Napoleon III wanted, by means of an alliance with England and a successful war, to consolidate his power, which was of criminal origin; the Russians wanted to seize Constantinople; the English wanted to consolidate the might of their trade and to hinder the influence of the Russians in the East. Under one guise or another it is always the same spirit of conquest and violence.

> *Richet.*

4

> Sometimes one ruler attacks another out of fear that the other will attack him. Sometimes war is begun because the enemy is too strong, and sometimes because he is too weak, sometimes our neighbors want what we possess, or possess what we lack. Then war begins and goes on until they seize what they need or give up what we need.

> *Jonathan Swift.*

5

*In none of people's actions is the power of suggestion, of submission not to reason but to tradition, seen with such clarity as in war. People, millions of people, do with enthusiasm, with pride, a deed that they all recognize as stupid, vile, harmful, dangerous, ruinous, tormenting, villainous, and needed for nothing, they know and repeat all the arguments against this deed—and go on doing it.*

The pretexts that the government puts forward for wars and for the maintenance of troops are always screens behind which entirely different motives are hidden.
