## BISHOP MYRIEL

In 1815 the Right Reverend Charles-François-Bienvenu Myriel was bishop in D.

One day someone knocked at the door of the bishop's house.

— Come in, — the bishop answered.

The door flew wide open all at once with a full swing, as if it had been pushed from outside with all one's strength.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 279 --> A man came in, took a step forward, and stopped, without shutting the door behind him. On his back was a knapsack; in his hands he held a stick. His face was bold, angry, weary, and coarse. The fire in the fireplace lit him up.

The bishop looked calmly at the man who had come in. He had just opened his mouth, meaning to ask what he needed, when the newcomer, leaning with both hands on his stick and measuring the old man with his eyes, began to speak:

— Here it is. My name is Jean Valjean. I am a convict. I spent nineteen years in the galleys. It's four days since they released me, and now I'm going to Pontarlier; that's where I've been assigned. Four days I've been walking from Toulon. Today I've walked thirty versts. Here at the inn they turned me out because of my yellow passport. I went to another inn, and there they wouldn't take me either. “Clear out!” they say. I went to the prison—the warder wouldn't let me in. I went into a dog's kennel—the dog bit me and drove me out, as if it too were a man, as if it too knew who I was. I wanted to spend the night in a field—but it's dark; I thought—it's going to rain, and I came back to the town to lie down somewhere under a gateway. I was all set to lie down to sleep on a stone bench, but some old woman showed me your door and says: “Knock there!” So I knocked. What is this place of yours? An inn? I have money, a hundred and nine francs, earned at hard labor. I'll pay. I have money. I'm tired—I've walked thirty versts, after all—and I'm hungry, too. Well, shall I stay?

— Madame Magloire, — the bishop said to his servant, — set another place.

The traveler took three steps forward and moved closer to the lamp that stood on the table.

— Listen, — he said, as if he had not quite understood the order. — Did you hear that I am a convict? Straight from hard labor, — he took a yellow sheet out of his pocket and unfolded it. — Here is my passport. Yellow—you see. Because of it I've been driven out everywhere. Do you want to read it? I can read; I learned at hard labor. There's a school there for those who want it. Look what is written: “Jean Valjean, released from hard labor, native of”... that's all the same to you. “Spent nineteen years at hard labor. Five years for burglary; fourteen for four attempts to escape. Very dangerous.” So everyone drives me out; and will you let me in? Or else, haven't you got a stable?

<!-- vol. 42, p. 280 --> — Madame Magloire, put clean sheets on the bed in the alcove.

Madame Magloire went to carry out the order. The bishop turned to his visitor.

— Sit down, sir, and warm yourself. We are about to have supper; while we eat, your bed will be made ready.

The traveler had evidently understood. The expression of his face, gloomy and cruel, changed to one of surprise, distrust, joy, and he began to mutter like a man thrown into confusion:

— Is that so? Well, I never! So I'm to stay? You don't drive me away! A convict! You call me sir. You say “vous” to me, not “tu”! You don't say: get out, dog, as everyone said to me. I expected you'd throw me out. That's why I told you straight off who I am. And you invite me to supper, and a bed with sheets, like everyone has! For nineteen years I haven't slept in a bed! Good people you are! Excuse me, Mister Innkeeper, what is your name? I'll pay whatever you ask. You are an honest man. You are the innkeeper, aren't you?

— I am a priest, — answered the bishop.

— A priest! — rejoined the convict. — You must be the priest of this big church? Really, I've gone stupid, not to have noticed your skullcap.

As he said this, he put his knapsack and stick in the corner, put his passport away in his pocket, and sat down.

While he was talking, the bishop got up and locked the door, which had been left open.

Madame Magloire came back. She brought another place setting and put it on the table.

— Madame Magloire, — said the bishop, — put the place setting nearer the fire, — and, turning to his guest, he added: — the night wind is cold in the Alps. You must be chilled, sir?

Every time he spoke the word “sir” in his grave, gentle voice, the convict's face shone.

To say “sir” to a convict is the same as giving a glass of water to a man who thirsts. Humiliation thirsts for respect.

— How dimly this lamp burns! — remarked the bishop.

Madame Magloire understood and went to the bishop's bedroom for the silver candlesticks, which she brought in with the candles lit and put on the table. She knew that the bishop liked them to be lit when he had guests.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 281 --> — You are kind, — said the convict, — you don't despise me. You have taken me in. I didn't hide from you where I come from and who I am.

The bishop gently took the convict by the hand: “You need not have told me who you are. This house is not mine, but God's. This door does not ask one who enters whether he has a name, but whether he has grief. You are suffering, hunger and thirst torment you; you are welcome, come in. I am not receiving you in my own house; the master here is the one who needs shelter. Everything that is here is all yours. Why should I know your name? Before you named yourself, I already knew what to call you.”

The guest looked at him in surprise.

— Really? You knew what my name is?

— Yes, — answered the bishop, — I knew that your name is my brother.

— Yes, I was hungry when I came in here, — said the guest, — but you have so surprised me that even my hunger has gone!

The bishop looked at him and asked:

— Have you suffered much?

— Ah, the red jacket, the ball tied to your leg, a plank instead of a bed, cold, heat, labor, beatings with sticks, double irons for any trifle, the punishment cell for a word in answer, and chains even in bed, even in the hospital. Dogs, even dogs are happier! And this for nineteen years. Now I am forty-six. Go and live with a yellow passport!

— Yes, — said the bishop, — you have come out of a place of sorrow. But listen: in heaven there will be more joy over the tear-stained face of a repentant sinner than over the spotless robe of a hundred righteous men. If you have carried out of that abode of suffering malice and hatred against people, you are worthy of pity; but if you have carried out feelings of meekness, peace, and forbearance—you are better than all of us.

Meanwhile Madame Magloire brought in the supper.

The bishop's face suddenly took on the cheerful expression of a hospitable host.

— Please come to the table, — he said with the animation with which he usually invited guests to the table.

The bishop said a prayer, then ladled out the soup. The guest fell to eating greedily.

— It seems to me that something is missing on the table, — the bishop suddenly said.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 282 --> Indeed, Madame Magloire had put on the table only the three necessary place settings. Yet it had become the custom to lay all six silver place settings on the table when anyone from outside was having supper.

Madame Magloire understood the hint, went out without a word, and a moment later the place settings the bishop had asked for were already gleaming on the tablecloth, laid out symmetrically before each of those sitting at the table.

After supper the bishop took one of the silver candlesticks from the table, handed the other to his guest, and said:

— I will take you to your room.

The convict followed him. At the moment they were passing through the bedroom, Madame Magloire was putting the silver away in the wall cupboard that was over the head of the bishop's bed. She did this every evening before going to bed.

The bishop led his guest to the alcove, in which a clean bed had been made ready, put the candlestick on a small table, and, wishing him good night, withdrew.

When the cathedral bell tower struck two o'clock, Jean Valjean woke up. What woke him was that the bed was too soft. For twenty years he had not slept in a good bed, and although he had lain down without undressing, the too unaccustomed sensation kept him from sleeping soundly. Many different thoughts came into his head, but one kept coming back and blotting out the others: he had noticed the six silver place settings and the big soup ladle that Madame Magloire had put on the table. These place settings gave him no peace. They lay here, a few steps from him. Passing through the bedroom, he had seen the old servant putting them away in the little cupboard over the head of the bed. He had taken careful note of the little cupboard. It was on the right hand as you came out of the dining room. The place settings were massive, of old silver; by selling them he could get twice as much as he had earned in the course of his nineteen years at hard labor.

He spent a whole hour in wavering and in struggle.

Three o'clock struck. He opened his eyes, raised himself up in bed, stretched out his hands and felt for the knapsack he had thrown into the corner of the alcove, then let his legs down and sat.

He stayed some minutes in this position, lost in thought, then got to his feet, stood several more minutes in indecision, <!-- vol. 42, p. 283 --> listening: all was quiet in the house. Then he stuck his shoes in his pocket, strapped up his knapsack, and put it on his shoulders. Holding his breath and stepping carefully, he made for the next room, which served the bishop as a bedroom. The bedroom door was pulled to: the bishop had not even locked it behind him. Jean Valjean pulled his cap down over his forehead and quickly, without looking at the bishop, went straight to the little cupboard. The key was sticking out of its door; he opened it; the first thing that caught his eye was the basket of silver; he took it, crossed the room with rapid steps, without any precautions and paying no attention to the noise he was making, reached the window, and, seizing his stick, stepped over the sill, stuck the silver in his knapsack, and, running quickly across the garden, climbed over the fence and disappeared.

The next day, at sunrise, the bishop was walking in the garden. Madame Magloire came running to him in alarm.

— Your Grace! He has gone and carried off our silver. Look, this is where he climbed over!

The bishop stood for a minute in silence, then, raising a thoughtful gaze, said meekly:

— First of all we still have to ask, was the silver ours? For a long time I have wrongly kept it; it belongs to the poor. And this man is poor.

A little while later the bishop sat down to breakfast at the same table at which Jean Valjean had sat the evening before.

He was just about to get up from the table when there was a knock at the door.

— Come in, — the bishop answered.

The door opened. Three men were holding a fourth by the collar. The three men were gendarmes, the fourth was Jean Valjean.

The bishop came toward them with all the briskness his advanced age allowed him.

— Ah, it's you! — he said, looking at Jean Valjean: — Very glad to see you. But listen, I gave you the candlesticks too; they are silver, like all the rest. Why didn't you take them along with the place settings?

Jean Valjean raised his eyes and looked at the bishop with an expression that no human language can convey.

— And he told you, — said the bishop, smiling, — that an old priest who let him stay the night gave him this? And you brought him here? This is a misunderstanding.

— Then we can let him go?

— Without doubt, — answered the bishop.

The gendarmes let go of Jean Valjean, who backed away.

— Is it true that they are setting me free? — he said soundlessly, as people speak in their sleep.

— Yes, they're letting you go; didn't you hear? — said one of the gendarmes.

— My friend, — the bishop said to him, — before you go, do take your candlesticks. Here they are.

He went up to the fireplace, took the silver candlesticks, and handed them to Jean Valjean.

Jean Valjean was trembling all over. He took the candlesticks mechanically and looked at them in bewilderment.

— Go in peace! — the bishop said to him. — By the way, my friend, if you come again, there is no need to go through the garden. You can always come in and go out by the door from the street. It is fastened day and night with a latch.

Then, turning to the gendarmes, he added:

— Gentlemen, you may go.

The gendarmes withdrew. Jean Valjean felt that he was close to fainting.

The bishop went up to him and said in a whisper:

— Do not forget, never forget your promise: you gave your word to use this money to become an honest man.

Jean Valjean, who remembered no promises, was confused. The bishop had spoken these words with particular emphasis. He went on solemnly:

— Jean Valjean, my brother, from now on you cease to belong to evil and pass into the power of good. I have bought your soul. I drive the spirit of darkness out of it and deliver it to God.

*Victor Hugo.*
