<!-- vol. 41, p. 302 -->

5 May

The basis of upbringing is religious teaching, that is, the explanation of the meaning and purpose of life.

1

People consider lying before a court a crime, and untruthful words in dealings with equals ignoble acts, but to tell children all sorts of nonsense and all sorts of lies is considered not only not wrong but, on the contrary, almost necessary. And yet how clear, one would think, it is that it is precisely with children that one must be especially careful about what is said to them by their elders.

2

Religious teaching, as an explanation of the meaning and purpose of life, which answered the demands of people a thousand years ago, cannot satisfy the people of our time. Yet children are taught first of all what answered the requirement of people thousands of years ago—this is a terrible mistake.

“If only it were possible to bring up children so that everything unclear remained wholly incomprehensible to them!” (Lichtenberg.)

These words mean that one must not instill in children, as is usually done, the idea that all the improbabilities of superstition may have some foundation. Children, accepting such a judgment, grow used to unclear half-proofs and take the incomprehensible for understood.

3

> Whatever we learn too much of, and prematurely, in childhood, of that we will surely know nothing afterward and in old age, and a person who loves thoroughness ends by becoming a sophist of his youthful errors.

> *Kant.*

4

One should pass on to children only what they can understand in such a way that even when they are grown up they could add nothing to that understanding.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 303 -->

5

> *Always be truthful, and especially with a child. Do what you have promised him, or you will accustom him to lying.*

> *The Talmud.*

6

> It would be good to investigate the question—is it not harmful to polish children too much in their upbringing? We do not yet know man well enough not to leave the carrying out of this to chance, if one may put it that way. I am sure that if our pedagogues succeeded in reaching their goal—I mean, if they proved able to bring up children entirely as they wish, then we would no longer have a single truly great man. What is most needed in life no one usually taught us.

> God forbid that a man, whose teacher is the whole of nature, should become a piece of wax on which some professor might stamp his own lofty image.

> *Lichtenberg.*

Do not tell the one you are bringing up, and above all do not pass off as sacred, unquestionable truth, what you either do not believe at all or have even some doubt about. To act so is a great crime.
