<!-- vol. 41, p. 486 -->

9 July

People mistakenly think that much knowledge is a merit. What matters is not the quantity but the quality of knowledge.

1

> Although Socrates considered foolishness incompatible with wisdom, he did not call ignorance foolishness. But not to know oneself and to imagine that one knows what one does not know—this is what he called madness.

> *Xenophon.*

2

> We live in an age of philosophy, of the sciences, and of reason. It seems that all the sciences have united to light the way in this labyrinth of human life. Enormous libraries are open to all; everywhere secondary schools, schools, and universities give us from childhood the possibility of making use of the wisdom of people that has shown itself over thousands of years. Everything, it would seem, contributes to the education of our mind and the establishment of reason. Well, have we become better or wiser from all this? Do we know better the way and the purpose of our calling? Do we know better what our duties consist in and, above all, the good of life? What have we acquired from all this vain knowledge besides enmity, hatred, uncertainty, and doubts? Every religious teaching and sect proves that it alone has found the truth. Every writer alone knows what our good consists in. One proves to us that there is no body, another—that there is no soul, a third—that there is no connection between soul and body, a fourth—that man is an animal, a fifth—that God is only a mirror.

> *Rousseau.*

3

> What an enormous advantage is on the side of the man who knows nothing about a subject and, what is very rare, knows that he knows nothing about it, over the one who really knows something but thinks that he knows everything! What an enormous advantage is on the side of the first!

> *Thoreau.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 487 -->

4

> How much unnecessary reading we could avoid by thinking for ourselves!

> Are reading and learning really one and the same? Someone asserted, not without grounds, that printing, if it did help the wider spread of learning, did so to the detriment of its quality and content. Reading too much is harmful to thinking. The greatest thinkers I have met among the learned men I have studied were precisely the least well-read.

> If people were taught how they should think, and not only what they should think, misunderstanding would be prevented.

> *Lichtenberg.*

5

*Do not fear ignorance, fear false knowledge. From it comes all the evil of the world.*

6

A person who knows how to hide his stupidity is better than a person who wants to show off his wisdom.

7

> To attain moral perfection, one must first of all care for purity of soul. And purity of soul is attained only when the heart seeks truth and the will strives for holiness. And all this depends on true knowledge.

> *Confucius.*

8

> The mind is strengthened or weakened by reading in exactly the same way as the body is by fresh or rotten air.

> *John Ruskin.*

Knowledge that provokes disputes is doubtful.
