<!-- vol. 42, p. 52 -->

17 September

Owning land as property is just as unjust as, even more unjust than, slavery, that is, owning a man as property.

1

> The one who first, having fenced off a piece of land, dared to say: “This land is mine,” and found people so simple that they could believe it—that man was the first founder of the civil society that exists now. From how many crimes, wars, murders, misfortunes, horrors would humanity have been delivered by the one who, pulling up the stakes and filling in the ditch, had said: “Beware, do not believe this deceiver; you are lost if you forget that the land can belong to no one and that its fruits belong to all.”

> *Rousseau.*

2

> Simple justice does not admit a right of property in land, because if one part of the land's surface can justly be the property of one person and can be held by him for his personal benefit and use, as a thing to which he has an exclusive right, then other parts of the land's surface can also become such property, and it is possible that the whole of the land's surface will become such, and therefore the globe will become private property.

> *Herbert Spencer.*

3

> It goes without saying that the fact that some chief or landowner bought some privilege or inherited it from his fathers does not yet give him any moral <!-- vol. 42, p. 53 --> right to it. The question is: is his claim just, is it reasonable in itself? Because wrong and evil are all the more wrong and evil the longer they continue.

> *Grant Allen.*

4

> It cannot be asserted that the existing rights to land are lawful. Let whoever thinks so look through the chronicles. Violence, fraud, power, cunning—these are the sources from which these rights proceed.

> *Herbert Spencer.*

5

People who own landed property condemn people, both in words and in the courts, for appropriating other people's property.

Do they really not understand that they, who unceasingly take from the people the most inalienable property, ought to burn with shame as soon as the word *theft* is mentioned, instead of condemning and punishing for what they themselves are unceasingly guilty of all around.

6

> Look, from the point of view of an observer of nature, at a landless man, at a being who has the possibility and the capacity to use the land, who is compelled by his needs to use it, and who at the same time is deprived of the right to the land. This is as unnatural as a bird without air or a fish without water.

> *Henry George.*

7

> Private property in land, which has never arisen from the natural relations of people but in history has always appeared as the consequence of seizure and robbery, presents itself as such an extreme absurdity, such a gross injustice, such an evident waste of productive forces, and such an obstacle to the most advantageous use of natural riches, such an opposite of sound social policy, and such a <!-- vol. 42, p. 54 --> brake on the true improvement of the condition of humanity, that it is tolerated only because the majority of people never think about it and do not hear others reasoning about it.

> *Henry George.*

8

> Of two systems of slavery, at one and the same level of moral development, the system that turns people into property is without doubt more humane than the system that turns land into private property. When land is recognized as private property, people are worn out with work and hunger, deprived of all the joys and charms of life, doomed to ignorance and a brutish existence, and driven to crimes and suicide, as if without the participation of anyone's will, but by force of what seems a fatal necessity for which no one is responsible.

> *Henry George.*

*The injustice of owning landed property, like every injustice, is inevitably bound up with a whole series of injustices and evil deeds needed to protect it.*
