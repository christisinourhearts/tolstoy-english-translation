## REASON

## I

As in all the things of this world every new means, new advantage, and every new superiority at once brings with it its own disadvantages too, so reason also, while giving man so great an advantage over the animals, brings with it its own disadvantages and opens such paths of temptation as an animal can never come upon. Through them motives of a new kind, to which the animal is inaccessible, acquire power over his will, namely *abstract* motives—mere thoughts, which are far from always drawn from one's own experience, but are often engendered by the words and examples of others, by suggestion and by literature. With the possibility of *understanding*, there at once opens to man the possibility of *error* as well. And every error, sooner or later, will cause harm, and the greater harm the greater the error was. For a personal error one will someday have to pay, and often at a high price; the same, on a large scale, with the errors of whole peoples. Therefore one cannot remind people often enough that every error must be pursued and rooted out, as an enemy of humanity, wherever it may be met, and that there can be no harmless, still less useful, errors. A thinking man must enter into struggle with them, must, even if humanity should cry out loudly at this, like a sick man whose abscess the doctor is lancing.

For the masses, the place of real education is taken by a kind of training. It is carried out by example, by habit, and by hammering in certain concepts firmly from earliest childhood, before enough experience of the intellect and power of judgment has accumulated to struggle against this. That is how thoughts are implanted which afterward sit so firmly and remain so unconquerable by any instruction whatever, as if they were *innate;* and indeed they are often taken to be such, even by philosophers. In this way one can implant in people, with equal success, both the just, and the reasonable, and the most absurd—accustom them, for example, to approach this or that idol only when filled with holy awe, and at the utterance of its <!-- vol. 41, p. 84 --> name to cast themselves into the dust not only with their bodies but with their whole souls; to lay down their lives and property voluntarily for words, for names, in defense of the most fantastic trifles; to consider, arbitrarily, this or that the greatest honor or the greatest disgrace, and in accordance with this to respect or despise a person from the depths of their souls; to abstain from all meat food, as in Hindustan, or to eat still warm and quivering pieces cut from a living animal, as in Abyssinia; to devour people, as in New Zealand, or to give their children as a sacrifice to Moloch; to castrate themselves, to throw themselves voluntarily onto the pyre on which a dead man is burned—in a word, one can accustom them *to anything whatever.* Hence the Crusades, the debaucheries of fanatical sects, hence the chiliasts and the Khlysty, the persecutions of heretics, the autos-da-fé (the pyres of the Inquisition), and all that can be found in the long scroll of human errors...

The tragedy of errors and prejudices is in their practical side, the comedy in the theoretical: there is no absurdity which, if it has been instilled at first into even three people, could not become the conviction of a whole people.

Such are the disadvantageous sides that are connected with the presence of reason in us.

*Schopenhauer.*

## II

The errors and disagreements of people in the matter of seeking and acknowledging truth come from nothing other than their distrust of reason; as a result, human life, guided by customs, traditions, fashions, superstitions, prejudices, violence, and anything you like except reason, flows on by itself, while reason exists by itself. It often happens, too, that if the organ of reason—thinking—is applied to anything at all, it is not to the matter of seeking and spreading truth, but to justifying and upholding, at all costs, customs, traditions, fashions, superstitions, prejudices.

The errors and disagreements of people in the matter of acknowledging the one truth are not because reason in people is not one or cannot show them the one truth, but because they do not believe in it.

If they believed in their reason, they would find a way to check the testimony of reason in themselves against its testimony in others.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 85 --> And having found this means of mutual verification, they would be convinced that reason is one, in spite of the fact that, because of the different degrees of strength of the organ of reason—thinking—it shows different things.

With reason it is the same as with sight. As the organs of sight—the eyes—open to people physical horizons differing in the size of their radii not because of an absence of unity in the laws of sight, but owing to the difference in degrees of farsightedness or in points of view (in the literal sense), so too the organ of reason—thinking—opens to people different intellectual and moral horizons not because of an absence of unity in the laws of thinking, but because of a difference either in degrees of intellectual farsightedness or in points of view (in the figurative sense).

And as, in the matter of surveying the physical horizon, the one-sidedness of separate particular points of view is corrected by uniting them into one common, for example, the highest point of view (in the literal sense of the word), and the difference in degrees of farsightedness is equalized by optical instruments: spectacles, binoculars, telescopes—so in the matter of studying the moral and spiritual horizons the same one-sidedness of single points of view is corrected by a similar uniting of them into one common, higher point of view; while the difference in degrees of intellectual farsightedness is balanced with the help of enlightenment, and the best organ of such equalizing is the word that comes from the mouths of the wisest people.

The sage *helps the independent birth in people of their own ideas and feelings*, placed in them from eternity. His role is wholly comparable to the role of a spyglass, which does not grant sight to the blind, but only strengthens the sight of even the poorest eyes. Socrates likened the sage to a midwife, who does not grant a woman a child, but only helps her to bring her own into the world.

But the cause of people's disagreement in the matter of acknowledging the one truth does not lie solely in the difference of points of view and degrees of understanding. The cause of such disagreement is hidden also in people's self-love, owing to which very often a person who has already inwardly acknowledged the reasonableness of his interlocutor's arguments nevertheless continues to defend the opinion he has once expressed.

*Fyodor Strakhov.*
