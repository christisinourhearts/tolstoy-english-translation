<!-- vol. 41, p. 548 -->

4 August

Self-renunciation is not renunciation of oneself, but only renunciation of the animal in oneself.

1

> Every person contains in himself the consciousness of the life of all humanity. It lies deep in a person's soul, but it is there. And sooner or later a person must come to the consciousness of this wider life.

> The renunciation of his personal aims that takes place in him is at once rewarded by the stronger life into which he enters.

> Only by renouncing his exclusive personality does a person become a real, living personality, and, recognizing the life of others as his own, he becomes conscious in himself of a life that has neither limits nor end.

> *Carpenter.*

2

> A person who thinks only of himself and seeks his own advantage in everything cannot be happy. If you want to live for yourself, live for others.

> *Seneca.*

3

The greatest good that a person can know, the state of fullest freedom and happiness, is the state of self-denial and love. Reason reveals to man the only possible path to this good, and feeling impels man along this path.

4

To many it seems that if personality and love for it are excluded from life, nothing will remain. It seems to them that without personality there is no life. But this only seems so to people who <!-- vol. 41, p. 549 --> have not experienced the joy of self-denial. Cast personality out of life, renounce it, and only then will you know the best good of life—love.

5

> *True life begins only when self-renunciation begins.*

> *Carlyle.*

6

> When the light goes out in your heart, darkness hides your path from you. Beware of this terrible darkness. No light of your reason can destroy the darkness that comes from your heart until all self-loving thoughts have been driven out of it.

> *Brahmanic wisdom.*

7

> The striving for personal good is only a continuation of animality in us; human life begins only with the renunciation of it.

> *Amiel.*

8

The more a person gives to people and the less he demands for himself, the better he is; the less he gives to others and the more he demands for himself, the worse he is. But the people of our time do not reason so. They devise the most varied and cunning reasonings, but only not the one that naturally presents itself to every simple person. According to their reasonings, it is not at all necessary to abstain from articles of luxury. One can sympathize with the condition of the workers, make speeches and write books in their favor, and at the same time continue to make use of those labors that we consider ruinous for them.

What we call self-renunciation is only the consequence of the transfer of consciousness from the animal in oneself into one's spiritual “I.” If this change of consciousness has taken place, then what seemed renunciation before this change no longer appears as renunciation, but only as a natural withdrawal from what is unneeded.
