<!-- vol. 41, p. 261 -->

16 April

The recognition of the dignity of man in oneself and in others is compatible neither with subjection, nor with patronage, nor with benefaction of one person toward another.

1

> Every person may demand respect for himself and in exactly the same way must respect his neighbor.

> No person can be either an instrument or an end. In this consists his dignity. And just as he cannot dispose of himself at any price (which would be contrary to his dignity), so too he has no right to depart from the obligatory, equal respect for all people, that is, he is obliged in reality to acknowledge the dignity of the human rank in every person and therefore must express this respect toward every person.

> *Kant.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 262 -->

2

> In their reasonings about the good of the working people, the representatives of power fall into a tone of condescending patronage. People who are conscious of the true dignity of labor are offended by this tone more than they could be offended by openly expressed contempt. In all the expressions of their sympathy one hears an acknowledgment that poverty is the natural state of the working people, into which they must fall everywhere they are not graciously patronized. No one even makes a show of thinking that landowners or capitalists need patronage. They, we are told, can take care of themselves; only the poor workers need to be patronized.

> *Henry George.*

3

> Patronage of the masses has at all times been a pretext for violence—a justification of monarchy, aristocracy, and privileges of every kind. But is there even one example in the history of the world when, whether under monarchical or under republican government, the patronage of the working masses did not mean their oppression? The patronage that people who held legislative power in their hands extended to the working people was, in the best cases, only such patronage as a man extends to his cattle. He patronizes them so as afterward to make use of their strength and meat.

> *Henry George.*

4

The most insignificant small things contribute to the formation of character.

Do not say that small things are trifles. Only a truly moral person sees the whole significance of small things.

5

There are believing people who have the custom of bowing down to the ground before every person with whom they have dealings. They say that they do this because the spirit of God lives in every person. However strange this custom may be, its basis is profoundly true.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 263 -->

6

*Man is timid and is always begging indulgence for himself. He hardly dares to say: I am, I think.*

*Emerson.*

A person, in serving another, must know that he is not submitting, not patronizing, not acting as a benefactor, but is fulfilling his duty—not before a person, but before the eternal law.
