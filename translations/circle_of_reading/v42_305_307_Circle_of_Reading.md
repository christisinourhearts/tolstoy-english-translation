<!-- vol. 42, p. 305 -->

2 December

*Thou shalt not kill* refers not to the killing of man alone, but to the killing of every living thing. And this commandment was written in the heart of man before it was heard on Sinai.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 306 -->

1

However convincing the arguments against a slaughter-free diet may be, a person cannot help feeling pity and revulsion at the killing of a sheep or a chicken, and most people will always prefer to give up the pleasure and benefit of meat food rather than commit these killings themselves.

2

“But if one must pity sheep and rabbits, then one must pity wolves and rats too,” say the enemies of vegetarianism. — We do pity them and try to pity them, the vegetarians answer, and we look for means against the harm they do other than killing, and such means are found. If you say the same of insects, then, although we do not feel immediate pity for them (Lichtenberg says that our pity for animals is directly proportional to their size), we think that one can feel pity for them too (as Silvio Pellico did for a spider), and means other than killing can be found against them.

“But plants are living beings too, and you destroy their life,” the opponents of vegetarianism say further. But this very argument best of all defines the essence of vegetarianism and points to the means of satisfying its demands. Ideal vegetarianism is feeding on fruits, that is, on the envelope of the seed, which contains life: apples, peaches, watermelons, pumpkins, berries. Hygienists recognize this food as the most healthful, and with this food a person does not destroy life. Significant here, too, is the fact that the pleasant taste of fruits, the envelopes of seeds, brings it about that people, picking and eating the fruits, carry them about the earth and multiply them.

3

*With the spread of enlightenment and the increase of population, people pass from eating people to eating animals, from eating animals to feeding on grains and roots, and from this way of feeding to the most natural feeding on fruits.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 307 -->

4

The seizure of great expanses of land by large owners has made fruits a luxury. The more evenly the land is distributed, the more fruit is grown.

5

> Reading and writing by no means constitute education if they do not help people to be kinder to all creatures.

> *John Ruskin.*

The unreasonableness, the unlawfulness, and the harm, moral and material, of eating meat have become clear in recent times to such a degree that meat-eating is now upheld no longer by arguments but only by the suggestion of long usage, by tradition, by custom. And therefore in our time there is no longer any need to prove the unreasonableness of meat-eating, obvious to all. It is ceasing of itself.
