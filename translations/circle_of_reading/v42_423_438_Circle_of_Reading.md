## \* THOUGHTS OMITTED BY THE PUBLISHER FOR CENSORSHIP REASONS IN THE FIRST EDITION OF “CIRCLE OF READING”

19 January

In order to know what is moral, one must know what is immoral; in order to know what to do, one must know what one must not do.

1

One of the errors of external religious teachings is that they prescribe particular deeds, sacrifices, sacraments, prayers, whereas the true religious teaching only points to what one must not do.

2

*Life demands its manifestation. If the paths of evil are closed to it, it manifests itself on the path of good.*

3

Voltaire said that if a person in Paris had only to press a button in order to kill a mandarin in China, many would press the button out of curiosity.

In our time Voltaire would no longer have said this. We now know the Chinese man, and without any special effort we see in him a human brother. What hardly seemed criminal in Voltaire's time is now plainly criminal even for the least sensitive person.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 424 -->

4

Life becomes more and more complex, and in accordance with life, moral demands too must become more complex.

If there was a Cain, then for Cain, in order not to do wrong, it was necessary only not to kill Abel; but in our time Cain, having joined a hussar regiment and gone off to the Far East, is very much mistaken if he thinks that he, killing Japanese, is better than Cain who killed Abel.

More than that, the tsar, admiral, technician who prepares guns, gunpowder, battleships, the marshal of the nobility presiding over the levy of recruits, the governor, the tax collector, the gendarme—all must understand, under conditions that have grown more complex, that they are the same kind of Cains. And that for them to abstain from service, from salary only, so as not to be participants in the killing of brothers, is the same as it was for Cain to abstain from direct fratricide.

5

How often one meets people who are indignant against war, prisons, violence, and at the same time, though not directly, take part in those very deeds which they condemn.

6

A person of our time cannot examine attentively enough the aims and applications of those seemingly innocent deeds which he does, if he wants to live a moral life. A person eating a cutlet must know that this cutlet is the body of a slaughtered lamb taken from its mother, and, receiving a salary at an arms factory, a powder factory, or as an officer or an official in the collection of taxes, must know that the money he receives is the sweat and blood of tormented and killed people, or of people who are yet to be tormented and killed, and that his participation in these deeds is a deed no better, though more hidden, than the deed of Cain.

7

*In our time the greatest and most harmful crimes are not those that are committed from time to time, but those that are committed chronically and are not recognized as crimes.*

Do not console yourself with the thought that if you do not see those whom you torment and kill, and if you have many comrades doing <!-- vol. 42, p. 425 --> the same, you are not a tormentor, not a murderer. It was possible for you not to be one so long as you did not know where the money that falls into your hands comes from, but if you know, then there is no justification for you, not before people (before people there is justification in everything and always), but before your own conscience.

22 January

1

They take a man in all his strength, in the best time of youth, put a gun in his hands, a knapsack on his back, and mark his head with a cockade, then they say to him: my fellow, sovereign so-and-so has treated me badly, and therefore you must attack all his subjects; I have announced to them that on such-and-such a date you will appear at their border in order to kill them...

You may perhaps, through inexperience, think that our enemies are people, but they are not people, they are Prussians, Frenchmen (Japanese); you will distinguish them from human nature by the color of their uniform. Try to perform your duty as well as possible, because I, staying at home, will be watching you; if you win, then you will come back, I will come out to you in uniform and say: soldiers, I am pleased with you. In case you remain on the field of battle, which is very likely, I will send news of your death to your family, so that they can mourn you and inherit from you. If you lose an arm or a leg, I will pay you what they are worth. But if you remain alive and are no longer fit to carry a knapsack, I will discharge you, and you can go and croak wherever you like, it is no concern of mine.

*Claude Tillier.*

5

*One cannot reign innocently; still less can one innocently be a military man.*

6

> Wars have various pretexts, but only one cause—the army.

> *Victor Hugo.*

If you do not want to be a party to murder, treat every military man as one naturally treats a person who has gone astray and been convicted of murder. One can overcome one's revulsion <!-- vol. 42, p. 426 --> toward such a person and pity him, but if, as happens among military men, they take pride in their position, one cannot, if it is impossible to bring him to reason, help wishing to be rid of all relations with such a person.

31 January

1

The government of churches by a hierarchy may be monarchical, aristocratic, or democratic; this concerns only the internal structure. The church itself, under all forms, always remains despotic. Where the statutes of faith are considered the fundamental law, there the clergy reigns, which considers itself entitled to need neither reason nor science, because it is the sole guardian, holding power, and interpreter of the will of the invisible lawgiver, and, having power, it can, instead of persuading, prescribe.

*Kant.*

9 February

5

Everyone who wears a uniform and embroidery on his sleeve and who is endowed with a share of power—inevitably becomes a despot.

*Urbain Gohier.*

17 February

Church faith replaces deeds with rites.

1

> Whoever puts in first place the observance of statutory laws, which require public manifestation, as a necessary part of religion, and moreover not merely as a means of arousing a moral disposition but as an objective condition of becoming, directly through this, pleasing to God, while he puts the striving for a good life in second place after this historical faith, instead of placing the first as something that can be pleasing to God only conditionally, after the second, which <!-- vol. 42, p. 427 --> alone (in itself) is pleasing to him—such a person turns the service of God into a mere making of a fetish for himself and performs a false worship, which turns back all preparation for true religion.

> *Kant.*

2

> If we have been sent into this world by a wise being, of which there is no doubt whatever, then let us try to do the best that is possible in this position, and let us not allow ourselves to be blinded by revelations. What a person needs to know for his blessedness, he surely knows without any revelation other than the one he possesses by his very nature.

> *Lichtenberg.*

3

> The direct and inevitable result of the pride of faith is that a multitude of people inclined to good, merciful, and meek, who could under other conditions have lightened by their patience the weight of the common cross and by their beneficent activity have overpowered the influence of evil, have withdrawn from this true service of people in order to devote the best part of their life to a false service of God; by this I mean striving for the unattainable, lamenting the inevitable, and meditating on the incomprehensible.

> *John Ruskin.*

4

> *Religion that joins with injustice to suppress the aspirations inherent in people is worse than atheism.*

> *Henry George.*

5

> We have a multitude of churches. Everywhere we have churches in which every possible religion is preached, except the one that teaches that it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God—except the one that once drove the traders out of the temple.

> *Henry George.*

Every external religious rite is hostile to religion. Rites cannot call forth a religious disposition. A rite calls forth only a semblance of it, making a person think that he possesses what he does not have.

6 March

True faith does not need a church.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 428 -->

1

> Faith—founded on authority—is not faith. The growth of the influence of authority in faith shows its decline. Christ astonishes and seems supernatural to sensual people. They cannot reconcile him either with history or with themselves. But as soon as these people begin to revere their own consciousness and try to live a holy life, their piety explains every event of Christ's life, every word.

> *Emerson.*

2

> We know nothing about our future, and indeed we should not strive to learn anything about it except what stands in rational connection with the incentives to morality and with its ends. To this belongs also the faith that there is not a single good act that would not bring consequences for the one who performs it; that therefore, however worthless a person may consider himself at the end of his life, this must not keep him from the intention of performing even just one more good act that he is still able to do, since he has grounds to hope that such an act, in proportion to how far the person was guided by a good intention in performing it, will still have greater value than those idle purifications from sins which, contributing nothing to the diminution of guilt, are supposed to make up for the lack of good deeds.

> *Kant.*

*The union of people is accomplished not by striving for union, but by striving for truth.*

Let all, without thinking of union, go toward truth, and they will unite. The world is like a great temple in which the light falls in the middle. In vain will people try to unite outside the light. Uniting in darkness, they will only be divided. Let all, without thinking of uniting, go toward the light, and, coming from different sides, they will all unite in the middle of the temple.

9 March

Man in general, and a Christian in particular, is bound not to take part in war or preparation for it, either personally, or with money, or with reasonings about it.

17 March

3

The situation of the majority of people, enlightened by true, brotherly enlightenment, and now crushed by the deceit and cunning of the men of violence, who force this majority itself to ruin its own life—this situation is terrible and seems to have no way out.

Only two ways out present themselves, and both are closed: one is to tear violence apart by violence, by terrorism, dynamite bombs, daggers, as our nihilists and anarchists did, to smash this conspiracy of governments against the peoples from outside ourselves; or to enter into agreement with the government, making concessions to it, and, by taking part in it, little by little to untangle the net that binds the people, and to free them. Both ways out are closed.

Dynamite and the dagger, as experience shows us, call forth only reaction, disrupt the most precious force—the only one that is within our power, public opinion.

The other way out is closed because governments have already found out how far they can admit the participation of people who wish to transform it. They admit only what does not violate what is essential, and they are very sensitive about what is harmful to them; sensitive because the matter concerns their existence. And they admit people who disagree with them and wish to transform governments not only in order to satisfy the demand of these people, but for themselves, for the government. These people are dangerous to governments if they should remain outside governments and rise up against them, influencing the only, the strongest instrument of government—public opinion; and therefore they need to render these people harmless, to draw them to themselves by means of concessions made by the government, to render them harmless like microbe cultures, and then to use these same people in the service of the aims of governments, that is, the oppression and exploitation of the people.

Both ways out are tightly and impenetrably closed. What, then, remains?

One cannot tear apart by violence—you increase the reaction; to enter the ranks of the government is also impossible—you become an instrument of the government. One thing remains: to struggle against the government with the weapon of thought, of the word, without entering its ranks, without increasing its strength with yourself.

This one thing is needed and will surely be successful. And this is what God wants, and this is what Christ taught.

25 March

The church is the chief culprit in the corruption of people.

What is great before people is an abomination before God.

CONCLUSION

<!-- vol. 42, p. 429 -->

<!-- vol. 42, p. 430 -->

1

One of the chief causes of the evil of our life is the faith, fostered in our Christian world, in a crude Jewish god—a personal one, whereas the chief mark (if one may put it so) of God is that he is limited by nothing, consequently not personal.

2

> The clergy must keep the people in their ignorance; without this, the Gospel is so simple that anyone would say to the spiritual pastors: “We know all this well enough without you.”

> *Montaigne.*

3

> The narrow gate and the tight way that leads into life is the way of a good life. The spacious gate and the wide way, along which many walk, is the church. And this does not mean that there is something in the church itself and in its tenets that destroys people, but that entering the church and acknowledging its statutes or performing its rites is taken by people for that by which they want truly to serve God.

> *Kant.*

4

> When people, for the sake of custom or fashion or for some other worldly consideration, profess or say that they believe what they do not believe, or cannot put forward any ground for why they believe—they violate their morality and, no longer being honest with themselves, they easily become untruthful toward others. For a person's happiness it is necessary that he be spiritually true to himself. Infidelity lies not in believing or not believing, but in professing a faith in which you do not believe. Incalculable moral evil has been and is being produced by this mental lie.

> When a person has so far corrupted and defiled the purity of his soul that he has acknowledged himself a believer in things in which he does not believe, he is ready for the commission of every crime. Can one imagine anything more ruinous to morality than this.

> With a pious lie it is the same as with a bad deed: it gives birth to an unhappy necessity of continuation.

> To the extent that a person hides his conviction in order to save his orthodoxy from suspicion, or twists words <!-- vol. 42, p. 431 --> from their usual use in order to defend his own, to that extent he clouds and corrupts his reason, undermines the honesty of his character.

> *Channing.*

5

> Religion makes the gods participants in the basest deeds.

> *Titus Livius.*

6

*The church is nothing other than an arbitrary assertion of the holiness and infallibility of those people who call themselves the church.*

The more unreasonable, the more harmful an institution is, the greater the external grandeur with which it is surrounded—otherwise it could not attract anyone. Such is the church.

The solemnity and external splendor of church rites are the chief signs of its unreasonableness and harmfulness.

4 April

State organization is a temporary form, which arose in its time and in its time is subject to destruction. That time has come. State organization in our time is incompatible with the consciousness of the majority.

1

> To renounce ideal justice before the might of power—in this lies the only unbelief, the only atheism that we should fear. Because only then is that better world which we are conscious of around us—the world of moral truth and beauty—destroyed, and it is not worth living in the ruins of this world.

> *Salter.*

2

The strength of governments lies in this, that they have in their hands a self-feeding circle of power: false teaching produces power, and power makes it possible to spread false teaching, removing all that is contrary to it, that exposes it.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 432 -->

3

> There is nothing surprising in the fact that sovereigns and ministers, wishing to make their revenues as large as possible, always give preference to the method that allows them, as the proverb says, “to pluck the goose so that it does not cry out”; and there is nothing surprising in the fact that persons at the head of popular government are inclined to the same preference. However, for this same reason for which indirect taxes are so pleasing to the persons who impose taxes—for that very same reason a people that wishes to preserve its freedom must insist that the taxes collected for revenue be direct and not indirect.

> *Henry George.*

4

> I do not say that a person must renounce his rights, but I affirm that these rights exist only as consequences of the duties he must fulfill, and that we ought to begin with fulfilling our duties in order to win our rights.

> *Mazzini.*

5

> When great social questions urgently demand one solution or another, then only for a short time can the voice of reason be heard. The popular masses, generally speaking, hardly think. It is terribly hard to make them calmly discuss anything even in sober moments. But when passions take hold of them, they become like herds of frightened bulls. I am not afraid that the existing order will continue. That is impossible. But I am afraid that the dams will hold until the flood acquires a furious force. I am afraid that stubborn resistance on one side may kindle the flame of passions on the other. I am afraid of demagogues and grave accidents.

> *Henry George.*

6

> A person who wishes to serve truth and justice must be ready to remain alone. On some days he will indeed find sympathy, but not in days of internecine strife, and not at the time when he will especially need this sympathy.

> *Bersier.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 433 -->

7

By an enormous, many-sided labor of thought and word, understanding spreads among people, is assimilated by them in the most diverse forms, and, using the strangest means, it seizes people—some out of fashion, some out of boastfulness, under the guise of the liberalism of science, philosophy, religion—it becomes natural to people. People believe that they are brothers, that one must not oppress brothers, that one must help progress, education, struggle against superstitions; it becomes public opinion, and suddenly... the terror of the French Revolution, 1 March, the murder of Carnot—and all the labors are lost for nothing. Just as water gathered drop by drop by a dam goes off at one blow and to no use washes away the fields and meadows.

How can the anarchists not see the harm of violence.

Everything is so, everything is right in what they reason and do, spreading notions of the uselessness, the harm of state violence. Only one thing they must replace: violence, murder—with nonparticipation in violence and murder.

8

> Societies are formed from our needs, and governments from our sins.

> *Thomas Paine.*

*For the destruction of the state organization that has outlived its time there is only one means: nonparticipation in that violence by which the state is maintained.*

12 April

Church faith is slavery.

1

> It always seems to me that the god who is worshiped in all civilized countries is not divine at all, although he is called God. People revere one another, not God.

> *Thoreau.*

2

Of all forms of slavery, religious slavery to tyrants—theologians—is the most terrible, because in it lies the root of every other slavery.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 434 -->

3

> When people lack the courage to rely on their inner conviction (the voice of the deity within us), then they become the victims and instruments of theological sophists, who promise them paradise somewhere in a distant future.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

4

*The most pitiful slave is the person who gives his reason into slavery and acknowledges as truth what his reason does not acknowledge.*

5

> There are few true Christians—I am speaking even of faith. There are many who believe only out of superstition, and many who do not believe, but only out of dissoluteness; there are few people who occupy the middle.

> *Pascal.*

Any slavery is easier to bear than religious slavery. A person who is servile before church despotism will be the slave of all those masters who want to take possession of him.

29 April

Christianity proclaimed the true law of God, but mankind was far from it. And so, feeling all the beauty, truth, beneficence of this law and, at the same time, its incompatibility with the established life, people accepted Christianity in words, but in deed distorted it so as not to change the existing order of life.

1

> True worship of God is free of superstition; when superstition penetrates it, it is destroyed. Christ showed us what true worship of God consists in. He taught that of all that we do in our life, one thing is the light and happiness of people—this is our love for one another; he taught that we can attain our happiness only when we serve people and not ourselves.

> *Pascal.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 435 -->

2

> Orthodox religious systems of various kinds are in considerable measure responsible for the chaos and corruption that are revealed in our worldly affairs. Touching on the evil that fills people's everyday life, church preachers of various kinds, in their meetings and talks, usually declare that it is not the business of religion to touch on trade, politics, or finance. “Religion,” they say, “must deal only with the matter of a person's salvation in his future life; it needs only that people believe that only through Jesus can they inherit heavenly bliss.” “The believer will go to paradise, the unbeliever will go to hell.”

> It is these teachings, together with the doctrines of the fall, redemption, and bodily resurrection, that usually tangle up the small remnant of thought which orthodox people apply to reasoning about religious questions, and the chaos that then sets in in their religious life is naturally reflected in their social life too.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

3

*Strange as this seems, church Christianity is not a formal, incomplete, distorted Christianity, but a teaching completely alien to Christianity and hostile to it.*

In order to profess true Christianity, one must first of all renounce the false.

And this is what all people who want to be Christians do.

4

> The resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead is in essence a spiritual resurrection, revealed to believers alone according to the degree of their understanding, by which I understand that Christ was called from life into eternity and that after his passion he rose from the dead (in the sense in which Christ said: leave the dead to bury the dead), that is, that he was exalted by his life and death, giving an example of incomparable holiness. In this same sense he raises his disciples from the dead insofar as they follow the example of his life and death. And I think that it is easy to explain the whole teaching of the Gospel by means of such a mode of expression.

> *Spinoza.*

Church teachings have distorted the true meaning of Christianity to such a degree that people who believe in nothing, who reject the whole Christian teaching, are closer to it than churchmen who ostensibly acknowledge it.

16 May

9

Many church Christians will be found who would sooner die than pour the communion out into a slop pit, and how few true Christians will be found who would sooner be ready to die than take part in the killing of people.

And if such reverence for a thing could be fostered, then the same reverence for the life of people could be fostered too.

18 May

The acceptance in our time of ancient religious legends as unquestionable truths is the source of the greatest calamities and sufferings of the people of our time.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 436 -->

1

> There can be three kinds of false faith that give us the possibility of transgressing the bounds of our reason with a view to attaining the supernatural.

> The first false faith is faith in the possibility of knowing through experience what, by the laws of experience, cannot be admitted. (Faith in miracles.)

> The second false faith consists in admitting, for our moral perfecting, that of which we can form no concept with our reason. (Faith in mysteries.)

> The third false faith is the acknowledgment of the possibility of calling forth by supernatural means such a mysterious action, through which the deity will influence our morality. (Faith in grace.)

> *Kant.*

2

> People in general believe in miracles with more difficulty than in traditions about miracles; and many a Turk, Jew, and so on, who would now let himself be killed for his traditions, would remain perfectly cool before a miracle. When a miracle happens, it receives no other assessment than the one given to it by the significance of the miracle itself.

> To explain a contemporary miracle by physical causes is not yet freethinking, just as it is not blasphemy to consider it a fraud. In general, the denial of a fact is in itself something innocent; it becomes dangerous in the world only insofar as <!-- vol. 42, p. 437 --> it contradicts the conviction of others, who have taken its certainty under their protection. Miracles must be looked at from a distance if you want to accept them as true, just as clouds too, if you want to take them for solid bodies.

> *Lichtenberg.*

3

> The demand for miracles is a proof of little faith. A true Christian needs neither demands nor miracles...

> If priests held firmly to the Gospel, they would prefer to speak to the people about the teaching of Christ instead of telling of imaginary miracles.

> *Jan Hus.*

4

> The idea of miracles comes from our pride, which makes us think that we are such important beings that for our sake the supreme being must break the whole order of the world.

> *Montaigne.*

5

> People ask for fresh bread, fresh butter, fresh eggs, and the like, for they understand that the fresher the food, the healthier and more nourishing it is; but when it comes to religion, here the older the spiritual food, the more it is valued.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

6

> That it is said in some book that it is from God is not yet a proof that it really is from God; but that our reason is from God is beyond doubt—the word God may be understood here however one likes.

> *Lichtenberg.*

7

> Never teach a child what you yourselves are not sure of, and if you want to instill something in him in his tender years, so that the purity of childhood and the force of first associations imprint it in him, then beware most of all lest it be a lie that you yourselves know to be a lie.

> *John Ruskin.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 438 -->

8

> Not infrequently one has to reproach people for excessive obedience. This is as natural and ruinous a vice as distrustfulness.

> *Pascal.*

Miracles, that is, stories of violations of the laws of nature in the past, present, and future, are not only of no use for anything, since they cannot convince people of anything, but are always harmful, dividing people.
