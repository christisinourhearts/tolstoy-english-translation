<!-- vol. 41, p. 438 -->

25 June

The freer a person is from pleasing people, from vanity, the easier it is for him to serve God, and vice versa.

1

> Live not so that others may think of you in a certain way, but so that you yourself may think well of yourself.

> *Lucy Mallory.*

2

> Those very faults which in others are heavy and unbearable seem in ourselves to weigh nothing at all: one does not feel them; some people, speaking of another and depicting him in a terrible light, do not notice that they are painting themselves.

> Nothing would cure us of our faults more quickly than if we were able to see ourselves in others. Seeing our faults at such a distance, as they really are, we would hate them as they deserve.

> *La Bruyère.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 439 -->

3

The comfort of the good is in their conscience, not in the mouths of people.

4

Man has an irresistible urge to believe that he is not seen when he sees nothing—like children who shut their eyes so as not to be seen.

It is very useful to picture to oneself the impression that our life, our actions, make on others.

5

> The quickest and surest means of being reputed a virtuous man is to work on oneself so as to be one. Examine all the virtues, and you will see that they all increase through labor and exercise.

> *Conversations of Socrates.*

6

> They condemn the one who is silent; they condemn the one who talks much; they condemn the one who talks little too. There is no person who is not condemned.

> *Dhammapada.*

7

> A praiseworthy trait in a person is modesty: the modest person will not soon sin.

> *The Talmud*

8

*Never justify yourselves.*

9

> Prefer a stranger who loves the truth to those close to you who do not respect it.

> *Demophilus.*

10

> Will you call happy the man who places his happiness in his children, in friends, in things unstable and perishing? In a single instant all his well-being may collapse.

> *Demophilus.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 440 --> Do not acknowledge any other support for yourselves except yourselves and the deity.

11

Vanity is the feeling most incompatible with true sorrow, and at the same time this feeling is so firmly grafted onto the nature of man that very rarely does even the strongest grief drive it out.

Vanity in sorrow expresses itself in the desire to seem either grieved, or unhappy, or firm; and these low desires, which we do not confess, but which almost never—even in the deepest sadness—leave us, deprive us of the compassion that a neighbor's grief usually arouses in people.

A share of vanity, of the desire for human praise, is mixed into the very best actions. This desire is harmless only when a person can say to himself that, had he earned blame instead of praise for his conduct, he would not have changed it.
