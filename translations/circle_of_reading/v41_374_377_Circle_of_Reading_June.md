## AFTERWORD TO CHEKHOV'S STORY “THE DARLING”

There is a story, deep in meaning, in the “Book of Numbers” about how Balak, king of Moab, invited Balaam to come to him in order to curse the people of Israel, who had drawn near to his borders. Balak promised Balaam many gifts for this, and Balaam, tempted, went to Balak, but on the way he was stopped by an angel, whom the donkey saw but Balaam did not see. Despite this stop, Balaam came to Balak and went up with him onto the mountain, where an altar with slaughtered calves and sheep had been prepared for the cursing. Balak waited for the curse, but Balaam, instead of cursing, blessed the people of Israel.

Chapter 23 (11) “And then Balak said to Balaam: What are you doing to me? I took you to curse my enemies, and here you are blessing?

(12) And Balaam answered and said: Must I not say in full what the Lord puts into my mouth?

(13) And Balak said to him: Come with me to another place... and curse it from there.”

And he took him to another place, where sacrifices had also been prepared.

But Balaam again blessed instead of cursing.

So it was at the third place as well.

Chapter 24 (10) “And Balak's anger was kindled against Balaam, and he threw up his hands, and Balak said to Balaam: I called you to curse my enemies, and you are blessing, and now this is the third time.

(11) So go off to your place; I wanted to honor you, but see, the Lord deprives you of honor.”

And so Balaam went away without receiving the gifts, because instead of cursing he had blessed Balak's enemies.

What happened to Balaam very often happens to true poet-artists. Whether tempted by Balak's promises—popularity—or by his own false, instilled view, the poet does not see even the angel who stops him and whom the donkey sees, and he wants to curse, and look, he blesses.

This very thing happened to the true poet-artist Chekhov when he wrote this lovely story, “The Darling.”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 375 --> The author obviously wants to laugh at the creature “the Darling,” pitiful by his reasoning (but not by his feeling), who now shares Kukin's cares about his theater, now is immersed in the interests of the timber trade, now, under the influence of the veterinarian, considers the fight against pearl disease the most important business, and now, finally, is absorbed in questions of grammar and the interests of the little schoolboy in the big cap. Kukin's surname is funny, funny even his illness and the telegram announcing his death, funny the timber merchant with his staidness, funny the veterinarian, funny the boy too, but not funny, rather holy and wonderful, is the soul of “the Darling,” with her capacity to give herself with her whole being to the one she loves.

I think that in the reasoning, not in the feeling, of the author when he wrote “The Darling” there hovered a vague idea of the new woman, of her equal rights with man, of the developed, learned, independent woman, working for the good of society no worse than, if not better than, a man, of that very woman who has raised and keeps up the woman question; and he, beginning to write “The Darling,” wanted to show what a woman ought not to be. The Balak of public opinion invited Chekhov to curse the weak, submissive, undeveloped woman devoted to man, and Chekhov went up the mountain, and the calves and rams were laid out, but, having begun to speak, the poet blessed what he had wanted to curse. I at least, despite the marvelous, merry comedy of the whole work, cannot read certain passages of this wonderful story without tears. I am moved by the account of how she loves Kukin and everything Kukin loves with complete self-denial, and likewise the timber merchant, and likewise the veterinarian, and still more by how she suffers when, left alone, she has no one to love, and how, finally, with all the force of both womanly and motherly feeling (which she had not directly experienced), she gave herself up to boundless love for the future man, the little schoolboy in the big peaked cap.

The author makes her love the funny Kukin, the insignificant timber merchant, and the unpleasant veterinarian, but love is no less holy whether its object is Kukin or Spinoza, Pascal, Schiller, and whether its objects change as quickly as with “the Darling,” or the object is one for the whole of life.

Some time ago I happened to read in “Novoye Vremya” a fine feuilleton by Mr. At about women. The author expressed <!-- vol. 41, p. 376 --> in this feuilleton a remarkably intelligent and profound thought about women. “Women,” he says, “try to prove to us that they can do everything the same as we men. I not only do not dispute this,” says the author, “but am ready to agree that women can do everything that men do, and even, perhaps, do it better; but the trouble is that men cannot do anything coming close to what women can do.”

Yes, this is undoubtedly so, and it concerns not only the bearing, nursing, and early upbringing of children; men cannot do that highest, best work, the work that brings a person nearest to God—the work of love, the work of complete giving of oneself to the one you love—which good women have done, do, and will do so well and so naturally. What would become of the world, what would become of us men, if women did not have this quality and did not show it? Without women doctors, telegraph operators, lawyers, scientists, and authors we will manage, but without mothers, helpers, companions, comforters, who love in a man all that is best in him and by imperceptible suggestion call forth and sustain all that is best in him—without such women it would be hard to live in the world. There would have been no Mary and Magdalene with Christ, no Clare with Francis of Assisi, no wives of the Decembrists at hard labor, no wives among the Doukhobors, who did not hold their husbands back but supported them in their martyrdom for the truth, no thousands and thousands of unknown women—the very best, as everything unknown is—comforters of drunken, weak, dissolute people, those who need the consolation of love more than anyone. In this love, whether it is turned to Kukin or to Christ, lies the chief, great, irreplaceable strength of woman.

What an astonishing misunderstanding is the whole so-called woman question, which has taken hold, as must happen with every vulgarity, of the majority of women and even of men!

“Woman wants to perfect herself”—what could be more legitimate and just than that?

But a woman's work, by her very calling, is different from a man's work. And therefore the ideal of a woman's perfection cannot be the same as the ideal of a man's perfection. Let us grant that we do not know what this ideal consists in; in any case <!-- vol. 41, p. 377 --> it is beyond doubt that it is not the ideal of a man's perfection. And yet toward attaining this male ideal is now directed all that ridiculous and unkind activity of the fashionable women's movement, which so confuses women now.

I am afraid that Chekhov, when writing “The Darling,” was under the influence of this misunderstanding.

He, like Balaam, intended to curse, but the god of poetry forbade him and ordered him to bless, and he blessed and involuntarily clothed this dear creature in such a marvelous light that she will forever remain a model of what a woman can be in order to be happy herself and to make happy those with whom fate brings her together.

This story is so beautiful because it came out unconsciously.

I was learning to ride a bicycle in a riding hall in which divisions are reviewed. At the other end of the hall a lady was learning to ride. I thought about how not to get in this lady's way, and began to look at her. And, looking at her, I involuntarily began to come closer and closer to her, and, although she, noticing the danger, hurried to get away, I ran into her and knocked her down, that is, did the complete opposite of what I wanted, only because I had directed intense attention at her.

The same thing, only in reverse, happened with Chekhov: he wanted to knock the Darling down, and turned on her the intense attention of a poet, and raised her up.

*L. Tolstoy.*
