<!-- vol. 42, p. 135 -->

10 October

Man, as an animal, cannot help resisting death; but as a spiritual being he does not know death and therefore can neither resist death nor desire it.

1

> The reason why the idea of death does not have the effect it might have lies in this, that by our nature, as active beings, we really ought not to think about it at all.

> *Kant.*

2

> Life has nothing in common with death. That is probably why we are never abandoned by a vague hope that darkens reason and makes us doubt the correctness of our <!-- vol. 42, p. 136 --> knowledge of the inevitability of death. Life strives to persist in being. It repeats, like the parrot in the fable, even at the moment when it is being strangled: “This, this is nothing!”

> *Amiel.*

3

*In the last moments before death the spiritual principle leaves the body, and whether, leaving it, it unites with the timeless, spaceless principle of all or passes into another, again limited form, we do not know; we know only that as a result of death the body is left by that which animated it and becomes only an object of observation.*

4

Death is a change or disappearance of the object of consciousness. But consciousness itself can no more be destroyed by death than a change of spectacle can destroy the spectator.

5

You came into this life not knowing yourself how, but you know that you came as that particular *I* that you are; then you walked, walked, reached the halfway point, and suddenly, half glad, half frightened, you balked and do not want to stir from the spot, to go farther, because you do not see what is there. But you did not see the place from which you came either, and yet you did come. You came in by the entrance gate and do not want to go out by the exit gate.

Your whole life has been a procession through fleshly existence; you walked, hurried to walk, and suddenly you became sorry that the very thing is taking place which you were ceaselessly doing. The great change in your condition at fleshly death frightens you, but such a great change took place in you at your birth, and not only did nothing bad come of it for you, but on the contrary something so good came of it that you do not even want to part with it.

If we believe that everything that has happened to us in our life has happened to us for our good—and a person who believes <!-- vol. 42, p. 137 --> in the good principle of life cannot help believing this—then we cannot help believing that what takes place with us when we die also takes place for our good.
