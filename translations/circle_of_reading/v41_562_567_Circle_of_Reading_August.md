## SOLITUDE

After a merry dinner in a bachelor company, my old friend said to me:

— Wouldn't you like to take a walk along the Champs-Élysées?

And we went, walking slowly, along the long avenue between trees thinly clothed in leaves. Not the slightest noise, except <!-- vol. 41, p. 563 --> that eternal muffled rumble of the ceaseless Paris traffic. A fresh breeze blew in our faces, and over the dark sky was scattered the golden dust of countless stars.

My companion began to speak:

— I don't know why, but at night I breathe more easily here than anywhere. My thought seems to grow. At moments my mind is lit up by bright flashes of light, and in those instants it seems to me that we shall guess the divine mystery of life. But the window slams shut... And it's over.

Double shadows flickered now and then between the trees; we passed a bench; a couple sitting side by side on it merged into one dark spot.

My friend said:

— Pitiable people! Not disgust but boundless pity is what I feel for them. Of all the mysteries of human life I have grasped only one: the torture of our existence is that we are eternally alone, and everything we do, we do in order to flee from this solitude. There, these lovers too, the couples on the benches under the open sky, are seeking, as we are, as all living beings are, the possibility of escaping their solitude at least for a moment; but they remain, and will forever remain, alone; and so do we.

Some people feel this more strongly, others more weakly, that is all.

For some time now I have been undergoing an unbearable torture: I have understood, I have grasped my terrible solitude, and I know that nothing—do you understand?—nothing in the world has the power to end it. All our attempts, efforts, impulses of the heart, all the appeals of our lips, all our embraces are in vain and in vain—we are always alone.

I drew you here, on this walk, so as not to go home—the solitude of my apartment now torments me unbearably. But this too is no use. I speak, you listen to me, we walk the two of us, side by side, together, but each of us is alone. Do you understand me?

“Blessed are the poor in spirit,” says the scripture. These have not lost the phantom of happiness. They do not know our grief of solitude, they do not wander along the path of life as I do, touching people only with their elbows, with no other joy than the egoistic satisfaction of understanding, seeing, guessing, and endlessly suffering from the consciousness of one's eternal solitude.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 564 --> You think that I have gone mad. Isn't that so?

But hear me out. Since I felt the solitude of my existence, it seems to me that with each day I am sinking deeper into some gloomy underground vault whose edges and end I do not see and from which, perhaps, there is no way out at all. I walk through it, but neither with me nor beside me is there a single living being. The underground vault is our life. At times I hear noise, voices, cries... I hurry toward them, groping. But where they come from I never know for certain; I meet no one, I do not find the hand of another person in the darkness that surrounds me. Do you understand?

There were people who sometimes divined this terrible suffering. Musset exclaims:

Who is that coming? Calling me?—No one.  
I am alone, as always—the hour has struck.  
O solitude! O poverty!

But with him it was a fleeting doubt, not complete certainty, as with me. He was a poet; he filled life with phantoms, with dreams. He was never entirely alone, as I am.

Gustave Flaubert, that great unfortunate of this world, because he was one of the few great seers, wrote to a woman friend these despairing words: “We are all in a desert; no one understands anyone.”

Yes, no one understands anyone; whatever we think, whatever we say, whatever we do—no one understands anyone. Does the earth know what goes on in these stars, scattered like fiery grains in space, so far away that we see only the most insignificant part of them, while their other countless hosts are lost in infinity? And perhaps these stars are so close to one another that they make up one whole, like the molecules of one body?

And as the earth does not know what goes on in these stars, so man does not know what takes place in another man. We are farther from one another than these heavenly bodies, and above all—more disunited, because thought is bottomless.

What can be more terrible than this constant contact of beings together with the impossibility of merging with them? We love as if we were chained close to one another, and, stretching out our arms, we cannot unite. A tormenting need <!-- vol. 41, p. 565 --> for union gnaws at us; but all our efforts are vain, our impulses barren, outpourings useless, embraces powerless, caresses empty. We want to merge, but for all our strivings, we only knock against each other. I feel most alone when I give my heart to another person: then this impossibility becomes still more evident to me. There he is—this person—he looks at me with his clear eyes, but his soul, behind them, I do not know. He listens to me. But what is he thinking? Yes, what is he thinking? Don't you understand this torment? Perhaps he hates me, despises me, laughs at me? He weighs all my words, judges, mocks, condemns me, considers me a mediocrity or a fool? How can one know what he is thinking? How can one know whether he loves me as I love him, and what stirs in his little round head? What a terrible mystery—the unknown thought of another being, a thought hidden and free, which we can neither know, nor direct, nor curb, nor conquer.

And I?.. However I try to give myself up, to open all the doors of my soul—it is impossible for me. Always at the bottom, at the very bottom, there remains a secret corner of my “I,” where no one penetrates. No one is able to open it, to enter there, because no one is like me, and no one understands anyone.

At least at this moment, do you understand me?—No. You think me mad! You are scrutinizing me, you are on your guard against me! You ask yourself: what is the matter with him? But if someday you succeed in understanding my terrible and refined suffering, oh, come then, only to say: “I have understood you”—and I will be happy, if only for a moment.

Women especially make me feel my solitude more strongly.

Oh, woe, woe! How I have suffered from them! More often than men they aroused in me the false hope that I was not alone.

When you enter into love, it seems that you are expanding; a superhuman bliss takes hold of you. Do you know why? Do you know where this sensation of enormous happiness comes from? Solely because you imagine yourself no longer alone. Solitude, the estrangement of the human being, has apparently ended. What a pitiful delusion!

<!-- vol. 41, p. 566 --> Woman is tormented even more than we are by that eternal need of love which gnaws at our lonely heart; and it is this very woman who makes up the chief lie of the dream.

You know the sweet hours face to face with this being, long-haired, captivating, whose glances alone drive us mad. Mad rapture clouds the mind! A wondrous illusion takes hold of us! It seems that any moment now she and I will merge into one. But it only seems so, and after weeks of waiting, hopes, and lying joys, I feel myself alone even more strongly than before.

After every kiss, after every embrace, solitude grows. And how terrible it is, how tormenting! The poet Sully Prudhomme says:

All caresses are only mad impulses,  
The fruitless attempts of pitiful love  
To attain by a union of bodies the impossible fusion of souls.

And then farewell. All is over! We hardly recognize the woman who for a moment was everything to us and whose innermost thought, banal of course, we never even knew.

Even in those minutes when it seems to us that in the mysterious accord of our beings, in the complete mingling of desires and of all strivings, we have penetrated to the very depth of her soul—a word, a single word, said by her by chance, uncovers our self-deception and, like lightning at night, lights up the abyss lying between us.

And yet there is nothing better than these evenings with a beloved woman—silent evenings, when you feel almost happy from her presence alone. Let us not demand more, because never will two beings merge together!

As for me, I have now shut my soul against everyone. I tell no one what I believe, what I think, what I love. Knowing that I am condemned to a terrible solitude, I look at everything with indifference and do not speak my mind. What do I care about other people's opinions, quarrels, pleasures, and beliefs. Being unable to share anything with people, I am indifferent to everything. My invisible thought remains unexplored. I have banal phrases in answer to everyday questions, and a smile when I do not want to answer.

Do you understand me?

<!-- vol. 41, p. 567 --> We walked the whole long avenue to the Arc de Triomphe de l'Étoile and went down to the Place de la Concorde; he spoke slowly and said much more besides, which I did not even remember.

At last he stopped before the granite obelisk standing on the Paris pavement. In the starlight there was barely outlined the long Egyptian profile of this monument in exile, on whose sides the history of its country is inscribed in strange signs. And suddenly, with a movement of his hand, my friend pointed to this obelisk and exclaimed:

— We are all like this stone!

And he went away without saying another word.

Whether he was drunk, mad, or wise, I do not know even now. At times it seems to me that he is right, at times—that he has gone mad.

*Guy de Maupassant.*
