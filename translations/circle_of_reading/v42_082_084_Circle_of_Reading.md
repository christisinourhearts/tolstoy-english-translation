<!-- vol. 42, p. 82 -->

29 September

Besides all the calamities and horrors of war, one of its greatest evils is the perversion of minds: there is an army, there are military expenses—this must be explained. It cannot be explained reasonably, and therefore reason is perverted.

1

> Here is what is said in Voltaire's tale by the character he presents, Micromegas, a being from another planet, talking with people:

> — O you rational atoms, in whom the eternal being has expressed its art and its power, you surely enjoy pure joys on your earthly globe, because, being so little material and so developed in spirit, you must spend your life in love and thought, since in this is the real life of spiritual beings.

> At this speech all the philosophers shook their heads, and one of them, the most candid, said that, with the exception of a small number of little-respected figures, all the rest of the population consists of madmen, villains, and unfortunates.

> — There is more corporeality in us than is needed, if evil comes from corporeality, and too much spirituality, if evil comes from spirituality, — he said. — So, for example, at this very minute thousands of madmen in hats are killing thousands of other animals in turbans or are being killed by them, and so it has gone on from time immemorial all over the earth.

> — And what do the little animals quarrel over?

> — Over some little piece of mud the size of your heel, — answered the philosopher, — and not one of the people who cut one another cares in the least about this little piece of mud. The only question for them is whether this little piece will belong to the one they call the sultan or to the one they call Caesar, although neither the one nor the other has ever seen this little piece of earth. And of those same animals who cut one another, almost none has ever seen the animal for whose sake they cut one another.

> — Wretches, — cried the inhabitant of the planet Sirius. — Can one imagine such insane fury! Really, <!-- vol. 42, p. 83 --> I feel like taking three steps and crushing the whole anthill of these ridiculous murderers.

> — Do not trouble to do that, — they answered him. — They take care of it themselves. Besides, it is not they who should be punished, but those barbarians who, sitting in their palaces, prescribe the killing of people and order that God be solemnly thanked for it.

> *Voltaire.*

2

> *Can there be anything more absurd than that a man has the right to kill me because he lives on the other side of the river and because his sovereign is in a quarrel with mine, though I have not quarreled with him?*

> *Pascal.*

3

> A time will come when peoples will understand the madness of war.

> Four centuries ago the inhabitants of Pisa and Lucca were divided by such cruel hatred that it seemed eternal, and that the most insignificant porter of Pisa would have considered it a shameful betrayal to accept anything from the very first citizen of Lucca. What, then, remains of this hatred now? What will remain of the absurd hatred of the Prussian for the Frenchman? One can boldly be sure that these feelings will seem to our descendants like the hatred of the Athenians for the Spartans or of the inhabitants of Pisa for the inhabitants of Lucca. People will understand that they have more needful things to do than attack one another; that their common enemies are poverty, ignorance, disease; and that their efforts should be directed against these terrible calamities, and not against their comrades in misfortune.

> *Charles Richet.*

4

> The various states of Europe have accumulated a debt of 130 billion, of which about 110 was contracted in the course of a single century. All this colossal debt was contracted exclusively for the expenses of war. In peacetime the European states keep more than 4 million people in their armies and can bring this <!-- vol. 42, p. 84 --> number up to 19 million in wartime. Two thirds of their budgets are swallowed up by interest on the debt and the upkeep of armies, on land and at sea.

> *Molinari.*

5

> If a traveler saw on some distant island people whose houses were set about with loaded guns, and around whose houses sentries walked day and night, he could not help thinking that the island was inhabited by nothing but robbers. Is it not the same with the European states?

> How little influence religion has on people, or how far we still are from true religion!

> *Lichtenberg.*

Do not try either to justify or to deny war and the existence of the military estate: every application of reasonable arguments to a plainly bad cause can only pervert the mind and heart.
