## I WOMEN

The calling of every person, man and woman, lies in serving people. With this general proposition, I think, all people who are not immoral agree. The difference between men and women in fulfilling this purpose is only in the means by which they achieve it, that is, in what they serve people with.

A man serves people both by physical work—acquiring the means of subsistence, and by mental work—studying the laws of nature in order to conquer it, and by social work—instituting forms of life, establishing relations among people. The means of serving people are very manifold for a man. All the activity of humanity, with the exception of childbearing and nursing, makes up the field of this service. A woman, however, besides her capacity to serve people by all the same means as a man, is called by her constitution, is drawn, to that service which alone is excluded from the sphere of man's service.

The service of humanity divides of itself into two parts: one, the increase of the good in existing humanity; the other, the continuation of humanity itself. Men are called chiefly to the first, since they are deprived of the possibility of serving the second. Women are called chiefly to the second, since they are exclusively capable of it. This distinction one cannot, must not, and it is sinful (that is, mistaken) to forget and to erase. From this distinction flow the duties of the one and the other—duties not invented by people but lying in the nature of things. From this same distinction flows the appraisal of the virtue and vice of woman and man—an appraisal that has existed in all ages and exists now, and will never cease to exist as long as there is and will be reason in people.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 297 --> It always has been and always will be that a man who spends the greater part of his life in the manifold physical and mental, social labor proper to him, and a woman who spends the greater part of her life in the labor proper exclusively to her of bearing, nursing, and rearing children, will equally feel that they are doing what they should, and, doing these things, will equally arouse the respect and love of other people, because both are fulfilling what is destined for them by their nature.

The calling of a man is more manifold and broader, the calling of a woman more uniform and narrower, but for that deeper; and therefore it always has been and always will be that a man who has hundreds of duties, having betrayed one or ten of them, remains not a bad, not a harmful person, one who has nevertheless fulfilled part of his calling. But a woman, who has a small number of duties, having betrayed one of them, at once falls morally lower than a man who has betrayed ten of his hundred duties. Such has always been the general opinion, and such it will always be, because such is the essence of the matter.

In order to fulfill the will of God, a man must serve him in the sphere of physical labor, and of thought, and of morality: he can fulfill his purpose by all these works. For a woman the means of serving God are chiefly and almost exclusively (because no one besides her can do this) children. Only through his works is a man called to serve God and people; only through her children is a woman called to serve.

And therefore love for her *own* children, implanted in woman, an exclusive love, which it is quite useless to fight against by reasoning, always will be and must be proper to the woman who is a mother. This love for the child in infancy is not egoism at all; it is the love of a workman for the work he is doing while it is in his hands. Take away this love for the object of one's work, and work is impossible.

It is the same with the mother. A man is called to serve people through manifold works, and he loves these works while he is doing them. A woman is called to serve people through her children, and she cannot help loving these children of hers while she bears, nurses, and brings them up.

In the general calling—to serve God and people—man and woman are entirely equal, despite the difference in the form <!-- vol. 42, p. 298 --> of this service. The equality is in this, that the one service is just as important as the other, that the one is unthinkable without the other, that the one conditions the other, and that for real service knowledge of the truth is equally necessary to man and to woman, without which the activity both of man and of woman becomes not useful but harmful to humanity. A man is called to carry out his manifold labor; but his labor is useful only then, and his work—physical, and mental, and social—is fruitful only then, when it is done in the name of truth and the good of other people.

It is the same with a woman's calling: her bearing, nursing, and rearing of children will be useful to humanity only when she raises not simply children for her own joy, but future servants of humanity; when the upbringing of these children is carried out in the name of truth and for the good of people, that is, when she brings up her children so that they may be the best workers for other people.

“Well, and those who have no children, who have not married, widows?”

They will do excellently if they take part in men's manifold labor.

Every woman who has finished bearing children, if she has the strength, will have time to take up this helping of man in his labor. A woman's help in this labor is very precious; but to see a young woman, ready for childbearing and occupied with man's labor, will always be a pity. To see such a woman is the same as to see precious black earth covered with rubble for a parade ground or a promenade. More pitiful still: because this land could bring forth only grain, while a woman could bring forth that for which there can be no appraisal, higher than which there is nothing—a human being. And only she alone can do this.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
