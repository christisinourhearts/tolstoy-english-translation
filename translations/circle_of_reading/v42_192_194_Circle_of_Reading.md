<!-- vol. 42, p. 192 -->

3 November

There is only one immutable law—the law of God, common to all people; human law, however, can be law only so long as it is not at variance with the law of God.

1

> Jesus, answering them, said: my teaching is not mine, but his who sent me; whoever wants to do his will will know of this teaching, whether it is from God, or whether I speak from myself.

> *John 7:16–17.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 193 -->

2

> What is the voice of duty, if not the prompting of God?

> But perhaps it is a prescription of your own imagination? The imperative mood of your conversation with yourself?

> Or perhaps it is only an echo of human opinions, submission to the demands of public opinion?

> But no: if this were a law invented by ourselves, we could forgive ourselves for violating it, we could repeal it. But we feel that the force of this law is outside our power and that we cannot disregard it.

> Nor can we admit that this is the influence of public opinion, because this voice has often raised us above public opinion, has given us strength to struggle against the injustice of the crowd, to struggle in the name of good alone and without hope of success. You would sooner convince me that daylight is a product of my eyes or of public opinion than that the consciousness of good is not a direct consciousness of God.

> As sensations teach us about what is outside our body, so the consciousness of God teaches us about what is outside our spiritual personality; it teaches us that justice, kindness, and truth are not products of my personality but have been put into me by God.

> *Martineau.*

3

The first difficulty that now presents itself for the realization of the law of God consists in this, that the existing human laws are directly opposed to it.

4

> *Human laws are good and valuable only insofar as they conform to the law of God, applying and developing it. And they are always bad when they contradict this law, and in that case we are not only entitled but obliged to destroy them.*

> *Giuseppe Mazzini.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 194 -->

5

Every person, in order to set about studying the most important questions of life, must, before resolving them, also refute the edifices of lies heaped up over the centuries and propped up by all the powers of the mind's ingenuity on each of the most essential questions of life.

6

> The institution of government is in essence a clear sign that man has lost in social life the consciousness of his divinity and therefore must resort to external authority. Having lost this consciousness, he must lean on external law. But external law is always mistaken. If every person kept this consciousness, one with the consciousness of his neighbors, there could not be this discord; but when this consciousness weakens, artificial means become necessary to sustain it, and thus, with the weakening of the consciousness of his unity, there arises a form of government that does not represent a real expression of the life of the whole people, but only the externally coercive power of the ruling class.

> *After Carpenter.*

The law of God contradicts human law: what, then, is to be done? Hide the law of God and proclaim the human law? This has been done for 1900 years already, but the divine law becomes more and more visible, and the inner contradiction stronger and more tormenting. One thing remains: to replace human law with the law of God.
