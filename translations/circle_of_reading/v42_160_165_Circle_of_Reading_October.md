## LAMENNAIS

Great minds and ardent hearts, those people who leave a deep trace behind them, present in their lives with particular vividness the very stages of development that are passed through, <!-- vol. 42, p. 161 --> to a greater or lesser degree, by all ordinary people.

These stages are as follows: 1) Childlike, instilled faith, complete submission to authority, calm and confident relations with all those around. 2) Going deeper into the essence of this faith, instilled and accepted on trust, unspoken doubts of its truth, and a particular fervor in affirming and spreading it. Approval and praise from those around. 3) An attempt to cleanse the creed accepted on faith of everything false, superfluous, superstitious, to improve it and to base life on it; a break with those who had sympathized before, their ill will, and, finally, 4) complete liberation from the teaching accepted on trust, acknowledgment only of what is in accord with reason and conscience, consciousness of one's solitude among people and of one's unity with God, the lofty and strong love of a small number of those close to one, and the fear and hatred of the majority, and—the end.

All people, whether they want it or not, pass more or less consciously through these stages. First 1) complete trust, then 2) doubts, sometimes barely noticeable, but doubts all the same, then 3) attempts, sometimes the feeblest, but attempts all the same, to establish somehow their understanding of life, and finally 4) standing face to face with God, full knowledge of the truth, solitude, and—the end.

All people pass through these states, but in Lamennais they showed themselves with particular strength and fruitfulness.

Félicité Lamennais was born in Brittany in 1782. In 1816 he was ordained a priest. Although Lamennais had always been religious from childhood, he did not become a priest by his own desire alone. It is evident from the letters of Lamennais that he became a priest only because those close to him insisted on it. His family, seeing his religiousness, wanted to use it for the benefit of the church. And indeed, having become a priest, Lamennais devoted all his strength to the service of his Catholic Church, in whose truth he had no doubt. Lamennais saw the decline of faith in society and among the people and tried to raise it, proving its truth chiefly by the fact that Catholicism is the most widespread religion, acknowledged by the majority of mankind. In his opinion at that time, truth is not grasped by the individual person; truth is grasped only by people in their totality. The greatest number acknowledge Catholicism, and therefore the truth <!-- vol. 42, p. 162 --> of Catholicism is beyond doubt. And since Catholicism is the highest truth, the state too must submit to this truth. The state cannot be without religion, religion cannot be without the church, the church—without the pope.

Such was the conviction of Lamennais at that time. In this spirit he wrote one of his first works: “Essay on Indifference in Matters of Faith.” This was the first phase of the state of soul of Lamennais—faith without doubts. Since Lamennais placed his hopes in the power of the state as the protector of the Catholic religion, these thoughts brought him close to the extreme defenders of monarchical power, and he began to write in the newspaper “The Conservative.” In this newspaper he occupied an exceptional position, and while his collaborators wrote in defense of the monarchy and for its benefit, Lamennais always and above all had religion in view; monarchical power interested him only to the extent that it could contribute to the triumph of Catholicism. But soon Lamennais saw that the interests of power and of religion are not only not the same but for the most part opposed, so that at times it is even to the advantage of power to oppress religion. Seeing this, Lamennais changed his view and, moving to another newspaper, began to demand of power no longer help for religion and an alliance with it, but complete freedom and noninterference in the affairs of religion. Lamennais could not stop even at this, and soon went still further and began to demand the separation of the church from the state and, condemning the power of the government, involuntarily took the side of the revolutionaries, justifying the revolution of 1830. This was the second phase of the state of soul of Lamennais.

During the revolution of '30, Lamennais, in collaboration with Montalembert and Lacordaire, began to publish the journal “The Future,” in which were preached: 1) the separation of the church from the state, 2) guarantees for the individual, 3) the abolition of the chamber of peers and of the extremes of centralization, 4) the abolition of the compulsory property qualification and the establishment of universal suffrage. In this newspaper he expressed the thought that, just as the power of the state must not interfere in church affairs, so the power of the church must not take part in state affairs, and therefore the pope must renounce temporal power, and the clergy—state salaries. Such thoughts could not find sympathy in Rome. Foreseeing this, Lamennais went there, hoping to convince the papal authorities of the necessity of such concessions for the sake of <!-- vol. 42, p. 163 --> keeping the peoples under the power of the church. But in Rome Lamennais was not received by the pope, and no answer at all was given to his proposal. Already deeply disappointed in his hope of renewing Catholicism, Lamennais returned to Paris and for some time more continued to publish his journal, in which he expressed the thought that the forms of Catholicism must be changed so that it could continue to rule over the peoples. This was the third phase.

In 1832 a papal encyclical came out condemning all the thoughts expressed by Lamennais, and, hard as it was for Lamennais to renounce everything he had believed in and served, he acknowledged that Catholicism was incurable, incorrigible. From that time he broke off all relations with Rome and wrote his famous work “Words of a Believer.” In the form of biblical psalms and Gospel parables, Lamennais in this work attacks the existing economic and political order, which stands in contradiction to the demands of religion. This book was immediately condemned by the pope. And then Lamennais separated himself from the church completely and devoted the last years of his life to the service of the people; this was the fourth and last phase.

Lamennais spent the last years of his life far from political activity, almost in solitude and poverty, devoting himself exclusively to literary work. Among other things, he finished a sketch of a “philosophy” and wrote one of the best commentaries on the four Gospels.

The fundamental thought of Lamennais, which he expressed in all his books, articles, and speeches (when he was a deputy in parliament), is that the people must themselves be the deciders of their destinies and the arrangers of their life. As in his defense of the Catholic Church, he is guided by the same principle, that the bearer of truth and moral perfection can be not the individual person but people in their totality—the crowd, the people, and at its limit the human race. While giving full power to the people, Lamennais nevertheless never ceased to prove that no external reforms and changes in the sphere of the structure of the state can improve the condition of the people if the people do not constantly strive for moral perfecting. “Desire only what is just,” he said to the people. “Justice will always triumph. Respect the right even of those who trample on your right. Let the safety of all without exception be sacred to <!-- vol. 42, p. 164 --> you. Duty is binding for all and always. If you once violate duty, where will you stop? Disorder is not helped by disorder. Of what do your enemies accuse you? Of wanting to replace their domination with your domination, of wanting to abuse power as they abuse it—of harboring thoughts of revenge, tyrannical intentions. Hence the vague fear of you, which your enemies skillfully use to prolong your slavery.”

“Nothing is possible in the social sphere without spiritual work in the depths of society,” he said.

Lamennais always regarded socialist and communist systems negatively. In his opinion, these teachings do not take into account the laws of human nature, replacing the natural course of life with the violence of power. He disapproved of these teachings chiefly because they all had in view only material aims and did not acknowledge the necessity of religion. In every social reorganization he considered necessary not material but spiritual aims, achieved by the victory of reason and duty over the passions.

At the beginning of the fifties Lamennais fell ill and, feeling that his illness was mortal, called his friend Barbet and appointed him administrator, in the house during his lifetime while he was ill, and after his death by his will. Besides this, he left a written statement that he wished to be buried among the poor and as a poor man; he asked that no monument be placed on his grave; the body was to be carried straight to the cemetery and not brought into the church. Despite all the attempts of the clergy to bring him back to the church, Lamennais, as before, answered all attempts of this kind with a meek but decided refusal to receive a priest. He died calmly and firmly, with the same living faith in God with which he had lived the whole time of his conscious life. His last words were: “I feel that the end has come; I must submit to the will of God; it will be well with me when I am with him.” And in the very last minutes he repeated several times: “This is a happy minute.” He died on 27 February 1854.

The work of Lamennais, “l'oeuvre,” as the French say, is great and far from appreciated. He, like all great minds and ardent hearts, laid down the path along which mankind will inevitably go and is already going, the path of liberation from an external, life-detached, falsely Christian faith and of establishing that fundamental <!-- vol. 42, p. 165 --> Christian teaching which changes the life both of the individual person and of the whole of human society.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
