## THE DIVINE AND THE HUMAN

## \* No. 1 (MS. No. 3, to ch. VII).

And meanwhile the cart with the youth was driving through the city, arousing a curious horror in those people who saw it. Svetlogub was still in the same rapturous mood of tender emotion. He thought neither of his comrades, nor of the people who were looking at him, nor of what would be said about him, nor even of the girl he loved and his mother. He thought only of God, of how he had tried to do what God wanted of him, and now, going to that God from whom he had come forth, by whose will he had lived and to whom he is going, he thought only of this: that he was giving himself into his hands.

So he thought and felt, but his ears listened, his eyes looked. And then to his eyes [^1] there appeared two students and a girl, who had run out of a door at a trot with merry laughter. The first student jumped down from the porch and suddenly, seeing the cart, stood rooted to the spot. The same thing happened to the other two. Their merriment did not vanish, but stopped, hid for a time. At that minute they felt terribly sorry, but all the same life, merry, young, joyful life, was seething in them. And Anatoly suddenly felt sad, sorry, sorry for himself. He remembered how in childhood his mother had stood him in the corner, and he had stood there a long time indifferently, but when he heard his younger brother start singing a song, he suddenly felt sorry for himself and burst into tears. Just so he felt sorry for himself now, and he very nearly burst into tears. But this did not last long. And when the cart drove up to the gallows, Anatoly was praying again, and with a calm feeling he gave himself up to the executioner. There was only one moment of despair: when the rope tightened on his throat, and he wanted to save himself, and he felt that it was impossible.

## \* No. 2 (MS. No. 22, to ch. V).

— Merciful Lord, — the priest began with a sigh, in an accustomed voice. Svetlogub looked at him with wide-open eyes and did not let him finish.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 519 --> — Can it be? — Svetlogub said in a gasping voice, — can it be me... Can it be the death penalty?

— As a sacred minister I have come to offer the consolation in which the merciful Lord...

— I don't need anything, I don't need it.

All the priest's persuasions were in vain. Svetlogub, in a piteous, weeping voice, asked the priest to go away and leave him alone.

— Bear in mind that by my duty I am always ready.

— All right, later, for God's sake, later.

When the priest had gone, Svetlogub fell onto his bed and closed his eyes. He did not believe that he was he, that he was in prison, that the priest had come to prepare him for death. This is a dream, he thought, I must wake up; he jumped up and took five steps. No: the wall, and the door, and the painted-over window, and the remains of the dinner before death.

— Can it be death? — he asked himself. — I will die. I will be no more. How can I be no more? What will there be, if I am no more?.. It cannot be. Guard! — he shouted, knocking at the door.

The rag over the little window rose, and a freckled, broad, snub-nosed face appeared.

— Call the warden.

The rag dropped.

He went up to the little table on which lay the writing materials that had been brought to him and, in order to occupy himself, lifted the inkwell and looked at it against the light to see whether there was much ink in it. And then he suddenly remembered what awaited him, and the fingers that were holding the inkwell suddenly let it go. It fell to the floor, and the ink spilled.

He hastily made a little tube out of paper, lay down on the floor, and, as he used to do as a gymnasium boy, kept sucking up the ink and emptying what he had gathered into the inkwell, trying not to lose a single drop. During this occupation the warden came.

— Is it true that they will execute me? — he asked.

— I have no information. Do you need anything else?

— No, nothing...

The warden left. But he had only just left when Svetlogub began calling him again.

— What do you want?

— I spilled the ink. Send me some.

— But even this is enough.

— If it's enough, it's enough.

The warden left. Svetlogub sat down to write a letter to the girl whom he loved, who loved him but did not share his convictions. The desire to express his feeling for her as quickly as possible, and the impossibility of betraying his convictions, absorbed all his attention. Some places in his letter made him weep tears of tender emotion. Having finished the letter, he opened the Gospel. And the tender emotion called forth in him by composing the letter <!-- vol. 42, p. 520 --> continued while he read. Everything that he had read calmly before now resounded in his soul with particular force. Especially those places that spoke of death: “Truly I say to you, if a grain of wheat, having fallen on the earth, does not die, it will remain alone; but if it dies, it will bear much fruit,” he read. And he repeated: “truly, truly.” And these words moved him to such tender emotion that he got up and walked from wall to wall, trying to take exactly five steps and to turn on the fifth. He desired one thing now: to keep to the end this joyful state of tender emotion that raised him above the world.

“I should sleep,” he thought, “so as not to grow weak afterward. He lay down on the cot and closed his eyes, but could not fall asleep. In the excited state in which he was, he was particularly receptive to all new impressions. His eyes were closed, but his hearing, on the other hand, was particularly keen. He heard the steps of the sentries, some distant hum, and with incredible clarity felt the crawling and the bites of fleas over his body. He got up, took off his shirt, and in the light of the lamp saw several of them crawling over the coarse linen of the shirt. He was just about to seize one when it jumped off somewhere from under his fingers. Another he did catch, and he threw it, as he usually did, into the flame of the lamp. He fell to thinking. “But isn't it all the same? Sooner, later. It lived and finished. So will I. Sooner, later. Isn't it all the same?” He looked at his naked, handsome, though thin body. And he felt sorry for himself. He felt that the rapturous state of tender emotion was leaving him. “But it cannot be,” he suddenly cried out, and again began to walk quickly back and forth in his cage.

“It cannot be,” he said to himself. “Something is not right: either they will pardon me at the place of execution, or, as they wanted”—he was thinking of his friends—“they will bribe the guards and come this very night and set me free.”

And, strange to say, the thought of death was so terrible, so alien to him, that he could not believe, did not believe what would happen to him, and, having calmed down and grown tired from walking, toward morning he dropped onto his bed and fell asleep.

At six o'clock he was woken by the sound of bolts being drawn back. He listened: many people were walking, approaching his door. The procession stopped at his door, and the key was searching for the lock.

It was the warden and the escort. They told him to dress and go with them.

His heart contracted in torment, but he still did not believe. He could not believe that people, the same as he was, people, could do such a terrible deed to him.

## \* No. 3 (MS. No. 22, to ch. VII).

And meanwhile the cart with the youth was driving through the city, arousing a curious horror in those people who saw it. That state of tender <!-- vol. 42, p. 521 --> emotion in which Svetlogub had been during the night while writing the letter and reading the Gospel had vanished completely, and he could not call it up again. In his soul there was only horror, both before death and before the physical sufferings awaiting him, and most of all before those people who could so calmly, coolly, confidently do to him the most terrible deed that a man can do to a man. He could not picture to himself what awaited him, as a man cannot look straight at the sun. He was only crushed, all of him, by something. He was crushed, and at the same time his ears heard, his eyes looked, his reason reckoned. When they brought him out of the prison and were leading him up onto the cart, he saw that people were looking at him from all the windows, and this consciousness lifted him for an instant, and he took heart and called up on his face a calm, joyful expression, looking over the windows. As he drove through the city, he looked ahead of him the whole time, holding his handsome pale head high. And the consciousness that he was astonishing people with his calm gave him strength.

Driving along one of the streets, he noticed an ice-cream man handing out ice cream to the boys standing around him. One of the boys, the older one, seeing the cart, nudged his comrade with his elbow and stood rooted to the spot. Both froze with frightened, suffering faces. Svetlogub saw this, and he felt sorry for the boys, and he smiled at them. But when the cart was driving across the haymarket square, where the remains of the cartloads were left, and Svetlogub was struck by the smell of hay and horse dung that he had loved since childhood, this smell instantly carried him back to the happy time of childhood, of horses, coachmen, of the causeless joy of life. And such a wave of young, vigorous, joyful life lashed into Svetlogub's soul. Death seemed to him something altogether unbelievable. When the cart began to approach the gallows and he saw the posts with crossbeams, doubt came over him for an instant, and he trembled, but at once he mastered himself and remembered that they were looking at him, and his pale face went on smiling barely perceptibly. He still did not believe. But suddenly something terrible happened. A man, a simple, living man with a simple human face and eyes looking downward—this was the executioner—quickly, resolutely sprang up to Svetlogub, threw a sack over his head, and dragged him off somewhere by his hands, which were tied behind his back. At one and the same time Svetlogub felt the crude violence of this man, understood that no one could see him any longer now and that there was no reason now to display calm and solemnity on his face, and, above all, for the first time fully understood and believed that this was death, that insane annihilation which must not, cannot be.

And, understanding this, he cried out, jerked forward, but his hands were tied. What is this? Can it be? A jolt, tormenting pain, and the cessation of everything.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 522 --> The governor-general, who prided himself on his early rising, had by this time already finished his coffee and, looking through the German newspapers, was letting out through his overhanging mustaches the fragrant smoke of a foreign cigar—a gift from a rich banker.

## \* No. 4 (MS. No. 22, to ch. IX).

It was especially hard for him, above all, from the thought that they would forget him and he would die here alone of illness, or that they would strangle him, burn him. It seemed to him that he had not been afraid of death at liberty, but here death seemed terrible to him, and when he pictured it vividly to himself, he would beat on the door until someone came to his door and sternly forbade him to make noise, threatening him with punishment for it. And then another tormenting feeling would come over him, of malice toward this free man who came to his door and who could go everywhere, associate with people, see rivers, birds, and yet busied himself with that terrible torture of the soul, solitary confinement, which was being done to him; and he would curse the one who came up to his door, and this relieved him for a time... He tried not eating, starving himself to death, but could not hold out, and on the third day he threw himself on the food.

It is tormenting to desire and not receive what is desired, but when one can also not desire what is desired, such torment is bearable; but when one desires what one cannot help desiring—freedom—and it is not there, and this deprivation of the most lawful condition of life depends on people, and a feeling of malice mixes with the feeling of suffering, the state of the soul is terrible. And this horror Mezhenetsky lived through in the first month of his confinement.

## \* No. 5 (MS. No. 22, to ch. X).

Sometimes he devised new means of struggle against despotism and used them in his imagination. These means consisted of explosive bombs which he, rising in a steerable balloon, threw onto palaces, senates, councils of state; in general, as one sprinkles powder when getting rid of bedbugs, he sprinkled them over all the villains and their nests. All this, however, he imagined not all at once but gradually, with all the details. First he saw the balloon being inflated, then the cylindrical bombs being handed to him, how he packed them into the gondola, how the balloon was released and he flew over the city, choosing his victims. The revolutionary party triumphed everywhere, the government authorities weakened, and a gathering was convened. The liberated people sent elected representatives, and in the Kremlin a people's assembly sat.

The main thing—the people were liberated, owned all the land, paid only those taxes that they needed, and formed a great commune of people living as brothers.

Sometimes he arrived at this too quickly, and then he would begin again from the beginning and reach the goal by another way. The chief foundations of his dreams were hatred of the oppressors, the tsar, the ministers, <!-- vol. 42, p. 523 --> the rich, and love for the people, the Russian, simple, intelligent, kind, laboring people.

So he lived a year, two, three, and his health did not worsen. By governing his imagination he freed himself from involuntary hallucinations. Only rarely did fits of horror come over him, but they did not last long. He overcame them. And he grew used to his fantastic life, so that when the prisoners' situation was somewhat relaxed, he was perfectly fresh, healthy, and able to take advantage of the relaxations, the walks, the books, that were given to the prisoners; whereas the others either, having deliberately insulted the authorities, were hanged, or killed themselves, went mad, or grew so weak that they could no longer and did not want to take advantage of the relaxations that were given them.

Only a year after the event did he learn of the first of March, and he was in raptures over it, confident that this would awaken Russian people, would lead them to new forms of life, above all to the liberation of the people.

## \* No. 6 (MS. No. 22, to ch. VI and XI).

Although Mezhenetsky, remembering his activity among the people, could not in the depths of his soul help agreeing that the people, such as he knew them, were not ready for revolution, he could not agree that the people must be made landless and led through all the calamities of the proletariat in order to win freedom for themselves. He argued, proving the impracticality and cruelty of this method. But Herman listened to him, smiling condescendingly, and cited in confirmation of his view the opinions of the socialist figures of Europe, and above all the works of Marx, Engels, Kautsky. Listening to Herman, Mezhenetsky lost his calm and grew heated. Mezhenetsky told himself that what grieved him was that all these young people, of whom Herman was an exact representative, had renounced what had moved them, love for the people, theoretical, it is true, but love all the same, and were putting an empty, dead doctrine in its place; but in essence he lost his calm and grew heated because he felt that Herman was right. And when Herman allowed himself to say that what Kibalchich, Khalturin, Perovskaya had done had all been childishness and vain expenditures of forces, Mezhenetsky lost his temper.

— So how are you different from the vilest bourgeois and prosecutors, — he shouted, frowning darkly. — We sacrificed ourselves, our lives, and you sit quietly in your homes, enjoying life, and only preach.

— We're not enjoying life very much, — Herman said calmly, looking around at his comrades, and burst out laughing triumphantly with his uninfectious but loud, distinct, self-assured laugh.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 524 --> — We're not enjoying life all that much. And if we are sitting here, we owe it to the reaction, and the reaction is the product precisely of the terrorists.

Mezhenetsky fell silent, because he felt that he was about to burst into tears, it hurt him so.

Yes, these are his successors. Here they are, these representatives of the new generation. In their view, everything that they, Mezhenetsky and his friends, had done had been not only not what was needed, but not good either. During all the time of his confinement Mezhenetsky had consoled himself with the thought that if he and his friends were perishing and suffering, their cause, on the other hand, was moving forward, growing, and the aim they had set was being achieved.

And what then? He saw now that the direction had deviated to such a degree that this aim could no longer be achieved now.

“The whole people must be remade into factory workers”... But is that possible? And if it is possible, can one desire the sufferings that are bound up with this transition, and can one assist it? Is it honest to assist it without sharing these sufferings? But even if one grants that the whole people will be remade into proletarians, why think that they will settle into that form assigned to them in advance by theoreticians? And if one grants this too, will they be happier? We worked to achieve aims that we saw clearly: to destroy those sufferings of the people that were obvious. But these cold doctrinaires, in the name of what are they striving?

## \* No. 7 (MS. No. 22, to ch. XII).

Mezhenetsky went up to him.

— Recognize me? — asked the little old man.

— Why, of course, you were asking about faith then.

— I was asking, — the little old man confirmed.

— Well, did you find it? — asked Mezhenetsky.

— I found it, my dear. Glory to you, Lord, — the old man brought out with difficulty, and with his withered hand, in which there was nothing but bone, he crossed himself in the Old Believer way. — It was revealed in a vision.

— What was revealed?

— Why, that it's all in the spirit. As we're alive now, so we'll be alive. Like that lad, so I too know everything now. There's nothing to seek. God lives, my soul lives. So you too, my dear. But back then you spoke badly. Spoke badly. That's not how it should be. One must understand in the spirit. Everything is in the spirit. And in the spirit all is good. Now, glory to the Lord, everything is revealed to me.

And indeed the old man felt that the whole mystery of life and death had been revealed to him. The mystery was that the whole world and everything in it and, above all, one's own body—was a mere appearance, and that life is in the spirit, and for it there is neither diminishing, nor constraint, nor cutting off. Only live in the spirit, and everything will be nothing but joy. And so, <!-- vol. 42, p. 525 --> now, feeling the approach of death, the old man rejoiced. He knew that the change would be great, as if jumping off a steep bank, but he knew that, having jumped off, he would not be lost, but only something new, better, would open.

— But what is it that's revealed? — asked Mezhenetsky.

— Everything is revealed in the spirit. That's how it is. — And the little old man smiled slightly, significantly, and, as it seemed to Mezhenetsky, self-assuredly, madly.

## \* No. 8 (MS. No. 22, to ch. XIII).

XII

In the corridor he met the warder Krasavtsev. He was a talkative, good-natured man of about 50, always affectionate and obliging with all the politicals. He was walking with his keys, locking the cells. Seeing Mezhenetsky, he at once entered into conversation with him.

— They've brought in five again today, — Krasavtsev began.

— Brought whom? — asked Mezhenetsky.

— Politicals again. Two young ladies, very young, just children. It's a wonder. And what on earth do they rebel for.

— But you yourself say that the order of things is bad, so they want to arrange it better.

— But is that possible? That's what the authorities are for. Why rebel? What's the use? Only ruin yourself. Why, back in '63 in Poland. They rebelled too. And what came of it? Nothing but pity, the way we bayoneted those lads. So they perished for nothing.

— But why did you bayonet them?

— Couldn't be helped, the oath. And of course one's sorry. Keenly sorry. But there's no getting around it. You can't break an axe-butt with a whip. That's how it is.

— Good night, sleep well, — he said. — I won't lock you in; you'll shut yourself in.

“Can't break it, that's how it is,” Mezhenetsky repeated, staring dully before him, and, having seen Krasavtsev off, he went on walking along the corridor with an even step, not raising his head. Mezhenetsky, during his revolutionary activity, disguised as a worker, had lived among the people and knew the whole inertness, as he put it, of the Russian peasant; he had also come together with soldiers, both in service and retired, and knew their dull faith in the necessity of obedience and the impossibility of acting on them by reasoning. He knew all this, but neither earlier nor later had he drawn from this knowledge the conclusion which [^2] inevitably followed [^3] from it. Only now, in the mood into which the conversation with the new revolutionaries had brought him, did he suddenly understand from Krasavtsev's words <!-- vol. 42, p. 526 --> the whole futility, uselessness, absurdity of all his past activity, and still more of that aimless, fantastic activity to which the new revolutionaries were now devoting themselves. Millions of people like Krasavtsev, drilled, disciplined, stupefied, were in the hands of the government. In the hands of the government were [^4] billions of rubles every year, there was an organized gang of officials, there was the school, there was literature, the press, and the lying, stupefying church. And the government knows its own untruth, knows that if it lets the truth triumph it will perish, and therefore, from a feeling of self-preservation, it unerringly and pitilessly crushes everything that may present even the slightest danger to it.

And it is against this powerful organization, against these hundred million Krasavtsevs, against these billions, against these censorships, the church, that tens, hundreds, let us say thousands of persecuted, poor men and women, bustling about Russia and disagreeing, arguing among themselves, want to fight.

“Now that really would be funny, if it were not so—not sad, but terrible,” he said to himself, and laughed maliciously with his throat alone. His whole life ruined because of trifles. To renounce his past is still worse. And not to renounce it means to lie.

## \* No. 9 (MS. No. 27, to ch. V).

This day was a peculiar one somehow for Svetlogub. Again steps were heard along the corridor and stopped at his door. The door opened, and an officer unknown to him came in, with red waxed mustaches and a smell of pomade, and, not looking at him, began to read him a long paper written in the ordinary language of the chancellery. The paper said that for proven participation in revolutionary activity directed at the overthrow of the existing government, Anatoly Svetlogub, candidate of law, was sentenced by a military court to deprivation of all rights of status and to the death penalty by hanging. The sentence of the court had been confirmed by His High Excellency the governor-general and was to be carried into execution on this 22 October 1878. Svetlogub, listening attentively to the reading of the paper, could not understand the significance of what was being announced to him. It was being announced to him that tomorrow he must die. This was an event the like of which he had never experienced. [^5] It was something unbelievable, terrible, that changed everything in the world, and yet the officer was adjusting his saber with his left hand in a suede glove, his mustaches were pomaded, and the guard was standing respectfully by the door, and the walls, and the little table, and the mug—everything was as always. This news was so incongruous with everything that had been and is, that Svetlogub heard, understood what had been read, but did not believe it.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 527 --> Only long after the officer had gone and everything had grown quiet did Svetlogub begin to understand the full significance of the terrible news the officer had communicated to him.

“Something is wrong here. I will be no more. How can I be no more? This is somehow not right... There's some sort of nonsense here. This cannot be. But it will be. They said they will kill, and they will kill. They executed Avdeyenko and Miloshevich, after all. So me too.”

Svetlogub felt such a force of life in himself that he could not imagine death: he could not join the consciousness of his “I” with death, with the absence of “I.”

Svetlogub sat down on the cot again and, closing his eyes, tried to understand, fully understand, what had been announced to him. “Is it possible not to be?” he thought. “Was there ever a time when I was not?.. It's only that in a dream I do not remember what there was in waking life before the dream. I woke up in this world, after all. So I will wake up again... But is that so? Is it so?”

The warden came.

Svetlogub had not heard him come in.

— Did you call? — the warden called out to him.

— Who is it? What is it? — Svetlogub said, not recognizing the warden. — Ah yes, I called you. When will it be? — he asked.

— That does not depend on us, — said the warden. — Is there anything else you need? — he asked, and, receiving no answer, hastily left.

“Yes, most likely. Perhaps in the morning.” He glanced at the paper and the inkwell, at the little table, and sat down to write a letter to his mother.

“My dear, my own,” he wrote, and burst into tears. He found out that he was crying because a tear dropped onto the paper. He remembered how often he had come across details about tears on letters in novels. He wiped away the tear with his sleeve [^6] and went on writing [^7]: “They have announced the sentence to me,” he wrote. The fact that he himself had written about it made him believe that it would happen. And this horrified him. He got up, walked about the cell, and, vividly remembering his mother's grief, sat down to write again.

## \* No. 10 (MS. No. 27, to ch. V).

The very question of what would become of him after death, which he had not been able to answer, now did not exist for him. That the life which was in him now could not be annihilated was beyond doubt for him.

In time it seemed that it had been and would be, but that true life which he was now conscious of in himself was, and is, and will be, and could not but be, and all that was, was it.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 528 --> What awaited him tomorrow meant only that a great, inevitable change, greater than all those there had been before, would soon take place in the form of this life. But life, life itself, cannot change. A change, yes, and the greatest change that a man can experience in this world, but why a bad one? A joyful one, rather. “I should sleep,” he suddenly thought, “so as not to grow weak afterward.” He lay down on the cot, closed his eyes, and at once fell asleep.

At six o'clock he was woken by the sound of bolts being drawn back in the corridor. He woke wholly under the impression of a bright, merry dream, in which, having opened his eyes, he still felt himself to be. He had dreamed that he, with Gapka, the cook's little daughter, was climbing about in some spreading trees strewn with ripe black cherries. The cherries were scattering onto the ground and—an amazing thing—dogs, who were also the girl, were catching them and tossing them up and catching them again, and Gapka, shaking the boughs, went off into peals of laughter, laughed, looking at this. And the shaking of the boughs and Gapka's laughter were one and the same. And looking at these boughs and listening to Gapka's laughter, it was impossible not to laugh. And Svetlogub woke up smiling. And, smiling, he looked at the iron door and listened to the footsteps approaching along the corridor and the clink of rifles.

## \* No. 11 (MS. No. 31, to ch. V).

V

And meanwhile the cart with the youth was driving through the city, arousing a curious horror in those people who saw it.

It was a clear, not hot, southern autumn day; the wind was blowing from the sea.

When Svetlogub was being led out of the prison and led up onto the cart, he saw that people were looking at him from all the windows, and he involuntarily thought of the impression he was making. And, having thought of this, he called up on his face a calm, joyful expression and, raising his head, smiled, looking at the windows.

With that same thought of showing people a manly, calm, joyful appearance, he drove through the city, sitting on the bench of the cart with his back to the driver and holding the Gospel in his hand. [^8]

This mood lasted while they drove out of the prison gates and through the first two streets. But once they had entered a more crowded part of the city, they began to meet people who were evidently hostilely disposed toward him. He saw frowning faces, angry gestures, evidently condemning words that people were saying about him. And he felt an unkind disposition toward these people, vexation with them, and the consciousness of his helplessness.

“They are executing me, they are killing me because I wished them good, and it's all the same to them. They curse me, despise me. Why then did I do all this? Why?”

<!-- vol. 42, p. 529 --> But this weakness did not last long.

“People? What do I care about people, about what they think of me? What matters is what I think of them, what I feel toward them. And I wish them only good. All of them, both this policeman and this blacksmith.”

When the cart drove into Haymarket Square, and Svetlogub was struck by the smell of hay and horse dung that he had loved since childhood, he was vividly carried back to the happy time of childhood, ~~of horses, coachmen~~, of the causeless joy of life. Such a wave of young, vigorous, joyful life surged into his soul that, remembering what awaited him, he suddenly grew weak. [^9]

And Svetlogub returned to the state in which he had been during the night, and again became wholly immersed in himself and in the desire to live with the full force of life to the last minute. And he forgot about himself, and again all the people whom he saw became dear to him.

Dear to him were the masons in aprons, briskly swinging their arms, hurrying to work; dear the cooks, both old and young, going with baskets for provisions; dear too the cabmen, and the yard porters, and all those people who looked at him now with horror, now with contempt, now with insolence, now with bewilderment. He wanted to come into contact with them, and he came into that contact which was possible in his situation, came into contact through the glances he exchanged with some of them. And this gladdened him. Two boys caught up with the cart at a trot and, with their heads turned, not looking ahead of them, strode along the sidewalk. One, the older, in a gray peaked cap, walked with a quick step, trying not to fall behind the cart. The other, a little one, in a pink shirt, without a cap, held on to the older one and, looking fearfully at Svetlogub, kept up with the older one with difficulty on his short little legs.

Svetlogub met his eyes, smiled at him, and nodded to him affectionately. This gesture and smile of the terrible criminal evidently confused the little one completely. He goggled his eyes, opened his mouth, and stood rooted to the spot. Svetlogub nodded to him once more. And the boy smiled. And this gave him great joy.

When the cart drove up to the gallows, Svetlogub was led down from it. Only here did he see the posts with crossbeams. For an instant he felt frightened, and he trembled, but at once he recovered himself and began to pray. He said the “Our Father,” which he still remembered from childhood and had refreshed in his memory by reading in prison. “Thy will be done. Yes, thine,” he thought, “the will of the one who sent me into this world.”

<!-- vol. 42, p. 530 --> Svetlogub did not believe in God and had even often laughed at people who believe in God; now, too, he did not believe in God, did not believe because he could not understand him. But an appeal to someone was necessary to him, and what he meant by this appeal, even if beyond his comprehension, was necessary to him. And he knew that what he meant by this appeal was the truth. The most indubitable truth of all that he knew.

He was led up onto the platform, and after him the priest came up with a cross. He held out the cross to him and began to read some prayer.

— Forgive me, — said Svetlogub. — You believe in this. But I cannot. I don't need it. I feel good as it is. I don't need anything.

The priest began to speak:

— Merciful Lord...

— I thank you, — Svetlogub interrupted him, — I am very grateful to you. — He held out his hand to the priest.

The priest was embarrassed, shook his hand, and then kissed Svetlogub and stepped away.

And at once, his boots noisy on the platform, a young man of peasant appearance, with a curly little beard and a preoccupied air, came up to Svetlogub with quick, light steps. This man, saying nothing, quickly, resolutely, and enveloping him in an unpleasant smell of wine and sweat, seized Svetlogub by the arms above the wrists with sweaty, hot, strong fingers and, squeezing them so that it hurt, bent them behind his back and tied them tightly there with something. Svetlogub wanted to tell him that he should not do what he was doing, that it was bad, but he did not have time. This man threw over his head a sack of hempen cloth that smelled of hemp, and led him off somewhere.

At one and the same time Svetlogub felt the crude violence of this man and understood that this was death. And again, just as at the announcement of the sentence, he knew that the greatest event of his life was being accomplished in him, but he did not have the strength, could not manage in time, to picture to himself the full significance of what was being accomplished.

“Into your hands I commit my spirit”—these words of the Gospel were all he could remember. He understood that a great change was being accomplished in him, which must be and cannot but be, but he could not picture to himself what this change consisted in. [^10] He did not resist death, saw nothing terrible in it, but his strong young body did not accept death, did not submit to it, and struggled with it to the last minute.

At the last minute, when the executioner put the noose on him and pushed him, he cried out, jerked forward, but his hands were tied. A jolt, a roaring in his head, which had filled with blood, the animal horror of suffocation, and suddenly—rapture and illumination.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 531 --> The executioner hung on the corpse so that the strangulation would be complete, and was pleased that he had carried out his duty without unpleasant complications.

An hour later the corpse was taken down from the gallows and carried off to an unconsecrated cemetery.

The governor-general, who prided himself on his early rising, had by this time already finished his coffee and, looking through the German newspapers, was letting out through his overhanging mustaches the fragrant smoke of a foreign cigar—a gift from a rich banker.

“There he is, a representative of the people,” thought Mezhenetsky, leaving the old man. “This is the best of them. And what darkness (he meant Roman and his friends). They say: with the people such as they are now, nothing can be done. So what, were we doing nothing but trifles?” he asked himself, and in the depths of his soul, remembering everything he had carried away from his contact with the people, he felt that they were right.
