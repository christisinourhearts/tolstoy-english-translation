<!-- vol. 41, p. 492 -->

13 July

One cannot cite the existing order in justification of one's actions. The existing order is not something constant; it is subject to constant change, to transition from worse <!-- vol. 41, p. 493 --> to better. And this transition can be accomplished only thanks to our disagreement with the existing arrangement.

1

> As long as the developed minority, consuming the life of generations, barely guessed why it had such an easy life, as long as the majority, working day and night, did not quite guess that all the profit of the work went to others, and both the one and the other considered this the natural order—the world of anthropophagy could hold together. People often take a prejudice, a habit, for truth—and then it does not oppress them. But once they have understood that their truth is nonsense—the matter is finished. Then only by force can a man be made to do what he considers absurd.

> *Herzen.*

2

> All our charitable institutions, all our punitive laws, all our restrictions and prohibitions, by which we try to prevent and suppress crimes—what are they, at best, but the contrivances of a fool who, having loaded the whole burden into the basket on one side of a donkey, decided to help the unfortunate animal by piling as many stones into the basket on its other side?

> *Henry George.*

3

> The loathsome poverty swarming in the very centers of our civilization, and the crimes, depravity, and rapacity it engenders, are the consequence of our land laws, which refuse to know the simple law of justice, a law so simple and clear that even the crudest of savages recognize it. What by its very nature is our birthright has been made the exclusive property of certain persons, and what by natural law ought to be a common fund from which all our public needs would be met, we give over to a few people so that they may lord it over their fellows. And so some gorge themselves, <!-- vol. 41, p. 494 --> while others starve, and more means are squandered than would be needed to keep everyone in luxury.

> *Henry George.*

4

> Wise consumption is far more difficult than wise production. What twenty people produce with difficulty, one can easily consume, and the question of life, both for each individual person and for a whole people, is not how much he produces, but on what these products are spent.

> People usually assert that personal practical activity is powerless to have any influence on changing or holding back the vast system of modern industry or of the methods of production and trade.

> But I, pondering the mass of clever talk that goes in at one long ear of the world and out at the other without making the slightest impression on its mind, sometimes feel an irresistible desire to try to spend all the rest of my life silently doing the work that I consider reasonable, and never to talk about anything again.

> *John Ruskin.*

5

> Should we not strive toward such an ideal of the people's life, in which rising up the steps of the social ladder would not so much captivate as frighten the best people?

> *John Ruskin.*

6

> We have lately studied and improved a great deal the great invention of civilization—the division of labor; only we give it a false name. Properly speaking, one should say: it is not the work that is divided, but the people are divided into particles of people, broken into little pieces, into crumbs; so that the small portion of intellect left in a person is not enough to make a whole pin or a whole nail, and is exhausted in making the point of a pin or the head of a nail. It is true that it is good and desirable to make many <!-- vol. 41, p. 495 --> pins a day; but if only we could see with what sand we polish them—with the sand of the human soul—then we would consider that this is also unprofitable.

> One can put people in chains, torment them, harness them like cattle, kill them like summer flies, and yet such people may in a certain sense, in the very best sense, remain free. But to crush the immortal souls in them, to choke and turn into rotting stumps the infant shoots of their human reason, to use their flesh and skin for belts to drive machines—that is where true slavery lies. Only this degradation and turning of man into a machine makes the workers struggle madly, destructively, and vainly for a freedom whose essence they themselves do not understand. Their bitterness against wealth and against the masters is called forth not by the pressure of hunger, not by the pricks of wounded pride (these two causes have always had their effect; but the foundations of society have never been so shaken as now). The point is not that people are badly fed, but that they find no pleasure in the work by which they earn their bread, and therefore they look upon wealth as the only means of pleasure.

> The point is not that people suffer from the contempt of the upper classes for them, but that they cannot bear their own contempt for themselves, because they feel that the labor to which they are condemned is degrading, corrupts them, makes them something less than men. Never have the upper classes shown so much love and sympathy for the lower as now, and yet never have they been so hated by them.

> *John Ruskin.*

7

> *If the state is governed on the principles of reason, then one should be ashamed if there is poverty and destitution; but if the state is not governed on the principles of reason, then one should be ashamed of wealth and honors.*

> *Chinese wisdom.*

For the realization of the law of God, as far as it has been made clear to us, our effort is needed, and this effort is made by people, and, however slowly, we are nevertheless drawing nearer to this realization.
