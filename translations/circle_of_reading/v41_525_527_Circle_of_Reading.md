<!-- vol. 41, p. 525 -->

24 July

*A man's consciousness of his own law is a manifestation of that God who lives in him.*

1

> The concept of duty in all its purity is not only incomparably simpler, clearer, and more comprehensible for everyone in practice than the same concept derived from the striving for happiness, or bound up with it and taking it into account (and always requiring no little artifice and subtle reasoning), but also, before the court of ordinary common sense, much more powerful, more insistent, and more promising of success than all motives proceeding from self-interest—provided only that the concept of duty has been taken in by common sense entirely independently of self-interested motives.

> The consciousness that I can because I must opens in a man a depth of divine endowments that lets him feel the greatness and sublimity of his true calling. And if a man turned his attention to this more often, and grew used to separating virtue completely from the whole mass of advantages that serve as its reward for the fulfillment of duty, and to picturing it to himself in all its purity; if unceasing exercise in virtue were made the basis of private and <!-- vol. 41, p. 526 --> public instruction (precisely the method of insisting on the fulfillment of obligations, which has almost always been left neglected), then the moral condition of people would soon improve. That historical experience has so far not given good results for the teaching of virtue is the fault of the false assumption that the motive derived from the idea of duty is supposedly too weak and remote, and that a nearer motive acts more strongly on the soul, one that proceeds from reckoning on the advantages to be expected for fulfilling the law partly in this world and also in the world to come. Whereas a man's consciousness of the divine principle within himself impels him to fulfill the law of good more strongly than any external rewards.

> *After Kant.*

2

> Morality is the direction of the will toward common, universal ends. He is immoral who acts for a private end. He is moral—we say this with Marcus Aurelius and Kant—whose end or motive can be set as the end of all rational beings. We affirm that this majestic conception or commandment lies in the consciousness of every person. This is precisely the presence of the eternal in every mortal.

> *Emerson.*

3

> Every person, from the emperor to the beggar, must above all be concerned with perfecting, because only perfecting brings the good to all people.

> *Confucius.*

4

> In the end people attain only what they set themselves as their aim. And therefore one must set as one's aim only the very highest.

> *Thoreau.*

Fulfilling the law of good has nothing in common with material, worldly good. Material good that coincides with <!-- vol. 41, p. 527 --> the fulfillment of the law harms a man's soul. But the highest uplift of the spirit is given by those conditions in which the opposition between moral good and material good produces suffering.
