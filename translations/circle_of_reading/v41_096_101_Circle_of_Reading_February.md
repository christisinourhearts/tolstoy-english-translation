## THE BUDDHA

Two thousand four hundred years ago there lived in India a king, Suddhodana. He had two wives, two sisters by blood, but he had no children by either the one or the other. The king grieved greatly over this, and suddenly, when he had already ceased to hope, his elder wife, Maya, bore a son.

The king could not rejoice enough in his son and spared nothing to gladden him, amuse him, and teach him all kinds of sciences. Siddhartha—so the son was named—was a boy both clever, and handsome, and kind. When Siddhartha had turned 19, his father married him to his cousin and settled the young couple in a magnificent palace, among beautiful gardens and groves. In the palace and gardens of young Siddhartha there was everything that a person can possibly desire.

Wishing to see his beloved son always happy and cheerful, King Suddhodana strictly ordered Siddhartha's attendants and servants not only not to upset him in any way, but also to hide from him everything that might sadden the young heir or lead him to sad thoughts.

Siddhartha did not ride out of his domains, and within his domains he saw nothing spoiled, unclean, or aging. The servants tried to clear away everything that might be unpleasant <!-- vol. 41, p. 97 --> to look at, not only removing everything unclean, but they also cleared away and plucked the withered leaves from the trees and bushes. So that young Siddhartha saw around him only everything young, healthy, beautiful, and cheerful.

Thus Siddhartha lived for more than a year after his marriage. Once, while driving about his gardens, Siddhartha took it into his head to ride out of his domains to see how other people live.

Siddhartha ordered his charioteer Channa to drive him to the town. Everything that he saw: streets, houses, men and women in various clothes, shops, goods, all this was new to Siddhartha and pleasantly occupied and diverted him.

But suddenly in one of the streets he saw such a strange man as he had never seen. The strange man was sitting hunched up by the wall of a house and groaning loudly and piteously. This man's face was pale and wrinkled, and he was trembling all over.

— What is the matter with this man? — Siddhartha asked the charioteer Channa.

— Ill, most likely, — said Channa.

— What does ill mean?

— Ill means that his body has gone out of order.

— And what, does it hurt him?

— It must hurt.

— Why has this happened to him?

— An illness fell on him.

— And can illness fall like that on everyone?

— On everyone.

Siddhartha asked no more.

A little farther on an old beggar came up to Siddhartha's chariot. Decrepit, with a bent back, with watering red eyes, the old man could barely move his dried-up, trembling legs and, mumbling with his toothless mouth, was begging for alms.

— Is this one ill too? — asked Siddhartha.

— No, this is an old man, — said Channa.

— What does old man mean?

— It means: he has grown old.

— What did this come from?

— He lived a long time.

— Do all people grow old? Does this happen to everyone who lives a long time?

— To everyone.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 98 --> — Will it happen to me too, if I live a long time?

— It is the same with everyone, — answered Channa.

— Take me home, — said Siddhartha.

Channa urged on the horses, but on the way out of the town they were held up by people. They were carrying on a stretcher something that looked like a man.

— What is that? — asked Siddhartha.

— That is a dead man, — answered Channa.

— What does dead mean? — asked Siddhartha.

— Dead means that life has ended.

Siddhartha got down from the chariot and went up to the people carrying the dead man. The dead man, with open, fixed, glassy eyes, bared teeth, and stiffened limbs, lay as motionless as only the dead lie.

— Why did this happen to him? — asked Siddhartha.

— Death came. Everyone dies.

— Everyone dies, — repeated Siddhartha, and, getting back into the chariot, without raising his head, he rode home.

All day long Siddhartha sat alone in a far corner of the garden and thought without ceasing about what he had seen.

“All people fall ill, all people grow old, all people die—how can people live, knowing that any hour they may fall ill, that with every hour they are growing old, becoming disfigured and losing strength, and, besides that, they know that any hour they may die, and certainly will die sooner or later. How can one rejoice in anything, do anything, how can one live, knowing for certain that you will die? This must not be, — Siddhartha said to himself. — One must find deliverance from this. And I will find it. And when I find it, I will pass it on to people. But in order to find it, one must leave this palace, where everything distracts my thoughts, leave my wife, my father and mother, and go to the hermits and sages and ask them how they understand all this.”

And, having decided this, Siddhartha on the following night called his charioteer Channa and told him to saddle a horse for him and open the gates. Before leaving home, he went in to his wife. She was asleep. He did not wake her, but, bidding her farewell in his mind, with quiet steps, trying not to wake the sleeping slaves and slave women, he went out of his palace forever and, mounting the horse, rode away alone from his native home.

Having ridden as far as the horse could carry him, he got off it and let it go, and he himself, having exchanged clothes with a <!-- vol. 41, p. 99 --> monk he met and having cut off his hair, went to the Brahmin sage-hermits and asked them to explain to him what he did not understand: what illness, old age, and death are for, and how to be delivered from them. One Brahmin took him in and passed on to him the Brahmin teaching. This teaching was that the human soul migrates from one being into others, that every person was an animal in a former life and after death, depending on his life, will migrate into a higher or a lower being. Siddhartha understood this teaching but did not accept it. He lived with the Brahmins for half a year and went away from them into the dense forests where famous hermit teachers lived, and lived with them for six years in fasting and labors. And he labored and fasted so much that his fame spread among the people, and disciples gathered around him, and people began to praise him. But in the teaching of these hermits too he did not find what he was seeking, and temptation came upon him, and he began to regret what he had left, and wanted to go back to his father and his wife. But he did not go home, but went away from his admirers and disciples and withdrew to a place where no one knew him, and kept thinking about the same thing: how to be saved from illness, old age, and death.

For a long time he was tormented, but once, when he was sitting under a tree and thinking still about the same thing, there was suddenly revealed to him what he had been seeking: the path of salvation from suffering, old age, and death was revealed to him. The path of salvation presented itself to him in four truths.

The first truth was that all people are subject to suffering. The second truth is that the cause of suffering is the passions. The third truth is that in order to be delivered from suffering, one must destroy the passions in oneself. The fourth truth is that in order to destroy the passions, four things are needed.

The first is the awakening of the heart; the second is the purification of thoughts; the third is freeing oneself from ill will and irritability; the fourth is the awakening in oneself of love not only for people but for every living thing.

To mortify one's flesh is superfluous; most needful of all is the cleansing of the soul from bad thoughts. True liberation, however, is only in love. Only a person who has replaced his lustful desires with love breaks the chains of ignorance, of the passions, and is delivered from suffering and death.

When this teaching was revealed to him, Siddhartha left the wilderness, stopped fasting and exhausting his body, and began to go about among the people and preach the truth that had been revealed to him.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 100 --> At first his disciples left him, but later, having understood his teaching, they joined him again. And, in spite of the fact that the Brahmins persecuted Siddhartha-Buddha, his teaching spread more and more.

Siddhartha preached his teaching to the people in *ten commandments:*

The first commandment: do not kill; preserve the life of every living thing.

The second commandment: do not steal, do not rob, do not take from people the products of their labor.

The third commandment: be chaste both in thoughts and in life.

The fourth commandment: do not lie; speak the truth when it is needed, fearlessly, but lovingly.

The fifth commandment: do not speak ill of people and do not repeat the ill that is said of people.

The sixth commandment: do not swear.

The seventh commandment: do not waste time on empty talk, but talk sense or be silent.

The eighth commandment: do not be covetous and do not envy, but rejoice in the good of your neighbor.

The ninth commandment: cleanse your heart of malice, hate no one, but love all.

The tenth commandment: strive to understand the truth.

For sixty years the Buddha, going from place to place, preached his teaching.

In his last years the Buddha was weak, but he still walked about and preached. On one of these journeys he felt the approach of death and, stopping, said: “Thirst torments me.” The disciples gave him water; he drank a little, sat a while, and went on. But near the river Haraneavata he stopped again and, sitting down under a tree, said to his disciples: “My death has come. When I am gone, remember everything that I have told you.” His beloved disciple Ananda, listening to him, could not restrain himself and, stepping aside, wept. Siddhartha at once sent for him and said: “Enough, Ananda! do not weep, do not be troubled. Sooner or later we must part with everything that is dear to us here. Is there anything eternal in this world? My friends, — he added, turning to the other disciples, — live as I have taught you. Free yourselves from the net of passions that entangles people. Go along the path that I have shown you. Remember <!-- vol. 41, p. 101 --> always that everything bodily is destroyed; only truth is indestructible and eternal. Seek salvation in it.”

These were his last words.
