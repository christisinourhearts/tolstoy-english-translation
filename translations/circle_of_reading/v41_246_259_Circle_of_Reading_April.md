## THE STREET VENDOR

Jérôme Crainquebille, a vegetable vendor, wheeled his cart around the city and kept calling out: “Cabbage, carrots, turnips!” And when he had leeks, he shouted: “Fresh asparagus!”—since leeks are the asparagus of the poor. One day, on October 20, at one o'clock in the afternoon, as he was going down Montmartre Street, Madame Bayard, the shoemaker's wife, came out of the shop and went up to the vegetable cart. Contemptuously lifting a bunch of leeks, she said:

— Your leeks aren't much good. How much a bunch?

— Fifteen sous, missus. You won't find better.

— Fifteen sous for such bad leeks?

And she threw the bunch back into the cart with disgust.

Just then a policeman, number 64, came up and said to Crainquebille:

— Move along!

Crainquebille had been going about from morning till evening for fifty whole years. The policeman's order seemed to him lawful and quite in the order of things. Ready to carry it out, he asked the missus to take quickly whatever was to her taste.

— I still have to choose the goods first, — the shoemaker's wife remarked crossly.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 247 --> And she again set about fingering all the bunches of leeks one after another, picked out for herself the one that seemed to her better than the others, and pressed it tightly to her breast.

— I'll give you fourteen sous. That's quite enough. I'll bring them to you from the shop right away; I don't have them on me.

And, taking her leeks with her, she went back into the shop, where a customer with a child in her arms had just come in.

At this minute the policeman, number 64, said to Crainquebille for the second time:

— Move along!

— I'm waiting for the money, — answered Crainquebille.

— I'm not telling you not to wait for the money; I'm only ordering you to move along, — the policeman said sternly.

Meanwhile the shoemaker's wife, in her shop, was trying little blue shoes on a child a year and a half old. The customer was in a great hurry, and the green heads of the leeks lay peacefully on the counter.

Crainquebille, who for fifty whole years had wheeled his cart through the streets of the city, knew how to obey the representatives of authority. But this time he had landed in an exceptional position between right and duty. He knew little about laws and did not understand that the exercise of a personal right did not free him from carrying out his social duties. He had concentrated his attention too much on his right to receive fourteen sous and had not taken seriously enough his duty to wheel his cart and move on ahead, always ahead. He did not move.

For the third time the policeman, number 64, calmly and without any irritation, gave him the order to move along:

— Don't you hear that I am ordering you to move along?

Crainquebille had a reason for staying where he was that was too important in his eyes. And again he set it out simply and artlessly:

— Lord, my God! Why, I'm telling you that I'm waiting for the money.

To this the policeman answered him:

— Perhaps you wish me to hold you responsible for nonobservance of the police regulations? If you wish this, you need only declare it.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 248 --> At these words Crainquebille only slowly shrugged his shoulders, looked dejectedly at the policeman, and raised his gaze to the sky. And this gaze said:

“God sees what an opponent of the laws I am!..”

Perhaps because he did not understand the expression of this gaze, or did not find in it sufficient justification for disobedience, the policeman again, in a sharp and stern tone, asked the vendor whether he had understood him.

Just at this minute there was an extraordinary congestion of vehicles in Montmartre Street. Cabs, droshkies, furniture vans, omnibuses, and carts, pressed one against another, seemed to be inseparably bound together. Shouts and curses came from every side.

The coachmen lazily exchanged strong abuse from a distance with the shop clerks, and the omnibus conductors, considering Crainquebille the cause of the confusion, called him a “nasty leek.”

Meanwhile curious people had gathered on the sidewalk and were listening to the dispute. And the policeman, seeing that he was being watched, now thought only of showing his authority.

— Very well, — he said, and took out of his pocket a dirty little notebook and a very short pencil.

Crainquebille persisted, obeying some inner force. Besides, it was now impossible for him to move either back or forward. The wheel of his cart had, unfortunately, caught on the wheel of the milkman's wagon.

And, tugging at his hair in despair, he exclaimed:

— Why, I'm telling you that I'm waiting for the money! What misfortune is this now! Here's trouble, real trouble! Lord, my God!

The policeman, number 64, considered himself insulted by these words, which, however, expressed despair more than protest. And since every insult was clothed, for him, in the traditional, proper, custom-hallowed and, one might even say, almost ritual form of expression: “Death to the cows!” [^1]— <!-- vol. 41, p. 249 --> it was in precisely this form that he received and realized in his brain the words of the criminal.

— Ah! You said: “Death to the cows!” Very well, follow me.

The vendor, in extreme bewilderment and despair, looked with his wide-open eyes at the policeman, number 64, and, crossing his arms over his blue smock, exclaimed:

— I said: “Death to the cows?” Me?.. Oh!..

This arrest was greeted with laughter by the shop clerks and the street urchins. It answered the passion of every human crowd for low and cruel spectacles. But at this moment an old man dressed all in black and in a tall hat pushed his way through the crowd of gawkers. He went up to the policeman and said to him in a quiet, meek, but very firm voice:

— You are mistaken. This man did not insult you.

— Don't poke your nose into what's not your business, — the policeman answered him, this time not accompanying his words with threats, since he was addressing a well-dressed man.

With great calm and restraint the old man went on insisting. Then the policeman informed him that he would have to give his explanations before the police commissioner.

Meanwhile Crainquebille exclaimed again:

— And I said: “Death to the cows!” O-oh!..

As he was uttering these strange words, Madame Bayard, the shoemaker's wife, came out of the shop to him with the money in her hand. But the policeman was already holding him by the scruff of the neck, and Madame Bayard, considering that it was not worth paying her debt to a man who was being taken to the police, put her fourteen sous back into her apron pocket.

Suddenly understanding that his cart was detained, his personal freedom lost, and that an abyss had opened under his feet and the sun had gone dark, Crainquebille muttered:

— It's all the same!

At the commissioner's, the unknown old man explained that, having been held up in the street by an extraordinary congestion of carriages, he had become a witness of the incident. He asserted that the policeman had by no means been insulted, that he had simply been mistaken. The old man gave his name and title: David Matthieu, chief physician of the Ambroise-Paré hospital, Knight of the Legion of Honor.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 250 --> Crainquebille, whose arrest continued, spent the night at the police station, and in the morning he was taken in the prisoners' cart to the prison.

The prison seemed to him neither humiliating nor hard. It appeared to him, rather, as something necessary. What especially struck him in it was the cleanliness of the walls and the floor.

He said:

— For a place like this it's very clean here. You can say it truly: here you could even eat off the floor.

Left alone, he wanted to move his stool, but saw that it was chained to the wall. The old man loudly expressed his surprise:

— Well, there's a trick! Not for anything would I have thought up anything like it, not for anything!

He sat down and in surprise touched everything around him with his hands. The silence and solitude depressed him. He was bored, and he thought anxiously about his cart, full of cabbage, carrots, celery, and lettuce. He asked himself in anguish: “Where have they put my cart?”

On the third day his lawyer, Mr. Lemerle, one of the youngest members of the court, came to see him.

Crainquebille tried to tell him about his case, which was far from easy for him, since he was not used to having command of words. Perhaps with some help he would have managed it; but his lawyer only shook his head distrustfully at everything the old man said and, leafing through the papers, muttered to himself: “Hm! hm!.. I see none of this in the file...”

Then, with a weary air and twirling his little fair mustache, he said to him:

— It would perhaps be better, in your interests, to confess everything; for my part, I consider your system of complete denial very unfortunate.

Perhaps Crainquebille really would have confessed now, if only he had known what he needed to confess.

Mr. President Bourriche devoted six whole minutes to the examination of Crainquebille. This examination might have shed somewhat more light if the accused had answered the questions put to him. But Crainquebille was not used to arguing, and besides, <!-- vol. 41, p. 251 --> in such company fear and respect shut his mouth. And so he kept silent, and the president gave the answers himself; they confirmed the charge. The president concluded:

— Finally, you admit that you said: “Death to the cows!”

Only now did sounds come from the throat of the accused Crainquebille, resembling the clatter of old iron or the tinkle of broken glass.

— I said: “Death to the cows!” because Mr. Policeman said: “Death to the cows!” Only then did I say: “Death to the cows!” — He wanted to make it understood that, astonished at so unforeseen an accusation, he had in his confusion repeated the terrible words that might be ascribed to him and which he had, of course, not uttered.

Mr. President Bourriche understood him otherwise.

— You declare, — he said, — that the policeman was the first to utter these words?

Crainquebille refused to explain. It was too difficult for him.

— You do not insist, and you have every ground for that, — said the president.

And he ordered the witnesses to be called.

The policeman, number 64, who bore the name Bastien Matra, swore that he would tell the truth and only the truth. Then he set out the following:

— In the discharge of my duty on the twentieth of October, at one o'clock in the afternoon, I noticed in Montmartre Street a man who appeared to me to be a street vendor. His cart was standing unlawfully in one place, at house number three hundred twenty-eight, which gave rise to a congestion of carriages there. Three times I gave him the order to move along, but he refused to comply with it. When, however, I warned him that I would draw up a report, he shouted to me: “Death to the cows!”—which seemed to me very insulting.

This simple and concise explanation was heard by the tribunal with visible goodwill. The defense presented Mrs. Bayard, the shoemaker's wife, and Mr. David Matthieu, chief doctor of the Ambroise-Paré hospital, Knight of the Legion of Honor. Mrs. Bayard had seen nothing and heard nothing. Doctor Matthieu had been in the crowd that had gathered around the policeman <!-- vol. 41, p. 252 --> who made the vendor move along. His testimony gave rise to a curious incident.

— I was a witness of the incident, — he said. — I noticed that the policeman was mistaken: no one had insulted him. I went up and pointed this out to him. But the policeman arrested the vendor all the same and invited me to follow him to the police commissioner, which I did. I have already given my testimony before the commissioner.

— You may sit down, — said the president. — Doorkeeper, call the witness Matra again.

— Matra, when you arrested the accused, did Doctor Matthieu not remark to you that you were mistaken?

— That is, he insulted me, Mr. President.

— What did he say to you, then?

— He said: “Death to the cows!”

Murmuring and laughter ran through the hall.

— You may go, — the president said hastily, and he warned the public that if these improper demonstrations were repeated, he would clear the hall. Meanwhile the defense was triumphant, and at that minute everyone thought that Crainquebille would be acquitted.

When silence had been restored in the hall again, Mr. Lemerle rose. He began his speech for the defense with praise of the police agents, “those modest servants of society who for a paltry wage endure fatigue, are exposed to incessant dangers, and daily perform heroic deeds. They are all former soldiers, and they remain soldiers. Soldiers!.. That word alone says everything...” And Mr. Lemerle set about adducing higher considerations on the subject of the military virtues. According to him, he himself was one of those “who do not allow the army to be touched, this national army, to which he had the honor to belong.”

The president nodded.

Mr. Lemerle was indeed a lieutenant of the militia. He was also the nationalist candidate in the Vieilles-Haudriettes quarter.

The lawyer continued:

— No, of course, I know well those modest and precious services which these guardians of tranquility render daily to the valiant population of Paris. And I would never <!-- vol. 41, p. 253 --> have agreed, gentlemen, to take on the defense of Crainquebille if I had seen in him one who insulted a former soldier. My client is accused of having said: “Death to the cows!” The meaning of this phrase is known to all. If you look into a well-known dictionary, you will read there: “Cow, idler, parasite. Lolls about lazily, like a cow, instead of working. Cow, selling itself to the police: police spy.” “Death to the cows!”—it is said in a certain circle of people. But the whole question is: how did Crainquebille say it? And did he even say it? Allow me, gentlemen, to doubt it. I do not suspect the policeman Matra of any evil intention. But he, as we have already noted, performs an arduous service. He is sometimes tired by it, worn out. Under such conditions he could easily have been the victim of a certain kind of hallucination. And if he tells you, gentlemen, that Doctor David Matthieu, Knight of the Legion of Honor, chief physician of the Ambroise-Paré hospital, a representative of science and a man of society, also shouted at him: “Death to the cows!”—we are compelled to recognize that Matra is the victim of a psychosis and, if the expression does not seem too strong to you, the victim of a delusion of persecution.

— And even if Crainquebille really did shout: “Death to the cows!”—it still remains to be found out whether these words have the character of a crime in his mouth. Crainquebille is the illegitimate son of a woman street vendor who perished from drink and debauchery; he was born an alcoholic. You see him here dulled by sixty years of poverty, and you will say, gentlemen, that he is not responsible.

Mr. Lemerle sat down, and President Bourriche read through his teeth the sentence condemning Jérôme Crainquebille to two weeks' imprisonment and a fine of 50 francs. The tribunal based its opinion on the testimony of the policeman Matra.

When Crainquebille was being led through the long, dark corridors of the courthouse, the old man felt a terrible need for sympathy. He turned to the guard escorting him and called to him three times:

— Soldier!.. Soldier!.. Eh?.. Soldier! — the old man sighed. — If anyone had told me two weeks ago that what has happened would happen to me!..

Then he expressed the following thought:

<!-- vol. 41, p. 254 --> — They talk too fast, these gentlemen. They talk well, only much too fast. There's no coming to an understanding with them... Soldier, how does it seem to you, do they talk fast?

But the soldier marched on without saying a word and without turning his head. Crainquebille asked him:

— Why don't you answer me, then?

The soldier went on keeping silent. The old man remarked to him bitterly:

— Why, people talk even to a dog. Why don't you say anything to me? Maybe you never open your mouth; that means you're afraid of airing it out now and then.

Taken back to prison again, Crainquebille sat down in anxious astonishment on his stool chained to the wall. He did not properly understand that his judges had been mistaken. Under the majesty of forms the tribunal had hidden its weaknesses from him. It was hard for him to believe that he was right, and not these important officials whose reasoning he did not understand. It never even entered his head that anything could be limping in so solemn a rite. Going neither to church nor to the Champs-Élysées, he had never in all his life seen anything more magnificent than the court of correctional police. He knew well that he had not said: “Death to the cows!” But if he had been sentenced to two weeks' imprisonment for these words, then the whole affair appeared in his brain as some majestic mystery, one of those dogmas of faith which devout people agree with without understanding them—some mysterious revelation, majestic and terrible at one and the same time.

This poor old man acknowledged himself guilty of having somehow mystically insulted the policeman, number 64, just as a little boy who is beginning to learn the catechism considers himself guilty of Eve's sin. In putting him in prison, they had told him that he had shouted: “Death to the cows!” Consequently, he really had shouted it, in some mysterious way unknown even to himself. He had been carried into a supernatural world, and his trial seemed to him a kind of apocalypse.

If he could not form for himself a clear idea of his crime, then no clearer was his idea <!-- vol. 41, p. 255 --> of the punishment. His condemnation seemed to him a solemn and majestic rite, a dazzling event which cannot be understood, cannot be disputed, and which ought neither to gladden nor to grieve.

On coming out of prison, Crainquebille went on as before wheeling his cart along Montmartre Street and shouting: “Cabbage, turnips, carrots!” He was not proud of his adventure and was not ashamed of it. Nor was he left with any painful memory of it. In his brain it had the look of a theatrical performance, a journey, a dream. One little old woman, coming up to the cart and choosing celery, asked him:

— What happened to you, Uncle Crainquebille? For three whole weeks we haven't seen you. You weren't sick, were you? You've grown a little pale.

— I've been living like a gentleman all this time, Madame Mailloche, — said the old man.

Nothing had changed in his life, except that on this day he went into the tavern more often than usual, because it kept seeming to him that it was a holiday now and that he would get acquainted with very kind people. He came back to his corner a little tipsy. Stretched out on his mattress and covered, instead of a blanket, with sacks that the chestnut vendor from the corner had lent him, the old man thought: “There's nothing to complain of about prison; there's everything there that a man needs. But all the same it's better in one's own home.”

His well-being did not last long. Soon he noticed that his customers looked at him sourly.

— Fine celery, Madame Cointreau!

— I don't need anything.

— What do you mean, you don't need anything? You don't feed on air, after all!

But Madame Cointreau, without answering him a word, went proudly back into her big bakery. The women shopkeepers and concierges who so recently had been waiting impatiently for his cart heaped with greens and flowers now turned away from him. Driving up to the shoemaker's shop where all his adventures had begun, he shouted:

— Madame Bayard, Madame Bayard, you still owe me fifteen sous.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 256 --> But Madame Bayard, sitting at her counter, did not even deign to turn her head.

All of Montmartre Street knew that Crainquebille had come out of prison, and nobody wanted to know him any more. The rumor of his imprisonment had reached both the suburb and the noisy corner of Richer Street. There, around noon, he caught sight of Madame Laure, his good and faithful customer. She was bending over little Martin's cart and feeling a big head of cabbage.

At the sight of this Crainquebille's heart tightened. He pushed little Martin's wagon with his cart and said to Madame Laure in a plaintive tone:

— It's not good of you to be unfaithful to me.

Madame Laure did not answer Crainquebille a word, playing the insulted woman.

And the old street vendor, feeling wronged, yelled at the top of his voice:

— Ah, you whore!

Madame Laure dropped her cabbage and shouted:

— Clear off, you old scoundrel! Look at them: they come out of prison and insult people on top of it!

Crainquebille, in a calm state, would never have reproached Madame Laure for her behavior. But this time the old man lost his temper. Three times he called Madame Laure a whore, a good-for-nothing, and a bitch. And this scene lowered Crainquebille once and for all in the eyes of the whole Montmartre suburb and Richer Street.

The old man went off, grumbling to himself:

— Such a whore! You won't meet another whore like her.

The worst of it was that she was not the only one who treated him like some outcast. Nobody wanted to know him any more.

And his character began to go bad. Having quarreled with Madame Laure, he now began to squabble with everyone. Over every trifle he said rude things to his regular customers, and if they took a long time choosing the goods, he called them chatterboxes and lazybones to their faces; in the tavern, too, he was constantly swearing at his companions. His friend the chestnut vendor simply did not recognize him and declared that Uncle Crainquebille had become a real porcupine. There was no denying it: he had become a cantankerous, quarrelsome man, coarse and insolent of tongue. As he was in uneducated society, it was, of course, harder for him than for some professor of social sciences at a university to express <!-- vol. 41, p. 257 --> his thoughts on the imperfection of the present order and on the changes needed in it, and besides, the thoughts themselves settled badly and in disorder in his head.

Misfortune had made him unjust, and he now took revenge on those who did not wish him any harm at all, or who were sometimes even weaker than he. Thus once he painfully struck Alphonse, the tavern keeper's little son, because the boy asked him whether it had been nice in prison.

— Ah, you nasty brat! — he shouted at him. — It's your father who ought to be sitting in prison, not making profits for himself by selling poison.

At last his spirits sank completely. In such a state a man can no longer rise. All the passersby push him with their feet.

Poverty came, the blackest poverty. The old street vendor, who had once carried away from the Montmartre suburb pockets full of five-franc pieces, now did not have a single sou. It was winter. Turned out of his corner, he now slept in a shed, under the carts. After almost a whole month of rain the drains overflowed and flooded the shed.

Squatting in his cart above the stinking water, in the company of rats, spiders, and hungry cats, the old man reflected in the darkness. Not having eaten all day and now not even having sacks to cover himself with, he remembered the days when the government had given him shelter and food. He envied the lot of the prisoners, who suffer neither from hunger nor from cold, and suddenly a thought came into his head:

“Why, I know the way now; why shouldn't I make use of it?” He got up and went out into the street. It was no later than eleven o'clock at night. The weather was dark and damp. Some sort of drizzle was falling, colder and more piercing than any rain. The few passersby huddled against the walls.

Crainquebille walked past the church of St. Eustache and turned into Montmartre Street. It was completely empty. A guardian of order was standing on the sidewalk at the entrance to the church, under a gas jet; around the light one could see the fine rain falling. The policeman was covered by his hood and looked completely frozen stiff. But whether because he preferred light to darkness, or simply because he was tired of walking, he stood motionless under his candelabrum, <!-- vol. 41, p. 258 --> as if beside a close friend. This trembling little light was his only interlocutor in the dark, deserted night. His immobility seemed almost inhuman; the reflection of his boots on the wet sidewalk, which had turned into a lake, lengthened his figure downward and gave him from a distance the look of a gigantic amphibian half emerged from the water. Close up, the policeman in his hood looked like a monk and like a military man. The large features of his face, which seemed larger still in the shadow of the hood, were calm and sad. He had a short, thick mustache, already gone gray. He was an old sergeant of over forty.

Crainquebille quietly went up to him and said in a trembling, weak voice:

— Death to the cows!

Then he began to wait for the effect of these sacred words. But no effect followed. The policeman stood silent and motionless, his arms crossed under his wide cloak. His wide-open eyes, shining in the darkness, looked at the old man attentively, sadly, and with a certain contempt. Crainquebille, surprised but still keeping a remnant of resolve, muttered:

— Why, I told you: death to the cows!

A long silence followed, during which only the fine rain kept falling and deep darkness reigned. At last the policeman said:

— That should not be said... I seriously advise you not to say that. At your age you ought to be a little more experienced... Go on your way.

— Then why don't you arrest me? — asked Crainquebille.

The policeman shook his head under his wet hood:

— If one were to seize all the rude fellows who say what shouldn't be said, there would be too much work!.. And what purpose would it serve?

Crainquebille, crushed by this magnanimous contempt, stood silent for a long time in bewilderment in the middle of a large puddle. But before going away, he tried to explain himself.

— It wasn't for you that I said: “Death to the cows!” And not for anyone else either. I said it for one definite purpose.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 259 --> The policeman answered him with stern calm:

— For some purpose or for something else, but it ought not to have been said at all, because when a man is doing his duty and endures no little suffering in doing it, he should not be insulted with empty words... I tell you again to go on your way.

And Crainquebille, his head lowered and his arms swinging, disappeared in the rain into the darkness of the night.

*Anatole France.*

## Notes

[^1]: In the thieves' slang of Paris, policemen are called “cows.” This expression is considered very insulting to them. *Translator's note.*
