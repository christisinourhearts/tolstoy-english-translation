<!-- vol. 41, p. 27 -->

9 January

Knowledge is knowledge only when it has been acquired by the efforts of one's own thought, and not by memory.

1

> Only when we completely forget what we have learned do we begin truly to know. I will not come a hair's breadth nearer to knowing a subject so long as I suppose that my relation to it has been established by a learned person. To know a subject, I must approach it as something completely unfamiliar.

> *Thoreau.*

2

> The continuous influx of other people's thoughts must hold back and stifle one's own, and over a long period of time even completely weaken the power of thought, unless it possesses a high degree of resilience with which to resist this unnatural influx. This is why constant reading and study disorder the head; and also because the system of our own thoughts and knowledge loses its wholeness and continuous connection if we so often arbitrarily interrupt it in order to make room for a completely foreign train of thought. To drive away one's own thoughts in order to make room for thoughts from books is, in my opinion, the same as selling one's own land in order to see other people's lands—which Shakespeare reproached the tourists of his time for doing.
>
> It is harmful even to read about a subject before one has thought about it oneself. For together with the new material, another person's point of view on it and another person's relation to it creep into the head; and this is all the more likely because it is natural for a person, out of laziness and indifference, to try to rid himself of the effort of thinking, to accept ready-made thoughts, and to set them in motion. This habit then takes root, and thoughts thereafter follow the usual road, like streams diverted into ditches: to find one's own new thought then becomes twice as difficult. This is why independence of thought is so rarely encountered among scholars.

> *Schopenhauer.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 28 -->

3

> Knowledge is like current coin. A person has some right to be proud of possessing it if he himself worked on its gold and tried to mint it, or at least honestly acquired it already tested. But when he did none of this and received it from some passerby who threw it in his face, what grounds does he have for being proud of it?

> *John Ruskin.*

4

*For the human mind it is less harmful not to study at all than to study too early and too much.*

5

> The merit of the greatest thinkers consists precisely in this: independently of the books and traditions that existed before them, they expressed what they themselves thought, and not what either people who lived before them or the people around them thought.
>
> In exactly the same way, each of us must lie in wait for and catch those bright thoughts which, like sparks, from time to time flash and flare up in our consciousness. For each of us, such inner illuminations have much more significance than the contemplation and study of a whole constellation of poets and sages.

> *Emerson.*

6

A thought moves life only when it has been obtained by one's own mind, or at least when it answers a question that has already arisen in the soul. A foreign thought, received by the mind and memory, does not influence life and coexists with actions contrary to it.

Read less, study less, think more. Learn both from teachers and from books only what you need and want to know.
