> THE DARLING

> Olenka, the daughter of the retired collegiate assessor Plemyannikov, was sitting on the little porch in her yard, lost in thought. It was hot, the flies were pestering persistently, and it was so pleasant to think that it would soon be evening. Dark rain clouds were moving in from the east, and from there now and then came a breath of moisture.

> In the middle of the yard stood Kukin, an impresario and the proprietor of the “Tivoli” pleasure garden, who lodged right there in the yard, in the outbuilding, and he was looking at the sky.

> — Again! — he said in despair. — There's going to be rain again! Rain every day, rain every day—as if on purpose! Why, it's a noose! It's ruin! Terrible losses every day!

> He threw up his hands and went on, turning to Olenka:

> — There you have it, Olga Semyonovna, our life. It's enough to make you weep! You work, you try your best, you torment yourself, you don't sleep nights, you keep thinking how to do things better—and what comes of it? On the one hand, the public is ignorant, savage. I give it the very best operetta, an extravaganza, magnificent couplet singers, but is that what it needs? Does it understand anything about that? It needs a fairground show! It wants vulgarity served up! On the other hand, look at the weather. Rain almost every evening. Once it set in on the tenth of May, it went on all May and June, simply awful! The public doesn't come, but I pay the rent, don't I? I pay the artists, don't I?

> The next day toward evening the clouds were moving in again, and Kukin said with hysterical laughter:

> — Well, what of it? Let it! Let it flood the whole garden, and me too! May I have no happiness in this world or the next! Let the artists sue me! What's a court? Hard labor in Siberia, if you like! The scaffold, if you like! Ha-ha-ha!

> And on the third day the same...

> Olenka listened to Kukin silently, seriously, and at times tears came into her eyes. In the end Kukin's misfortunes touched her, and she came to love him. He was short, scrawny, with a yellow face, with the hair at his temples combed down, he spoke in a thin little tenor, and despair was always written on his face, but all the same he aroused a real, deep feeling in her. She was always loving somebody and could not do without it. Earlier <!-- vol. 41, p. 364 --> she had loved her papa, who now sat sick, in a dark room, in an armchair, and breathed heavily; she had loved her aunt, who sometimes, once every two years, came from Bryansk; and still earlier, when she was at the progymnasium, she had loved her French teacher. She was a quiet, good-natured, compassionate young lady with a meek, soft gaze, very healthy. Looking at her full, pink cheeks, at her soft white neck with a dark mole, at the kind, naive smile that came over her face when she listened to something pleasant, men smiled too, and the lady visitors could not help suddenly seizing her hand in the middle of a conversation and saying in a rush of pleasure:

> — Darling!

> The house in which she had lived since the day she was born, and which was put down in her name in the will, stood at the edge of town, in the Gypsy Settlement, not far from the “Tivoli” garden; in the evenings and at night she could hear the music playing in the garden and the rockets bursting with a crackle, and it seemed to her that this was Kukin making war on his fate and taking by storm his chief enemy—the indifferent public; her heart would sweetly stand still, she did not feel like sleeping at all, and when he came home toward morning, she would tap softly on the little window of her bedroom and, showing him only her face and one shoulder through the curtains, smile tenderly...

> He proposed, and they were married.

> He was happy, but since it rained on the wedding day and then in the night, the expression of despair did not leave his face.

> After the wedding they lived well. She sat in his box office, looked after the order of things in the garden, wrote down the expenses, paid out the wages, and her pink cheeks, her sweet, naive smile, like a radiance, flashed now in the box-office window, now in the wings, now at the buffet. And she was already telling her acquaintances that the most remarkable, the most important and necessary thing in the world was the theater, and that only in the theater could one get true enjoyment and become educated and humane.

> — But does the public understand that? — she said. — It needs a fairground show! Yesterday we gave “Faust Inside Out,” and almost all the boxes were empty, but if Vanechka and I had put on some vulgarity, then, believe me, the theater would have been packed full. Tomorrow Vanechka and I are putting on “Orpheus in Hell”; do come.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 365 --> And whatever Kukin said about the theater and about the actors, she repeated too. Like him, she despised the public for its indifference to art and for its ignorance, at rehearsals she interfered, corrected the actors, kept watch on the musicians' behavior, and when the local newspaper spoke disapprovingly of the theater, she wept and then went to the editorial office to have it out.

> The actors loved her and called her “Vanechka and I” and “darling”; she felt sorry for them and lent them small sums, and if it happened that they cheated her, she only wept quietly, but did not complain to her husband.

> And in the winter they lived well. They took the town theater for the whole winter and let it out for short terms now to a Little Russian troupe, now to a conjurer, now to local amateurs. Olenka grew plumper and was all beaming with pleasure, while Kukin grew thinner and yellower and complained of terrible losses, although all winter business went not badly. At night he coughed, and she gave him raspberry and linden blossom to drink, rubbed him with eau de cologne, wrapped him in her soft shawls.

> — What a nice little fellow you are, my own! — she said quite sincerely, smoothing his hair. — What a pretty one you are, my own!

> During Great Lent he went off to Moscow to get together a troupe, and without him she could not sleep, but kept sitting by the window and looking at the stars. And at this time she compared herself to the hens, which also do not sleep all night and feel uneasy when there is no rooster in the henhouse. Kukin was delayed in Moscow and wrote that he would be back by Easter Week, and in his letters was already giving instructions about the “Tivoli.” But on the eve of Holy Monday, late in the evening, there suddenly came an ominous knocking at the gates; someone was beating on the wicket gate as on a barrel: boom! boom! boom! The sleepy cook, slapping her bare feet through the puddles, ran to open up.

> — Open up, be so kind! — someone was saying beyond the gates in a hollow bass. — A telegram for you!

> Olenka had received telegrams from her husband before, but this time for some reason she was simply stunned. With trembling hands she unsealed the telegram and read the following:

> “Ivan Petrovich passed away suddenly today syuchala await instructions huneral Tuesday.”

> That is just how it was printed in the telegram: “huneral,” and some other incomprehensible word, “syuchala”; the signature was that of the director of the operetta troupe.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 366 --> — My dear! — Olenka burst out sobbing. — My sweet little Vanechka, my dear! Why ever did I meet you? Why did I come to know you and love you? To whom have you left your poor Olenka, poor and unhappy?..

> Kukin was buried on Tuesday, in Moscow, at Vagankovo; Olenka came home on Wednesday, and as soon as she went into her rooms, she fell onto the bed and sobbed so loudly that it could be heard in the street and in the neighboring yards.

> — Darling! — the neighbor women said, crossing themselves. — Darling Olga Semyonovna, dear soul, how she's killing herself with grief!

> Three months later Olenka was coming home from mass one day, sad, in deep mourning. It happened that walking beside her, also coming back from church, was one of her neighbors, Vasily Andreich Pustovalov, the manager of the merchant Babakaev's timber yard. He wore a straw hat and a white vest with a gold chain and looked more like a landowner than a tradesman.

> — Everything has its order, Olga Semyonovna, — he said sedately, with sympathy in his voice, — and if one of those near to us dies, then it means that this is pleasing to God, and in that case we must keep ourselves in hand and bear it with submission.

> Having seen Olenka to the wicket gate, he said goodbye and went on. After that all day long she heard his sedate voice, and as soon as she closed her eyes, she seemed to see his dark beard. She liked him very much. And apparently she had made an impression on him too, because a little later an elderly lady whom she knew only slightly came to have coffee with her, and as soon as she sat down at the table, began without delay to talk about Pustovalov, about how he was a good, solid man and any bride would be glad to marry him. Three days later Pustovalov himself came to call; he did not stay long, about ten minutes, and said little, but Olenka came to love him, loved him so that she did not sleep all night and burned as if in a fever, and in the morning she sent for the elderly lady. Soon she was betrothed, then there was the wedding.

> Pustovalov and Olenka, having married, lived well. Usually he sat in the timber yard until dinner, then went out on business, and Olenka took his place, sitting in the office until evening, making out the bills there and dispatching the goods.

> — Now timber gets twenty percent dearer every year, — she said to customers and acquaintances. — For pity's sake, we used to trade in local timber, but now Vasechka <!-- vol. 41, p. 367 --> has to go for timber to Mogilev province every year. And what a tariff! — she said, covering both her cheeks with her hands in horror. — What a tariff!

> It seemed to her that she had been dealing in timber for a long, long time, that the most important and necessary thing in life was timber, and something dear and touching sounded to her in the words: beam, roundwood, planking, sheathing, odd-board, lathing, cant, slab...

> Whatever thoughts her husband had, she had too. If he thought that the room was hot or that business had gotten slack now, then she thought so too. Her husband did not like any amusements and on holidays stayed at home, and so did she.

> — And you're always at home or in the office, — acquaintances said. — You ought to go to the theater, darling, or to the circus.

> — Vasechka and I have no time to go to theaters, — she answered sedately. — We are working people, we have no time for trifles. What's good about these theaters?

> On Saturdays Pustovalov and she went to the all-night vigil, on holidays—to early mass, and, coming back from church, they walked side by side, with tender faces, they both smelled nice, and her silk dress rustled pleasantly; and at home they drank tea with rich bread and various jams, then ate pie. Every day at noon in the yard and beyond the gates in the street there was a delicious smell of borscht and roast mutton or duck, and on fast days—of fish, and one could not pass the gates without wanting to eat. In the office the samovar was always boiling, and customers were treated to tea with bagels. Once a week the couple went to the bathhouse and came back from there side by side, both red.

> — We're all right, we live well, — Olenka said to acquaintances, — thank God! God grant everyone may live the way Vasechka and I do.

> When Pustovalov went to Mogilev province for timber, she missed him badly and did not sleep at night, she cried. Sometimes in the evenings the regimental veterinarian Smirnin came to see her, a young man who lodged in her outbuilding. He would tell her something or play cards with her, and this diverted her. Especially interesting were the stories from his own family life; he was married and had a son, but had separated from his wife, since she had been unfaithful to him, and now he hated her and sent her forty rubles every month for the son's keep. And, listening to this, Olenka sighed and shook her head, and she was sorry for him.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 368 --> — Well, Lord save you, — she said, taking leave of him and lighting him with a candle to the stairs, — thank you for sharing my boredom, God give you health, Queen of Heaven...

> And she always expressed herself so sedately, so reasonably, imitating her husband; the veterinarian was already disappearing below behind the door, and she would call after him and say:

> — You know, Vladimir Platonych, you should make it up with your wife. You should forgive her, if only for your son's sake!.. The little fellow, no doubt, understands everything.

> And when Pustovalov came back, she told him in an undertone about the veterinarian and his unhappy family life, and they both sighed and shook their heads and talked about the boy, who was probably missing his father, then, by some strange train of thought, they both stood before the icons, bowed down to the ground, and prayed that God would send them children.

> And so the Pustovalovs lived quietly and peaceably, in love and complete harmony, for six years. But then one winter Vasily Andreich, at the timber yard, after drinking hot tea, went out without his cap to dispatch timber, caught a chill, and fell ill. He was treated by the best doctors, but the illness had its way, and he died after being ill for four months. And Olenka was widowed again.

> — To whom have you left me, my dear? — she sobbed, having buried her husband. — How am I to live now without you, bitter and unhappy as I am! Good people, pity me, a complete orphan...

> She went about in a black dress with weepers and had already given up hats and gloves forever, went out of the house rarely, only to church or to her husband's grave, and lived at home like a nun. And only when six months had passed did she take off the weepers and begin to open the shutters on the windows. Sometimes she could already be seen in the mornings going to market for provisions with her cook, but how she lived at home now and what went on in her house could only be guessed. People guessed, for example, from having seen her drinking tea in her little garden with the veterinarian while he read the newspaper aloud to her, and also from the fact that, meeting a lady she knew at the post office, she said:

> — We have no proper veterinary inspection in our town, and that is why there is so much disease. Every so often you hear that people fall ill from milk and catch infections from horses and cows. In essence, one must take care of the health of domestic <!-- vol. 41, p. 369 --> animals just as of the health of people.

> She repeated the veterinarian's thoughts and now was of the same opinion as he about everything. It was clear that she could not live without an attachment even for one year and had found her new happiness in her own outbuilding. Another woman would have been condemned for this, but no one could think ill of Olenka, and everything in her life was so understandable. She and the veterinarian told no one of the change that had taken place in their relations and tried to hide it, but they did not succeed, because Olenka could not have secrets. When guests came to see him, his comrades from the regiment, she, pouring them tea or serving supper, would begin to talk about the cattle plague, about the pearl disease, about the town slaughterhouses, and he would be terribly embarrassed and, when the guests had gone, would seize her by the hand and hiss angrily:

> — I did ask you not to talk about what you don't understand! When we veterinarians are talking among ourselves, please don't interfere. It's boring, after all!

> And she looked at him with amazement and alarm and asked:

> — Volodechka, what am I to talk about, then?!

> And with tears in her eyes she embraced him, begged him not to be angry, and both were happy.

> But, however, this happiness did not last long. The veterinarian went away with the regiment, went away for good, since the regiment had been transferred somewhere very far away, practically to Siberia. And Olenka was left alone.

> Now she was completely alone. Her father had died long ago, and his armchair lay about in the attic, dusty, missing one leg. She had grown thin and lost her looks, and in the street the people she met no longer looked at her as before and did not smile at her; evidently the best years were already over, left behind, and now some new life was beginning, an unknown one, which it was better not to think about. In the evenings Olenka sat on the little porch, and she could hear the music playing in the “Tivoli” and the rockets bursting, but this no longer called up any thoughts. She looked indifferently at her empty yard, thought about nothing, wanted nothing; she ate and drank as if against her will.

> And, the main thing, what was worst of all, she no longer had any opinions. She saw the objects around her and understood everything that <!-- vol. 41, p. 370 --> was going on around her, but could not form an opinion about anything and did not know what to talk about. She sees, for example, a bottle standing, or the rain falling, or a peasant driving by in a cart, but what the bottle is for, or the rain, or the peasant, what sense there is in them, she cannot say. In Kukin's and Pustovalov's time, and then in the veterinarian's, Olenka could explain everything and would have given her opinion about anything you like, but now both in her thoughts and in her heart there was the same emptiness as in the yard.

> The town was little by little spreading out in all directions; the Gypsy Settlement was already called a street, and where the “Tivoli” garden and the timber yards had been, houses had already grown up and a row of lanes had formed. How fast time runs! Olenka's house had darkened, the roof had rusted, the shed had sagged, and the whole yard was overgrown with tall weeds and stinging nettles. Olenka herself had aged and lost her looks; in summer she sits on the little porch, and in winter she sits by the window and looks at the snow. If there comes a breath of spring, if the wind carries the ringing of the cathedral bells, suddenly memories of the past come flooding in, the heart sweetly contracts and abundant tears pour from the eyes, but this is only for a minute, and then there is emptiness again, and one does not know what one lives for. The little black cat Bryska fawns on her and purrs softly, but these cat caresses do not touch Olenka. Is that what she needs? She needs such a love as would seize her whole being, her whole soul, her reason, would give her thoughts, a direction in life, would warm her aging blood. And she shakes black Bryska off her skirt and says to her with vexation:

> — Go on, go on... Nothing for you here!

> And so day after day, year after year—and not a single joy, and no opinion at all. Whatever Mavra the cook said, that is fine.

> One hot July day, toward evening, when the town herd was being driven along the street and the whole yard was filled with clouds of dust, suddenly someone knocked at the wicket gate. Olenka went to open it herself and, the moment she looked, was stunned: outside the gates stood the veterinarian Smirnin, already gray and in civilian clothes. Suddenly she remembered everything, she could not hold back, she burst into tears and laid her head on his chest without saying a single word, and in her great agitation did not notice how they both then went into the house, how they sat down to drink tea.

> — My dear! — she murmured, trembling with joy. — Vladimir Platonych! Where has God brought you from?

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 371 --> — I want to settle here for good, — he told her. — I've sent in my resignation and come to try my luck at liberty, to live a settled life. And besides, it's time to send my son to the gymnasium. He's grown. And I, you know, have made it up with my wife.

> — And where is she? — asked Olenka.

> — She's at the hotel with my son, and here I am going about looking for lodgings.

> — Lord, my dear sir, why, take my house! What's wrong with it for lodgings? Oh, Lord, and I won't take anything from you, — Olenka grew agitated and began to cry again. — Live here, and the outbuilding is enough for me. Oh, the joy of it, Lord!

> The next day they were already painting the roof on the house and whitewashing the walls, and Olenka, arms akimbo, walked about the yard giving orders. Her old smile lit up her face, and she came alive all over, grew fresh, as if she had awakened from a long sleep. The veterinarian's wife arrived, a thin, plain lady with short hair and a capricious expression, and with her the boy, Sasha, small for his age (he was already in his tenth year), plump, with clear blue eyes and dimples in his cheeks. And no sooner had the boy come into the yard than he ran after the cat, and at once his merry, joyful laughter rang out.

> — Auntie, is this your cat? — he asked Olenka. — When she has puppies, please give us one little kitten. Mama is very afraid of mice.

> Olenka talked with him, gave him tea, and the heart in her breast suddenly grew warm and sweetly contracted, as if this boy were her own son. And when in the evening he sat in the dining room going over his lessons, she looked at him with tenderness and with pity and whispered:

> — My dear, my pretty one... My little child, and you were born so clever, so fair!

> — What is called an island, — he read, — is a part of dry land surrounded on all sides by water.

> — What is called an island is a part of dry land... — she repeated, and this was the first opinion she had uttered with conviction after so many years of silence and emptiness in her thoughts.

> And now she had opinions of her own, and at supper she talked with Sasha's parents about how hard it is now for children to study at the gymnasiums, but that all the same a classical education is better than a modern one, since from the gymnasium the road is open everywhere: if you like—become a doctor, if you like—an engineer.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 372 --> Sasha began going to the gymnasium. His mother went away to Kharkov to her sister's and did not come back; his father went off somewhere every day to inspect droves of cattle and sometimes was not at home for three days at a time, and it seemed to Olenka that Sasha was utterly neglected, that he was superfluous in the house, that he was dying of hunger; and she moved him over to her outbuilding and settled him there in a little room.

> And now it is already half a year that Sasha has been living with her in the outbuilding. Every morning Olenka goes into his room; he is fast asleep, with his hand under his cheek, not breathing. She is sorry to wake him.

> — Sashenka, — she says sadly, — get up, my dear! It's time for the gymnasium.

> He gets up, dresses, prays to God, then sits down to drink tea; he drinks three glasses of tea and eats two large bagels and half a French loaf with butter. He has not yet quite woken up and is therefore out of sorts.

> — And you, Sashenka, haven't learned your fable thoroughly, — says Olenka, and she looks at him as if she were seeing him off on a long journey. — You're such a worry to me. Now do try, my dear, study... Mind your teachers.

> — Oh, leave off, please! — says Sasha.

> Then he walks down the street to the gymnasium, small himself, but in a big cap, with a satchel on his back. Behind him, noiselessly, walks Olenka.

> — Sashenka-a! — she calls after him.

> He looks around, and she thrusts a date or a caramel into his hand. When they turn into the lane where the gymnasium stands, he feels ashamed that a tall, stout woman is walking behind him; he looks around and says:

> — You go home, Aunt, and now I'll get there by myself.

> She stops and looks after him without blinking until he disappears into the entrance of the gymnasium. Ah, how she loves him! Of her former attachments not one was so deep, never before had her soul surrendered so unreservedly, so disinterestedly, and with such gladness as now, when the maternal feeling in her was kindling more and more. For this boy, a stranger to her, for his dimples, for his cap, she would have given her whole life, would have given it with joy, with tears of tender emotion. Why? But who knows—why?

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 373 --> Having seen Sasha off to the gymnasium, she comes back home quietly, so contented, serene, full of love; her face, which has grown younger in the last half year, smiles, shines; the people she meets, looking at her, feel pleasure and say to her:

> — Hello, darling Olga Semyonovna! How are you getting on, darling?

> — It's become hard to study at the gymnasium now, — she says at the market. — It's no joke, yesterday in the first class they were set a fable to learn by heart, and a Latin translation, and a problem... Well, how's a little one to manage?

> And she begins to talk about the teachers, about the lessons, about the textbooks—the same things that Sasha says about them.

> Between two and three o'clock they have dinner together, in the evening they do the lessons together and cry. Putting him to bed, she makes the sign of the cross over him for a long time and whispers a prayer, then, lying down to sleep, she dreams of that future, distant and misty, when Sasha, having finished his studies, will become a doctor or an engineer, will have a big house of his own, horses, a carriage, will marry, and children will be born to him... She falls asleep and keeps thinking about the same thing, and tears run down her cheeks from her closed eyes. And the little black cat lies at her side and purrs:

> — Purr...purr...purr...

> Suddenly a loud knocking at the wicket gate. Olenka wakes up and does not breathe for fear; her heart is pounding. Half a minute passes, and again a knocking.

> “It's a telegram from Kharkov,” she thinks, beginning to tremble all over. “His mother is demanding Sasha back to her in Kharkov... Oh, Lord!”

> She is in despair; her head, her feet, her hands go cold, and it seems to her that there is no person in the whole world more unhappy than she. But another minute passes, voices are heard: it is the veterinarian come home from the club.

> “Well, thank God!” she thinks.

> Little by little the weight lifts from her heart, again it becomes easy; she lies down and thinks of Sasha, who is sleeping soundly in the next room and now and then says in his sleep:

> — I'll t-teach you! Get out! Don't fight!

> *Anton Chekhov.*
