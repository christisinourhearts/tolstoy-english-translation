<!-- vol. 41, p. 101 -->

12 February

*There is nothing more certain than that death awaits each of us, and yet we all live as if it will never come.*

1

> Whether our life ends with death, this is a question of the very greatest importance, and one cannot help thinking about it. Depending on whether we believe in immortality or not, our actions too will be reasonable or senseless.

> Therefore our chief concern must be to decide the question: do we die entirely or not entirely in fleshly death, and, if not entirely, then what exactly in us is immortal. And when we understand that there is in us that which is mortal and that which is immortal, then it is clear that in this life too we must care more for what is immortal than for what is mortal. People, however, usually act exactly the reverse.

> *After Pascal.*

2

> It is a terrible world if the sufferings in it do not produce good. It is some kind of evil contrivance, made in order to torment people spiritually and bodily. If this is so, then the world is unspeakably immoral, since it does evil not for the sake of future good, but idly, aimlessly. It is as if it deliberately lures people in only so that they may suffer. It strikes us from birth, mixes bitterness into every cup of happiness, and makes death an ever-threatening horror. And, of course, if there is no God and immortality, then the aversion to life expressed by people is understandable: it is called forth in them by the existing order or, rather, disorder—a terrible moral chaos, as it ought to be called.

> Which of the two suppositions is more probable? Can one really allow that moral beings—people—should be placed under the necessity of justly cursing the existing order of the world, when before them there is a way out that resolves their contradiction. They must curse the world and the day of their birth if there is no God and future life. If, on the contrary, there are both the one and the other, life in itself becomes a good, and the world a place of moral perfecting and of infinite increase of happiness and holiness.

> *Erasmus.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 102 --> But if only there is a God above us and eternity before us, then everything changes. We discern good in evil, light in darkness, and hope drives away despair.

3

*The more deeply you are conscious of your life, the less you believe in annihilation in death.*

4

We often try to imagine death and the passage *there,* but this is completely impossible, just as it is impossible to imagine God. All that is possible is to believe that death is—like everything that proceeds from God—good.

5

> Whatever that principle in man may be which feels, understands, lives, and exists, it is holy, divine, and therefore must be eternal.

> *Cicero.*

Only he who has never seriously thought about death does not believe in immortality.
