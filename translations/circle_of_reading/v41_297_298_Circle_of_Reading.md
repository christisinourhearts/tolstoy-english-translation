<!-- vol. 41, p. 297 -->

2 May

People most often do not agree with the truth because they feel offended by the form in which the truth is offered to them.

1

> A quarrel that is beginning is like a stream breaking through a dam: once it has broken through, you will no longer hold it back.

> *The Talmud.*

2

A person can easily start a dispute, but, like a fire that has blazed up, it is hard to put out.

3

*As soon as we have felt anger in a dispute, we are no longer disputing for the truth but for ourselves.*

*Carlyle.*

4

> I can never convince another person except by his own thoughts. That means I must assume that he has a good and sound intellect; otherwise it is useless to hope that I can draw him over to my side by my arguments. In just the same way I cannot touch the heart of another except by his own feelings. That means I must assume that the other person possesses a certain goodness of heart; otherwise he will never feel aversion to vice and an impulse toward virtue at my description of vice and at my praise of virtue.

> *Kant.*

5

> Try in a dispute to make your words soft and your arguments firm. One must not vex an opponent but convince him.

> *Wilkins.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 298 -->

6

> Nothing so furthers the triumph of reason as the calmness of those who serve it. Truth often suffers more from the heat of its defenders than from the attacks of its opponents.

> *Penn.*

7

*Let the speaker be a fool—listener, be wise!*

A meek answer drives away malice; offending words stir up anger.

8

> If a person deserves praise, try not to deny it to him; otherwise you not only may thereby turn him aside from the true path, depriving him of the support and approval he needs, but you yourselves are deprived of the joy of giving a person his due for his labor.

> *John Ruskin*

If you know the truth, or even only think you know it, then pass it on as simply as possible, and above all, as gently as possible, as lovingly as possible toward the one to whom you pass it on.
