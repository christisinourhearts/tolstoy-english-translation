<!-- vol. 42, p. 181 -->

28 October

As the sensation of pain is a necessary condition for the preservation of our body, so sufferings are necessary conditions of our life from birth until death.

1

> As our body would burst if the pressure of the atmosphere on it ceased, so, if the pressure on human life of need, burdensome labor, and every kind of reversal of fortune ceased, the arrogance of people would grow, if not to the danger of bursting, still to manifestations of the most unbridled folly and even madness.

> *Schopenhauer.*

2

> A doctor prescribes one treatment for one patient, another for another; so too providence prescribes for us illnesses, mutilations, and grievous losses.

> As the doctor's prescription tends toward the restoration of the patient's health, so exactly do the accidents to which providence subjects a person tend toward his moral healing, toward the restoration of the connection of his torn-away personal existence with the common life of all mankind.

> So accept everything that falls to your lot as the sick accept a doctor's medicines. The restoration of the body's health—that is the meaning of these bitter medicines; but for universal reasonable nature the preservation by each being of its purpose is just as important as the preservation of the body's health is for the sick person.

> Therefore you must welcome everything that happens to you, even the most bitter, and according to the meaning of such accidents there is the health and wholeness of the universe. Nature, living by its reason, acts reasonably, and everything that proceeds from it unerringly furthers the preservation of unity.

> *Marcus Aurelius.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 182 -->

3

> Suffering is a spur to activity, and only in activity do we feel our life.

> *Kant.*

4

> It is good for a person to bear the misfortunes of this earthly life, for this draws him into the sacred solitude of his heart, where he finds himself as it were an exile from his native land and bound not to trust in any worldly joys. It is good for him also to meet with contradictions and reproaches, when people think and speak ill of him, even though his intentions are pure and his actions right, for such a course of action keeps him in humility and is an antidote to empty glory. This is good chiefly because in such moments, when we are despised in the world, not respected, and deprived of love, we can converse with that God who lives in us.

> *Thomas à Kempis.*

5

> If God gave us such teachers of whom we knew for certain that they were sent by him, we would surely obey them freely and gladly.

> And we do have such teachers: they are need and, in general, all the misfortunes of life.

> *Pascal.*

6

> Only in a storm is the art of the seafarer fully shown, only on the field of battle is the bravery of the warrior tested; but a person's courage is known only by what he proves to be in the difficult and dangerous situations of life.

> *Daniel.*

7

*Both what we call happiness and what we call unhappiness are equally useful to us if we look on the one and the other as a trial.*

<!-- vol. 42, p. 183 -->

8

> Do not grow used to prosperity—it is passing: whoever possesses—learn to lose, whoever is happy—learn to suffer.

> *Schiller.*

9

Torments and sufferings are experienced only by him who, having separated himself from the life of the world, not seeing those sins of his by which he brought suffering into the world, considers himself not guilty and therefore rebels against the sufferings that he bears for the sins of the world.

As the legend of the Wandering Jew, condemned as punishment to eternal life without death, is true in its thought, so a legend of a man condemned by way of punishment to life without suffering would be just as true.
