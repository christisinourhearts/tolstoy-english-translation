## THE THIEF'S SON

A jury was assembled in a certain town. Among the jurors were peasants, and nobles, and merchants. The foreman of the jury was a respected merchant, Ivan Akimovich Belov. Everyone respected this merchant for his good life: he dealt honestly, deceived no one, shortchanged no one, and helped people. He was an old man, close to seventy. The jurors gathered, took the oath, sat down in their places, and the accused was brought before them, a horse thief, for having driven off a peasant's horse. They were just about to begin the trial when Ivan Akimovich stood up and says to the judge: “Forgive me, Your Honor, I cannot judge.”

The judge was surprised: “What,” he says, “why?”

— I just cannot. Let me go.

And suddenly Ivan Akimovich's voice trembled, and he began to weep. He wept and wept, so that he could not speak. Then he collected himself and says to the judge:

— I cannot judge, Your Honor, because I and my father may be much worse than this thief; how can I judge someone the same as myself? I cannot; let me go, I beg you.

The judge let Ivan Akimovich go, and then in the evening called him to his house and began asking: “Why,” he says, “do you refuse to judge?”

<!-- vol. 41, p. 23 --> — This is why, — said Ivan Akimovich, and he told the judge this story about himself.

— You think, he says, that I am a merchant's son and that I was born in your town. That is not true. I am a peasant's son; my father was a peasant, the first thief in the district, and he died in prison. He was a kind man, only a drunkard, and when drunk he beat my mother, and brawled, and was ready for any bad deed, and afterward he would repent of it himself. Once he took me along with him to steal. And that very time my fortune was made.

— This is how it was. My father was in a tavern with some thieves, and they began talking about where they might make a haul. And my father says to them: “Here's what, lads. You know, he says, the merchant Belov's storehouse, the one that faces the street. Well, there's no end of goods in that storehouse. Only it's tricky to get in. But I've thought of something. And here's what I've thought of. There's a little window in that storehouse, only it's high and narrow; a grown man can't get through. So here's my idea. I have, he says, a boy, a nimble little fellow,” — that means me, — “so we'll take him with us, he says, tie a rope around him, boost him up to the window, he'll climb in, we'll let him down on the rope, and give him another rope in his hands, and on that rope he'll tie the goods from the storehouse for us, and we'll pull them out. And when we've got as much as we need, we'll pull him back out.”

— And the thieves liked this, and they say: “Well, then, bring your boy.”

— So father comes home and calls me. Mother says: “What do you want him for?” — “I need him, since I'm calling him.” Mother says: “He's out in the street.” — “Call him.” Mother knows that when he's drunk there's no talking to him; he'll beat her black and blue. She ran for me and called me. And father says to me: “Vanka! Are you good at climbing?” — I'll climb in anywhere you like. — “Well, he says, come with me.” Mother started to talk him out of it; he raised his hand at her, and she fell silent. Father took me, dressed me, and led me off with him. Led me off with him, brought me to the tavern; they gave me tea with sugar and something to eat, and we sat there till evening. When it got dark, they all went—there were three of them in all—and took me along.

— We came to that same house of the merchant Belov. At once they tied one rope around me, gave me the other in my hands, and lifted me up. “Aren't you afraid?” they say. — What's there to be afraid of? I'm not afraid of anything. — “Climb in the window, and see you get the best things from in there—mostly furs—and tie them to the rope, the one in your hands. And tie them, <!-- vol. 41, p. 24 --> mind, not at the end of the rope but in the middle of the rope, so that when we pull it out, you'll still have the end. Understand?” they say. — How could I not understand? I understand.

— So they boosted me up to the little window, I squeezed through it, and they began letting me down on the rope. I got my feet on solid ground and at once began feeling about with my little hands. I can't see a thing—it's dark—I just feel. Whenever I felt something of fur, straight to the rope with it: I tie it on, not at the end but in the middle, and they pull. I draw the rope back again and tie on again. They pulled out three such things or so, drew the whole rope up to themselves—that means, enough—and began pulling me up again. I hold on to the rope with my little hands, and they pull. They had pulled me only halfway up: snap! the rope broke, and I fell down. Good thing I landed on some pillows and didn't hurt myself.

— Only at that very moment, as I learned afterward, the watchman saw them and raised the alarm, and they took to their heels with what they had stolen.

— They ran off, and I was left; they had gone. I lie alone in the dark, and fear comes over me; I cry and shout: Mama, mama! Mama, mama! And I got so tired from fear and from tears, and I hadn't slept that night either, that I didn't even notice falling asleep on the pillows. Suddenly I wake up: in front of me stands that same merchant Belov with a lantern, and a policeman. The policeman began asking me who I had been with. I said—with father. —“And who is your father?” And I began to cry again. But old Belov says to the policeman: “God be with him. A child is a soul of God. It isn't right to make him inform on his father, and what's lost is lost.”

— He was a good man, God rest his soul. And his old wife was more tenderhearted still. She took me with her into the upper room, gave me sweets, and I stopped crying: a child, as everyone knows, is glad of everything. In the morning the mistress asks me: “Do you want to go home?” I don't know what to say. I say: yes, I do. “And do you want to stay with me?” she says. I say: I do. “Well, then, stay.”

— So I stayed. Stayed and stayed, and so lived with them. And they had papers drawn up for me, as for a foundling, and made me their adopted son. First I lived as an errand boy; then, as I grew up, they made me a clerk, and I ran the shop. I must have served not badly. And they were kind people, too; they grew so fond of me that they even gave me their daughter in marriage. And they <!-- vol. 41, p. 25 --> made me as their own son. And when the old man died, the whole estate came to me.

So that is who I am. A thief myself and a thief's son; how can I judge people? Besides, it is not a Christian thing, Your Honor. We must forgive and love all people, and if he, the thief, went wrong, he must not be punished but pitied. Remember what Christ said.

So said Ivan Akimovich.

And the judge stopped asking, and himself fell to thinking whether it is possible to judge people under Christian law.

*After Leskov, retold by L. N. Tolstoy.*
