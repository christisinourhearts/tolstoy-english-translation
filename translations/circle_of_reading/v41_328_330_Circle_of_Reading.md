16 May

Humanity has never lived and cannot live without religion.

1

The learned people of our time have decided that religion is not needed, that science will replace it or has already replaced it, and yet, before as now, no human society, no rational person has ever lived or can live without religion (I say *a rational person*—an irrational person, like an animal, can live without religion too).

And a rational person cannot live without religion because religion gives a rational person an understanding of his relation to the infinite world in the midst of which he lives, and the guidance in his actions that flows from this understanding.

A bee gathering food cannot have any doubts about whether it is good or bad to gather it. But a person, gathering a harvest or fruit, cannot help thinking about whether he is not destroying the growth of grain or fruit for the time to come, and about whether by this gathering he is not taking food away from his neighbors. Nor can he help thinking about what will become of those children whom he feeds, and much else. The most important questions of conduct in life cannot be finally decided by a rational person precisely because of the abundance of consequences, which he cannot help seeing. Every rational person, if he does not know, then feels that in the most important questions of life he cannot be guided either by personal promptings of feeling or by considerations of the nearest consequences of his activity, because he sees too many of these consequences, various and often contradictory, that is, such as may also, probably, be beneficial or harmful both for him and for other people.

Therefore a rational person cannot be satisfied with those considerations that guide the actions of animals. A person can regard himself as an animal among animals, living for the present day; he can regard himself also as a member of a family and as a member of a society, a people, living for centuries; he can and even absolutely must (because his reason irresistibly draws him to this) regard himself as part of the whole infinite world, living for infinite time. And <!-- vol. 41, p. 329 --> therefore a rational person had to establish and always did establish, besides his relation to the nearest phenomena of life, his relation to the whole world, infinite in time and space, understanding it as one whole. And such an establishment of a person's relation to that whole of which he feels himself a part and from which he derives guidance in his actions is precisely what was called and is called religion.

And therefore religion always has been and cannot cease to be a necessity and an inescapable condition of the life of a rational person and of rational humanity.

2

The stronger the religious feeling in a person, the clearer is the idea of what ought to be, and the more definite the guidance of actions.

Those who do not have this feeling, or have it in small degree, are guided, on the contrary, by what was—by the past, by tradition, and it is they, these people, whom the crowd calls religious. But the truly religious person disregards the past and, guided only by what ought to be, often appears to the crowd a godless man.

3

That one often sees people sacrificing everything, even life, for the sake of superstitions: duels, war, suicides, and rarely such as would give their life for truth, comes from this: that without conviction one can easily give one's life under the influence of suggestion produced by the approval of the crowd, and it is very hard to be so firmly convinced of the truth as to be ready to die for it in disagreement with the crowd.

4

> It is enough to stop up one's ears in a hall where people are dancing to imagine oneself in a madhouse. On a person who has destroyed religious consciousness in himself, all the religious acts of humanity must produce the same impression. But it is dangerous to put oneself outside the law of the human race and to consider oneself more right than all other people.

> *Amiel.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 330 -->

5

It is often said that religion has lost its power over people. But this is not so and cannot be. This comes from the fact that the people who think so observe only a certain class of people, devoid of religious feeling.

*If a person lives wretchedly, then the cause is always one: the absence of faith; the same is true of a society of people.*
