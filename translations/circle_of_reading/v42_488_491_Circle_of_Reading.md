## PASCAL

## \* No. 1 (MS. No. 1).

It is necessary to show that faith is necessary and that this faith exists, and both Pascal and Gogol take the one there is, submitting to it, without examining it. As a man who was dying of thirst throws himself on water without examining its quality. It is enough that it is water, enough that it is faith. Both the one and the other do not yet rely on faith in their life, do not yet know how it will act on them. They give themselves to the one there is. They have no time to examine it. They are both sick, suffering, close to death.

About Gogol we do not know, but Pascal is wholly visible in his Pensées. One sees all the intense work of his thought, directed chiefly at proving the futility, the stupidity of worldly life, the necessity of faith. And here he is extraordinarily strong. I know no one who could compare with him in this field. But where he occasionally, in snatches, seeks proofs, confirmation of the truths of Catholicism, he strikes one by the [^1] weakness of his argument and even of his methods.

## \* No. 2 (MS. No. 2).

~~A life the most simple and ordinary in its outward events, and a life terrible, tragic in its inner content.~~

A person needs two faiths for his good: one—to believe that there *is* an explanation of the meaning of life, that there *is* a true good for man, and the other—to find this best explanation of life and the way to this true good.

Pascal did this first thing as no one else did. Fate, God, did not let him do the second.

But even the first thing, especially in our time, is so important and great that one cannot value highly enough the work of this remarkable man, one of the true benefactors of humanity.

A man of great mind and great heart, one of those people who is able to see over the heads of other people and ages <!-- vol. 42, p. 489 --> what must be revealed to all people and make up the content of their life, one of those who are called prophets, begins to perceive the truth [^2] and sees that he himself lives madly, not thinking about what awaits him, about his purpose, and ruins the life given to him. He looks around him and sees that all [^3] people, just as he does, live a senseless life, trying to forget themselves and to hide their situation from themselves. And the prophet uses all the powers of his enormous mind and mighty word to turn attention to what is the one thing needful, and intends to show them that light where he sees it. He knows that it is [^4] in faith [^5]. And faith for him is that very Catholicism in which he was born, brought up, and grew firm. He [^6] believes that [^7] in Catholicism is the indubitable divine truth. The time will come, and he will prove the indubitability of this truth, but now he has no time: he has another task, one that precedes the proof of the truth of Catholicism—to prove the impossibility of life without faith in God and his law, and all that mighty, truthful light of his mind, [^8] he directed at this aim, and with remarkable force he attains it.

For him there could be no middle; he would have had to destroy everything and build it up again, or to believe without wavering what he believed. He chose the second. And the old house was enough for his life and death. And he dies with a blind faith in what he could not have believed in if he had had the time, but which nevertheless saves him. He believed, but sensed that his house was not sound. [^9]

## \* No. 3 (MS. No. 3).

And yet most of Pascal's critics, both new and old, [^10] and the whole intellectual crowd after them, not only do not understand all that Pascal did, but even allow themselves from the height of their enlightened freethinking and dull-wittedness [^11] to judge him.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 490 --> We had this with Belinsky, judging Gogol, and with the whole crowd, who, just like Belinsky, were unable to understand the height on which Gogol stood and therefore followed one who, just like them, did not understand him. The same happened with all the dull-witted literary critics who judged Pascal, inaccessible to them because of the height on which he stood.

Pascal showed people that people without religion are either animals or madmen—rubbed their noses in their ugliness and madness, and that no science can replace religion.

But Pascal believed in God, in the Trinity, in the Bible, and therefore for them it is a settled matter that what he told them about the ugliness of their life and the futility of the sciences is also untrue. That very science, that very vanity of life, whose madness Pascal made clear so irresistibly and truly, this very vanity and this very science—are the truth, and Pascal's reasonings—the fruit of his morbid abnormality (the same about Gogol). They liked the literary side of these arguments, they counted him among the classics, but *their content* is unnecessary to them, pitiful dull people, who are not able to understand Pascal's state of soul—the highest state of soul that a person can reach.

## \* No. 4 (MS. No. 4).

~~Both the one and the other do not yet rely on faith in their life, do not yet know how it will act on them. They hold to the one there is; they have no time to examine it. They are both sick, suffering, close to death.~~

~~About Gogol we do not know, but Pascal is wholly visible in his Pensées. One sees all the intense work of his.~~ Pascal puts all his energy into proving the futility, the stupidity of worldly life, without faith. This part of his Pensées is the height of perfection, persuasiveness, and force. I know no one who could compare with him in this field. But where he, in these same Pensées, occasionally, in snatches, sketches out those proofs which he will bring forward in confirmation of the truth of Catholicism, one sees searching, distrust of himself, and, above all, putting off to a future time. He evidently says to himself: first one must prove, so that people have no way out, the necessity of faith. When this has been proved, I will prove that this faith exists, and that this faith is precisely the one I profess.

## \* No. 5 (MS. No. 5).

Of course, one must remember that Pensées is not a work, but scraps of paper on which he jots down drafts of thought. But if Pensées is not a work, it is a document that makes it possible to look into the inner work of this great man. The necessity of religion, the madness of life without religion, is for him an indubitable truth, and with remarkable force he shows this truth from different sides. But the truth of dogmatic <!-- vol. 42, p. 491 --> Catholicism, though he thinks that he believes in it, is doubtful for him. From different sides he makes conjectures as to how to prove it, but puts off the proofs, sure that Catholicism is the truth, and the truth can always be proved.

## \* No. 6 (MS. No. 5).

But the light shines in the darkness too, and there are people who, not sharing Pascal's faith in Catholicism, but understanding that he, being a great thinker, could believe in it, understand and will understand all the great force of his arguments, which destroy, if not old-church Catholicism, then that new-church scientific superstition from the height of which people want to judge and condemn [^12] what they do not have the strength to understand.

## \* No. 7 (MS. No. 5).

And having realized this, such people direct all the powers of their mind and soul to saving people from that terrible delusion from which they have just emerged, and to showing them that one cannot live without faith, that only faith saves, try to snatch out of people's hands this screen which, as Pascal says, people hold in front of themselves while they run into the abyss.
