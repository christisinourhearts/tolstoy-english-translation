<!-- vol. 42, p. 274 -->

23 November

The defining of the meaning of life is either a very difficult and insoluble task—it is such when a person asks God: why did he send him into this world?—or a very simple one, when a person asks himself: what is he to do?

<!-- vol. 42, p. 275 -->

1

Human life, which can be cut off at any second, must, in order not to be the crudest mockery, have a meaning such that the significance of life does not depend on its length or brevity.

2

Travelers dirty and wreck the premises of an inn and then condemn the owner of the inn, who gave it over entirely to the use of the travelers. In the same way people condemn God for the evil of the world.

3

> It is as unbecoming for a wise man to reason much about the nature of beings standing higher than he as about the nature of beings lower than he. It is too immodest to suppose that man can comprehend the former, just as it is too degrading to suppose that he can concentrate his attention wholly on the latter. To recognize one's eternal relative greatness and insignificance, to know both oneself and one's place in nature, to be content with one's subordination to God, not being able to comprehend him, and to rule the lower creatures with love and kindness, not sharing their animal passions and not imitating them—this is what it means to be humble in relation to God, kind in relation to his creatures, and wise in relation to oneself.

> *John Ruskin.*

4

*In order to live without understanding the meaning of one's life, there is only one means: to live in a constant bodily stupor produced by tobacco, alcohol, morphine, or in a constant sensual stupor of entertainments and all kinds of amusements and diversions.*

5

This world is not a joke, not a vale only of trial and of passage into a better, eternal world, but it is one of the eternal worlds, which <!-- vol. 42, p. 276 --> is beautiful, joyful, and which we not only can but must make more beautiful and more joyful for those living with us and for all those who after us will live in it.

6

That the perfecting of our soul is the only aim of our life is true if only because every other aim, in view of death, is meaningless.

Do not think that perplexity before the meaning of human life and failure to understand it represent something lofty or tragic. A person's perplexity before the meaning of life is like the perplexity of a person who has come into a company occupied in reading a good book. The perplexity of this person, who does not listen closely to, or does not understand, what is being read, and who bustles about among people who are occupied, represents not something lofty and tragic, but something ridiculous, stupid, and pitiful.
