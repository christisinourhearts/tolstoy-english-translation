20 July

Compassion for living beings arouses in us a feeling like bodily pain. And just as one can grow callous to bodily pain, one can also grow callous to the pain of compassion.

1

> Compassion for all living beings is the surest and most reliable guarantee of moral conduct. Whoever is truly compassionate will surely insult no one, wrong no one, cause no one pain, call no one to account, forgive everyone, so that all his actions will bear the stamp of justice and love of mankind. Let someone say: “This is a virtuous man, but he knows no pity,” or: “This is an unjust and evil man, but he is full of pity”—and you will feel the contradiction.

> *Schopenhauer.*

2

Enough, you people, of defiling yourselves with food that is not permitted!  
You have the grains for bread; under the weight of a rich burden  
Of juicy, ruddy fruits the branches of the trees bow down;

Clusters hang full and ripe on the vines; roots and herbs,  
Tender and tasty, ripen in the fields; and others—  
Those that are coarser—fire softens and makes sweeter;  
The pure milky moisture and the fragrant combs  
Of sweet honey that smells of a sweet-scented herb, thyme,  
Are not forbidden you. Prodigally, generously, all good things  
The earth offers you; without cruel killings and without blood  
She prepares tasty dishes for you.  
Only wild beasts  
Satisfy their hunger with living flesh; and not all beasts even:  
Horses, sheep, oxen—they feed peacefully on grass, after all;  
Only the ferocious breeds of predators: fierce tigers,  
Mercilessly cruel lions, greedy wolves, bears  
Rejoice in the shedding of blood...  
And what a criminal custom,  
What a terrible abomination: guts swallowing guts!  
Can we fatten on the flesh and blood of creatures like us  
Our greedy bodies, and by the killing of another creature—  
By another's death—sustain our life?  
Is it not shameful  
For us, surrounded so generously by the gifts of the bountiful earth,  
Our mother and nurse—for us—not animals, but people—  
Greedily with cruel teeth to rip and tear with relish  
Shreds of wounded corpses, like fierce wild beasts?  
Is it not possible to satisfy, without sacrificing another's life,  
People, your frenzied hunger, the greed of insatiable bellies?  
There was—the tradition has been preserved—a golden age—not in vain  
So named; people lived happy, meek—simply;  
They were content and fed with the fruits of the earth alone,  
They did not defile their lips with blood. And the birds then safely  
Cut through the air all around; and the timid hares fearlessly  
Roamed the field; the little fish did not then hang on the hook,  
A victim of its trust; there were no cunning snares and traps;  
Fear, treachery, malice no one knew. And everywhere  
Peace reigned.  
Where is it now? And how have you deserved your death,  
You harmless sheep, gentle, meek creatures,  
Born for the good of people? You who generously give us to drink  
The moisture of your bountiful teats and warm us with soft wool.

You, whose happy life is more useful to us than your cruel death?  
How are you guilty, ox, destined to be a help to people,  
You, the uncomplainingly obedient comrade and friend of the plowman?  
How can one forget gratitude, how bring oneself with a cruel hand  
To bring the sharp ax down on the obedient, meek neck  
Rubbed raw by the heavy yoke? To stain the mother and nurse, the earth,  
With the hot blood of the worker who gave her a harvest?..  
Terrible is your vile custom, and slippery your path to crimes,  
People! It is not hard to kill a man for one who, listening  
To the pitiful death rattle, slaughters innocent calves,  
Who kills the lamb whose feeble cries are like  
The weeping of a child, who strikes down a bird of the sky for amusement  
Or—having fed it on purpose with his own hand—devours it!  
Right beside your habitual cruelty stands cannibalism!  
Oh, abstain, come to your senses, I implore you, brothers!  
Do not tear the farmer's ox from the plow by killing;  
Let him who served you faithfully die no violent death:  
Do not destroy the defenseless flocks: let them clothe you,  
Warm you with soft fleece, and generously give you their milk to drink,  
Living peacefully, dying calmly in your pastures.  
Throw away the snares and traps! Do not touch the little birds of the sky;  
Let them, fluttering carefree, sing to us of happiness and freedom.  
Cunningly woven nets, hooks with deadly bait  
Throw away! Do not catch trusting fish by treacherous deceit,  
Do not defile human lips with the blood of living creatures;  
Mortals—spare mortals!  
Feed on permitted food—  
Food fit for the loving, pure soul of man.

*Ovid (translated by A. P. Barykova).*

> *The first condition for carrying religion into life is love and pity for all that lives.*

> *Fo-pen-hing-tzi-king.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 514 -->

4

> Compassion for animals is so closely bound up with goodness of character that one may confidently assert that whoever is cruel to animals cannot be a good man.

> *Schopenhauer.*

5

> All killing is loathsome, but perhaps the most loathsome of all is killing for the purpose of eating the creature that has been killed. And the more a man thinks out the form of the killing, the more he concentrates his attention and care on eating the killed animal with the greatest pleasure, on giving the killed creature the greatest tastiness, the more loathsome this killing is.

> *Goldstein.*

When you feel pain at the sight of another being's suffering, do not give way to the first animal feeling of hiding the spectacle of suffering from yourself, of running away from the sufferer, but, on the contrary, run to the sufferer and look for means to help him.
