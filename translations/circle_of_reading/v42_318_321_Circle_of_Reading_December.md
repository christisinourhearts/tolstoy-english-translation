## THE TEACHING OF THE TWELVE APOSTLES PREFACE

In 1883 the Greek Metropolitan Bryennios found in Constantinople, in an old collection of ancient Christian instructions, a work under the title: “The Teaching of the 12 Apostles” or “The Teaching of the Lord, Imparted to the Nations through the 12 Apostles.” Of this book, which some teachers of the church regarded as sacred, only its title had been known until then.

This work contains the very essence of the Christian teaching. It conveys in different words, and with some additions and explanations, those great truths and instructions that are set forth in the Sermon on the Mount in Matthew and in chapter 6 of Luke. Thus, for example, to the instruction about giving to him who asks, there is added: “Blessed is he who gives according to the commandment; for he is free from punishment; but woe to him who receives, for he who takes when he is in need is in the right; but he who is not in need will give an account of why and for what he took.” There is also an explanation, absent from the Gospels, that alms are alms only when they come from a sweating hand, that is, when what is given has been earned by the labors of the giver.

The same is said still more clearly in chapter 4. There it is said that a Christian must consider nothing his own property and can help the needy only by his own labor.

There is also in this work a beautiful and very important instruction, not found in the Gospels, about how a <!-- vol. 42, p. 319 --> Christian should relate to people according to their state of soul. “Have no hatred toward anyone,” it says there, “but rebuke some, pray for others, and love others more than your own soul.”

Evidently, the advice to rebuke some refers to those who err out of ignorance, out of being carried away, to those whom rebuke can help to enter on the good path. To pray, on the other hand, is advised for those who are deaf to rebuke and exhortation. This evidently refers to those about whom it is said in the Gospel that one should not cast pearls before those who cannot value them. Here the same thought is expressed more gently and kindly. This teaching advises not turning away from such people, but praying for them, that is, never ceasing to wish them the true good and always being ready, should they soften, to come to their help. To love more than one's own soul evidently refers to those who are united by one faith.

Also important and new is the instruction in chapter 6 about how to answer the usual objection against the teaching of Christ made by people who do not wish to accept it. “If one is to fulfill it, then one must fulfill all of it,” such objectors say. “But if one fulfills all of it, then one must renounce life, and that is impossible.” The answer to this objection is this:

“Beware of him who would lead you astray from this path, since he teaches you not in God's way, because if you can bear the whole yoke of the Lord, you will be perfect, and if you cannot, then do what you can.”

Besides these and many other new and remarkable explanations, this book contains definite directions about how baptism should be performed. It is said that one must baptize thus: having communicated all the above teaching to the one being baptized (consequently, an adult), baptize in the name of the father and the son and the holy spirit. Likewise the Eucharist is spoken of as a prayer of thanksgiving read in the common assembly at a meal, without any hint of a sacrament. Of prayer the same is said as in the Gospel, that prayer must consist in reading the “Our Father.”

Directions are also given about how to choose bishops and deacons—as elected officials of the community, without the slightest indication of ordination. There are also many other rules about apostles and prophets that in no way agree with the institutions that exist now.

<!-- vol. 42, p. 320 --> And now this book appears, recognized by all scholars as a work of the end of the first or the beginning of the second century, that is, a Christian monument earlier than the Gospel of Luke and contemporary with the Gospel of John, a voice confirming, clarifying, and strengthening all that we know of the moral and practical side of Christianity, and disagreeing in much, and in what is most essential, with the external side of Christianity. And what then? It would seem that the discovery of such a monument ought to produce the greatest excitement in the Christian world. All Christians, it would seem, ought to seize upon this monument, examine its content, go deeply into its meaning, compare their established tenets with it, and correct them by it; they ought to have spread this work among the people in millions of copies and read it in churches. Nothing of the kind is happening or has happened. A dozen scholars have examined this monument from the general church and historical point of view; a few specialists in false interpretation from among the priests have devised some arguments from which it comes out that the later institutions are right, and not those written of in this work; so that the discovery of “The Teaching of the Lord to the Nations through the 12 Apostles,” that is, the hearing of the voice of holy people of the first centuries of Christianity, a voice confirming, clarifying, and strengthening all that we know of the moral side of Christianity—the discovery of this monument produces a far smaller impression on Christian society than the discovery of a piece of a naked Venus in some excavation.

The posthumous writings of some unhappy madman, Nietzsche or Verlaine, come to light, and hundreds of thousands of copies are printed and distributed. But the words of that Christ whom we supposedly confess ring out, and we only try somehow to be rid of them as quickly as possible, so that they will not hinder us from occupying ourselves with our important affairs: the discovery of a new planet, disputes about the origin of species, discussions about the properties of radium, which are of no use for anything.

Yes, it is precisely this: “The heart of these people has grown coarse, and with their ears they hear with difficulty, and they have closed their eyes, lest they see with their eyes and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and lest they turn, so that I should heal them” (Isaiah 6:9–10, Matthew 13:15).

But, thank God, there are still people among the masses of the common people for whom this voice from the times of the first century is important and who will joyfully <!-- vol. 42, p. 321 --> find in it a still greater clarification and confirmation of that truth which illumines their life and gives them strength. It is for these people that I am writing this and reprinting this work.

*L. N. Tolstoy.*
