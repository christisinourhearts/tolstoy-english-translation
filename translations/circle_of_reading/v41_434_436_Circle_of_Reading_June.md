> THE EAGLE

> We also had an eagle (Karagush) living with us in the prison for some time, of the breed of small steppe eagles. Someone brought him into the prison wounded and exhausted. The whole penal camp crowded around him; he could not fly: his right wing trailed on the ground, one leg was dislocated. I remember how he glared furiously about him, surveying the curious crowd, and opened his hooked beak, getting ready to sell his life dearly.

> When they had looked their fill at him and begun to disperse, he hobbled off, limping, hopping on one leg and flapping his sound wing, to the very farthest end of the prison, where he huddled in a corner, pressed tightly against the palisade. Here he lived with us about three months, and in all that time he never once came out of his corner. At first people often came to look at him and set the dog on him. Sharik would hurl himself at him furiously but evidently was afraid to come closer, which greatly amused the convicts.

> — A wild beast! — they said, — he won't give in!

> Then Sharik too began to hurt him badly; his fear had passed, and when they set him on, he contrived to seize him by the injured wing. The eagle defended himself with all his strength, with claws and beak, and, huddled in his corner, proudly and wildly, like a wounded king, surveyed the curious who came to look at him. At last everyone grew tired of him, everyone abandoned and forgot him, and yet every day one could see near him scraps of fresh meat and a potsherd with water. Someone, then, was looking after him. He <!-- vol. 41, p. 435 --> would not even eat at first, did not eat for several days; at last he began to take food, but never from anyone's hand or in front of people.

> More than once I happened to watch him from a distance. Seeing no one and thinking he was alone, he would sometimes venture a little way out of his corner and hobble along the palisade, about twelve paces from his place, then go back again, then come out again, just as if he were taking exercise.

> On catching sight of me, he would at once, with all his strength, limping and hopping, hurry to his place and, throwing back his head, opening wide his beak, bristling, at once make ready for battle. I could not soften him with any caresses: he bit and struggled, would not take beef from me, and the whole time I stood over him he would look intently, intently into my eyes with his fierce, piercing gaze. Alone and in anger he awaited death, trusting no one and reconciled with no one.

> At last the convicts seemed to remember him, and although no one had cared about him or even mentioned him for about two months, suddenly sympathy for him seemed to appear in everyone. They began saying that the eagle should be carried out.

> — Let him die if he must, only not in the prison, — some said.

> — Sure, he's a free bird, a fierce one; you won't get him used to prison, — others agreed.

> — Seems he's not like us, — someone added.

> — Look what he's blurted out: that's a bird, and we, that means, are human beings.

> — The eagle, brothers, is the king of the forests... — began the glib talker Skuratov, but this time they would not listen to him.

> Once after dinner, when the drum had beaten for work, they took the eagle, clamping his beak shut with a hand, because he had begun to fight savagely, and carried him out of the prison.

> They came to the rampart. The twelve or so men who were in this work party were curious to see where the eagle would go. A strange thing: everyone was somehow pleased, just as if they themselves had in part received freedom.

> — Look at him, dog's meat: you do him good, and he keeps on biting! — said the one holding him, looking almost lovingly at the fierce bird.

> — Let him go, Mikitka!

> — No use building him a devil in a suitcase, you know. Give him liberty, real liberty, sweet liberty.

> <!-- vol. 41, p. 436 --> They threw the eagle from the rampart into the steppe. It was late in the autumn, on a cold and gloomy day. The wind whistled over the bare steppe and rustled in the yellowed, withered, tufted steppe grass. The eagle set off straight ahead, flapping his injured wing and as if hurrying to get away from us, wherever his eyes might lead him.

> The convicts watched with curiosity as his head flickered in the grass.

> — Look at him! — one said thoughtfully.

> — And he won't even look back! — added another.

> — Not once, brothers, has he looked back; off he runs!

> — And did you think he'd come back to thank us? — remarked a third.

> — Sure thing, it's liberty! He's scented liberty.

> — Freedom, that is.

> — And you can't see him now, brothers...

> — What are you standing there for? March! — the escort guards shouted; and everyone trudged off to work in silence.

> *F. M. Dostoevsky (from “Notes from the House of the Dead”).*
