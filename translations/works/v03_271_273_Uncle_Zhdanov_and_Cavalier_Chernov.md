## \* UNCLE ZHDANOV AND CAVALIER CHERNOV.

(1854)

## (First redaction)

~~In 1828, twenty-five recruits were driven to one of the artillery companies stationed on the Caucasian Line. They were all young men—fleshy, awkward, with white-cropped heads and gloomy, heavy faces. Among them there was only one, Chernov, a tall man with light-brown moustaches and deft, self-confident movements, who drew attention to himself. Chernov wore a pink shirt; he played the balalaika, danced, and was forever joking and laughing. The mess naturally came under his influence; they obeyed him and tried to imitate him, but the merriment of the other recruits was somehow awkward and pitiful.—Only one recruit never tried to stupefy himself with vodka, the balalaika, and laughter; he did not hide his grief and sincerely gave himself up to it. He was a small, white-headed lad with large blue eyes; he never went up to his comrades, did not drink, did not talk or listen, but always sat apart with his head lowered, took out a folding knife, his only possession, picked up some little stick, whittled it, and cried.—What did he think about, what did he cry about? God knows.~~

~~His comrades mocked him and made him drink. He got drunk and cried still more, muttering to himself. They wanted him to stand a round too. He refused. They beat him, and he gave up his last two rubles and began crying again. When the recruits were brought to the company, the noncommissioned officer told the sergeant major that a smart soldier would come out of the recruits.~~

## (Second redaction)

I want to tell the simple story of two people whom I knew for a long time and as closely as only comrades know one another. I loved one of them very much, while I often reflected bitterly on the fate of the other.—They were two soldiers in the battery, <!-- vol. 3, p. 272 --> in which I served as a junker in the Caucasus, and neither of them is now in this world. In 1828 they were brought to the Line in a party of recruits.—

One of them, Chernov, from among the household serfs of Saratov Province, was a tall, slender man with black moustaches and lively, darting eyes. Chernov wore a pink shirt—throughout the march he played the balalaika, danced, drank vodka, and treated his comrades.

The other recruit, Zhdanov, from the peasants of the same province, was a short, fleshy lad of about nineteen, with large round blue eyes and a white-cropped nape.—

All Zhdanov possessed was four shirts, a folding knife, and a twenty-kopeck piece. He could not give his comrades drinks, but like them he tried to stupefy himself with wine and merriment. His merriment, however, was somehow awkward ~~wild~~ and pitiful. Once they got him drunk, and he actually began dancing on tiptoe in soldier fashion, but suddenly burst into tears, threw himself on Chernov's neck,[^1] and [began] saying such nonsense that everyone found it funny. The next day he stood a round and cried again. Most of the time on the marches he slept, and when he did not sleep, he went up to Chernov and, with his mouth hanging open, listened to his stories and jokes and kept laughing.

The noncommissioned officer who drove the party, and whom Zhdanov feared worse than fire, told the company sergeant major: Chernov and some of the others are good, but Zhdanov is a complete little fool and there will be a great deal[^2] of beating for him. And indeed Zhdanov was beaten a great deal. They beat him at drill, beat him at work, beat him in the barracks. His meekness and lack of any gift for speech gave his superiors the very worst opinion of him; and a recruit has many superiors: every soldier a year senior to him ordered him about wherever and however he pleased.—

At first the transition from the lax supervision under which recruits are kept to the strictness and even injustice with which young soldiers are treated once they arrive utterly bewildered poor Zhdanov. He imagined that he was very bad and that he had to try to become better, and he began trying. He became zealous—to the point of stupidity—but his position only became worse because of it. He had not a minute's rest: every soldier ordered him about like a boy, considered himself entitled to demand from him what he had previously done of his own free will, and to call him to account for it.—When at last he understood that zeal only harmed his position, despair took possession of him. “But what is this, really!” he thought. “What am I to do? So this is soldiering!”—and <!-- vol. 3, p. 273 --> the poor fellow saw no way out and cried bitterly at night on his bunk.—

This moral state did not last long—the truth was that there was no way out. One thing remained—to endure. And he endured not only without complaint, but with the conviction that his one duty was to endure and endure.

They drove him out to drill—he went; they put a hanger in his hand and ordered him to move his arm this way—he did as best he could; they beat him—he endured. They did not beat him so that he would do better, but because he was a soldier, and a soldier had to be beaten. They drove him out to work; he went and worked, and they beat him; they beat him again not so that he would work more or better, but because that was how it had to be.—He understood this. When the work or drill was over, he went to the kettle, took a piece of bread, sat down apart, and bit into his piece, thinking about nothing. As soon as a thought entered his head, he was frightened of it as though it were a visitation of the unclean spirit and tried to fall asleep.—

When an older soldier came up to him, he took off his cap, drew himself up rigidly, and was ready to run at full speed wherever he was ordered; and if the soldier raised his hand to scratch the back of his head, Zhdanov already expected to be beaten, squeezed his eyes shut, and grimaced.—
