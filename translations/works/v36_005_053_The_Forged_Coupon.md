# \*\* THE FORGED COUPON

## PART ONE

## I

Fyodor Mikhailovich Smokovnikov, chairman of the Treasury Chamber, a man of incorruptible honesty, and proud of it, and grimly liberal, and not merely a freethinker but a man who hated every manifestation of religiosity, which he regarded as a remnant of superstitions, came home from the Chamber in the worst of tempers. The governor had written him an exceedingly stupid official paper, from which one might infer a reprimand to the effect that Fyodor Mikhailovich had acted dishonestly. Fyodor Mikhailovich became very angry and at once wrote a brisk and cutting reply.

At home, it seemed to Fyodor Mikhailovich, everything was being done to thwart him.

It was five minutes to five. He thought dinner would be served at once, but dinner was not ready yet. Fyodor Mikhailovich slammed the door and went off to his room. Someone knocked at the door. “Who the devil is it now?” he thought, and shouted:

“Who is it now?”

Into the room came a schoolboy of the fifth class at the gymnasium, a boy of fifteen, Fyodor Mikhailovich’s son.

“What do you want?”

“It’s the first today.”

“What? Money?”

It had been arranged that on the first of every month the father gave his son an allowance of three rubles for amusements. Fyodor Mikhailovich frowned, took out his wallet, searched in it, and drew out a coupon for two and a half rubles; then he took out a little purse with silver and counted out another fifty kopeks. His son said nothing and did not take it.

“Papa, please give me some in advance.”

“What?”

“I wouldn’t ask, but I borrowed on my word of honor, I promised.

“As an honest man I can’t... I need another three rubles; really, I won’t ask again... not that I won’t ask again, but simply... please, Papa.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 6 --> “You’ve been told...”

“But Papa, it’s only once...”

“You get an allowance of three rubles, and it’s still not enough. At your age I didn’t get even fifty kopeks.”

“All my schoolmates get more now. Petrov, Ivanitsky get fifty rubles.”

“And I’ll tell you this: if you behave like that, you’ll be a swindler. I’ve said it.”

“So what if you’ve said it. You’ll never put yourself in my position; I’ll have to be a scoundrel. You have it easy.”

“Get out, you good-for-nothing. Out.”

Fyodor Mikhailovich sprang to his feet and rushed at his son.

“Out. Boys like you ought to be thrashed.”

The son was frightened and angered, but more angered than frightened, and, lowering his head, he walked quickly to the door. Fyodor Mikhailovich did not want to hit him, but he was glad of his anger and went on shouting abuse after his son for a long time.

When the maid came and said that dinner was ready, Fyodor Mikhailovich got up.

“At last,” he said. “Now I don’t even feel like eating.”

And he went in to dinner, scowling.

At the table his wife began speaking to him, but he grunted such a short, angry reply that she fell silent. His son too did not raise his eyes from his plate and said nothing. They ate in silence, and in silence they got up and went their separate ways.

After dinner the schoolboy went back to his room, took the coupon and the small change out of his pocket and threw them on the table, and then took off his uniform and put on a jacket. First the schoolboy took up his tattered Latin grammar, then he fastened the door with the hook, swept the money off the table into the drawer with his hand, took some cigarette tubes out of the drawer, filled one, plugged it with cotton wool, and began to smoke.

He sat over his grammar and exercise books for about two hours without understanding anything, then got up and began pacing the room, stamping his heels, and remembering everything that had happened with his father. All his father’s abusive words, and especially his angry face, came back to him, as though he were hearing and seeing him at that very moment. “Good-for-nothing. Ought to be thrashed.” And the more he remembered, the angrier he became with his father. He remembered his father saying to him: “I can see what you’re going to turn into—a swindler. Mark my words.” “And of course you’ll turn into a swindler, if that’s how it is. He has it easy. He’s forgotten what it was like to be young. Well, what crime have I committed? I simply went to the theater, I had no money, I borrowed from Petya Grushetsky. What’s wrong with that? Someone else would have felt sorry for me, would have asked, but this one only scolds and thinks about himself. When he hasn’t got something, there’s shouting through the whole house, but I’m the swindler. “No, even if he is my father, I don’t love him. I don’t know whether everyone is like this, but I don’t love him.”

The maid knocked at the door. She had brought a note.

“They said you must send an answer.”

The note said: “This is already the third time I have asked you to return the six rubles you took from me, but you keep dodging.

“ <!-- vol. 36, p. 7 --> Honest people don’t behave that way. Please send it at once with the bearer of this. I am in desperate need myself. Can it be that you can’t get it?

“Your schoolmate, who despises or respects you according to whether you pay it back or not,

“Grushetsky.”

“Well, now think what to do. What a pig he is. He can’t wait. I’ll try once more.”

Mitya went to his mother. She was his last hope. His mother was kind and did not know how to refuse, and she might perhaps have helped him, but that day she was worried by the illness of the youngest, two-year-old Petya. She became angry with Mitya for coming in and making a noise, and refused him at once.

He muttered something under his breath and went out the door. She felt sorry for her son and called him back.

“Wait, Mitya,” she said. “I haven’t got it now, but tomorrow I’ll get it.”

But Mitya’s anger at his father was still boiling.

“What good is tomorrow to me, when I need it today? Then you should know that I’m going to a schoolmate.”

He went out, slamming the door.

“There’s nothing else to do. He’ll show me where to pawn the watch,” he thought, feeling the watch in his pocket.

Mitya took the coupon and the change from the desk, put on his coat, and went to Makhin.

## II

Makhin was a schoolboy with a moustache. He played cards, knew women, and always had money. He lived with his aunt. Mitya knew that Makhin was not a good sort, but when he was with him he could not help submitting to him. Makhin was at home getting ready to go to the theater: his dirty little room smelled of scented soap and eau de cologne.

“That, my friend, is the last resort,” said Makhin, when Mitya had told him his trouble, shown him the coupon and the fifty kopeks, and said that he needed nine rubles. “You could pawn the watch, or you could do better.” Makhin said this winking with one eye.

“Better how?”

“Very simple.” Makhin took the coupon.

“Put a one in front of the 2 rubles 50, and it’ll be 12 rubles 50.”

“But are there coupons like that?”

“Of course. On thousand-ruble securities. I’ve passed one like that myself.”

“It can’t be?”

“Well, then, shall I go ahead?” said Makhin, taking up the pen and smoothing out the coupon with a finger of his left hand.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 8 --> “But it’s wrong.”

“Oh, what nonsense.”

“And so it is,” Mitya thought, and again he remembered his father’s abuse: swindler. “Well, then I’ll be a swindler.” He looked into Makhin’s face. Makhin was looking at him, smiling calmly.

“Well, shall I go ahead?”

“Go ahead.”

Makhin carefully wrote in the figure one.

“There. Now let’s go to the shop. Right here on the corner: photographic supplies. As it happens, I need a frame, for this person here.”

He took out a photograph of a large-eyed young woman with an enormous mass of hair and a magnificent bust.

“What a darling, eh?”

“Yes, yes. But how...”

“Very simple. Come on.”

Makhin got dressed and they went out together.

## III

The bell on the entrance door of the photographic shop rang. The schoolboys came in, looking around the empty shop with its shelves set out with supplies and with display cases on the counters. A plain woman with a kind face came out of the back door and, taking her place behind the counter, asked what they needed.

“A nice little frame, madam.”

“At what price?” asked the lady. With her hands in fingerless gloves, the joints of her fingers swollen, she quickly and deftly sorted through frames of different styles. “These are fifty kopeks, and these are a little more expensive. And this one here is very pretty, a new style, one ruble twenty.”

“Well, give us this one. But couldn’t you come down a little? Take a ruble.”

“We don’t bargain here,” the lady said with dignity.

“Well, have it your way,” said Makhin, putting the coupon down on the display case.

“Give us the frame and the change, and be quick about it. We mustn’t be late for the theater.”

“You still have time,” said the lady. She began examining the coupon with her short-sighted eyes.

“It’ll look sweet in this little frame. Eh?” Makhin said, turning to Mitya.

“Haven’t you any other money?” said the saleswoman.

“That’s just the trouble, we haven’t. My father gave it to me, and I’ve got to change it.”

“Don’t you really have a ruble twenty?”

“There’s fifty kopeks. But what is it, are you afraid we’re cheating you with counterfeit money?”

“No, I didn’t mean anything.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 9 --> “Then give it back. We’ll change it.”

“So how much do I owe you?”

“Well, that makes eleven and something.”

The saleswoman clicked the beads of the abacus, unlocked the desk, took out ten rubles in a note, and, stirring the small change with her hand, gathered up six twenty-kopek pieces and two five-kopek pieces more.

“Kindly wrap it up,” said Makhin, taking the money without any hurry.

“Right away.”

The saleswoman wrapped it up and tied it with string.

Mitya only drew breath when the bell of the entrance door rang behind them and they were out in the street.

“Well, here are ten rubles for you, and give me these. I’ll pay you back.”

And Makhin went off to the theater, and Mitya went to Grushetsky and settled up with him.

## IV

An hour after the schoolboys left, the owner of the shop came home and began counting the day’s takings.

“Ah, you clumsy fool! What a fool!” he shouted at his wife when he saw the coupon and at once noticed the forgery. “And why take coupons at all?”

“But you yourself have taken them in front of me, Zhenya, and twelve-ruble ones at that,” his wife said, embarrassed, upset, and ready to cry. “I don’t know myself how they fooled me,” she went on, “those schoolboys. A handsome young man, he seemed so comme il faut.”

“A comme-il-faut fool,” her husband went on abusing her as he counted the cash. “When I take a coupon, I know and see what is written on it. And you, I suppose, were only looking at the schoolboys’ mugs in your old age.”

His wife could not bear this and became angry herself.

“A real man! Only good at judging others, but when you yourself lose fifty-four rubles at cards—that’s nothing.”

“I’m a different matter.”

“I don’t want to talk to you,” said his wife. She went to her room and began remembering how her family had not wanted to give her in marriage, considering her husband far beneath her in position, and how she alone had insisted on this marriage; she remembered her dead child and her husband’s indifference to this loss, and came to hate her husband so much that she thought how good it would be if he died. But, having thought this, she was frightened by her own feelings and hurried to dress and leave. When her husband returned to the apartment, his wife was already gone. Without waiting for him, she had dressed and gone off alone to a French teacher she knew, who had invited them to an evening party that day.

## V

At the French teacher’s, a Russian Pole, there was a formal tea with sweet cookies, and afterward they sat down at several tables to play vint.

The wife of the seller of photographic supplies sat down with the host, an officer, and an old, deaf lady in a wig, the widow of the proprietor of a music shop, who was very fond of playing and very good at it. The cards were coming to the wife of the seller of photographic supplies. Twice she declared a slam. Beside her stood a small plate with grapes and a pear, and she felt cheerful at heart.

“Why isn’t Yevgeny Mikhailovich coming?” the hostess asked from the other table. “We had him down as a fifth player.”

“He’s probably caught up in his accounts,” said Yevgeny Mikhailovich’s wife. “Today he settles up for the provisions, for the firewood.”

And remembering the scene with her husband, she frowned, and her hands in their fingerless gloves began trembling with anger at him.

“Here he is, just as we were talking about him,” said the host, addressing Yevgeny Mikhailovich as he came in. “Why are you so late?”

“Oh, all sorts of business,” Yevgeny Mikhailovich answered in a cheerful voice, rubbing his hands. And, to his wife’s surprise, he came over to her and said:

“And you know, I got rid of the coupon.”

“Really?”

“Yes, to a peasant, for firewood.”

And Yevgeny Mikhailovich told everyone with great indignation—his wife put details into his story—how the shameless schoolboys had swindled his wife.

“Well, now to business,” he said, sitting down at the table when his turn came, and shuffling the cards.

## VI

Yevgeny Mikhailovich had indeed got rid of the coupon, for firewood, to the peasant Ivan Mironov.

Ivan Mironov traded in this way: at the wood depots he bought one sazhen of firewood, carted it about the town, and stacked it in such a way that one sazhen yielded five quarters, which he sold for the same price a quarter cost at the woodyard. On this day, so unlucky for Ivan Mironov, he had taken out an eighth early in the morning and, having quickly sold it, loaded up another eighth and hoped to sell it, but he drove it about until evening, trying to find a buyer, but nobody bought. Again and again he came upon experienced townspeople who knew the usual tricks of peasants selling firewood and did not believe that he had brought the wood, as he assured them, from the village. He himself had grown hungry and was chilled through in his rubbed-bare sheepskin coat and torn <!-- vol. 36, p. 11 --> homespun overcoat; by evening the frost had reached twenty degrees; his wretched little horse, which he did not spare, since he meant to sell it to the flayers (the knackers), had come to a complete standstill. So Ivan Mironov was ready to let the wood go even at a loss when he met Yevgeny Mikhailovich, who had gone to the shop for tobacco and was returning home.

“Take it, sir. I’ll let you have it cheap. The little horse has given out completely.”

“And where are you from?”

“We’re from the village. It’s our own wood. Good and dry.”

“We know you people. Well, what will you take?”

Ivan Mironov named a price, began lowering it, and finally sold the wood at cost.

“Only for you, sir, because I don’t have to haul it far,” he said.

Yevgeny Mikhailovich did not bargain very hard, glad at the thought that he would get rid of the coupon. Somehow, pulling at the shafts himself, Ivan Mironov got the wood into the courtyard and unloaded it into the shed himself. The porter was not there. At first Ivan Mironov hesitated to take the coupon, but Yevgeny Mikhailovich persuaded him so well and seemed such an important gentleman that he agreed to take it.

Having come in by the back steps into the maids’ room, Ivan Mironov crossed himself, thawed the icicles from his beard, and, turning back the skirt of his kaftan, took out a leather purse and from it eight rubles and fifty kopeks, and gave the change; and the coupon he wrapped in a scrap of paper and put into the purse.

After thanking the gentleman, as was the custom, Ivan Mironov drove his empty cart to the tavern, urging on, no longer with the lash but with the handle of his whip, the frost-covered little nag, doomed to death, which could scarcely move its legs.

At the tavern Ivan Mironov ordered eight kopeks’ worth of vodka and tea and, having warmed up and even broken into a sweat, was talking in the merriest of moods with a porter sitting at his table. He got talking with him and told him all his circumstances. He told him that he came from the village of Vasilyevskoye, twelve versts from town; that he had separated from his father and brothers and now lived with his wife and two children, of whom the elder only went to school and did not yet help with anything. He told him that he was lodging here at a “partment” (an apartment) and would go to the horse market tomorrow, sell his old jade, and look around, and, if it came to that, buy a little horse. He told him that he had now got together twenty-five rubles less one, and that half his money was in the coupon. He took out the coupon and showed it to the porter. The porter was illiterate, but said that he had changed such money for the tenants, that it was good money, but there were forged ones too, and so he advised him, to be sure, to hand it over here at the counter. Ivan Mironov handed it to the waiter and told him to bring the change, but the waiter did not bring the change; instead a bald clerk with a shiny face came over, holding the coupon in his plump hand.

“Your money is no good,” he said, showing the coupon but not giving it back.

“It’s good money. A gentleman gave it to me.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 12 --> “That’s just it: it isn’t good, it’s forged.”

“If it’s forged, then give it here.”

“No, brother. Your kind need to be taught a lesson. You forged this with some swindlers.”

“Give me the money! What full right have you got?”

“Sidor! Go and fetch a policeman,” the barman said to the waiter.

Ivan Mironov had been drinking. And when he had been drinking, he was unruly. He seized the clerk by the collar and shouted:

“Give it back! I’ll go to the gentleman. I know where he is.”

The clerk jerked away from Ivan Mironov, and his shirt gave a tearing sound.

“Oh, so that’s how it is. Hold him!”

The waiter grabbed Ivan Mironov, and a policeman appeared at once. Having heard, like a man in authority, what the matter was, he at once settled it:

“To the station.”

The coupon the policeman put into his own wallet, and he took Ivan Mironov, together with his horse, to the police station.

## VII

Ivan Mironov spent the night at the police station with drunks and thieves. It was already about noon when they called him in to the precinct inspector. The precinct inspector questioned him and sent him with a policeman to the seller of photographic supplies. Ivan Mironov had remembered the street and the house.

When the policeman called out the gentleman and presented to him the coupon and Ivan Mironov, who insisted that this very gentleman had given him the coupon, Yevgeny Mikhailovich assumed first an astonished expression and then a stern one.

“What’s the matter with you? You must have gone mad. I’m seeing him for the first time.”

“Sir, it’s a sin. We all have to die,” Ivan Mironov said.

“What has got into him? You must have forgotten. You sold the wood to somebody else,” Yevgeny Mikhailovich said. “But wait. I’ll go and ask my wife whether she took any firewood yesterday.”

Yevgeny Mikhailovich went out and immediately called the porter, a handsome, extraordinarily strong and agile dandy, a cheerful fellow, Vasily, and told him that if anyone asked where the latest firewood had come from, he was to say it was from the depot, and that they had not bought wood from peasants.

“Because there’s a peasant here claiming that I gave him a forged coupon. The peasant is dimwitted; God knows what he is saying, but you are a man with some sense. Just say that we buy our wood only at the depot. And this I’ve been meaning for a long time to give you toward a jacket,” Yevgeny Mikhailovich added. And he gave the porter five rubles.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 13 --> Vasily took the money, flashed his eyes at the note, then at Yevgeny Mikhailovich’s face, tossed his hair, and smiled slightly.

“Of course. The common people are dimwitted. Lack of education. Don’t trouble yourself, sir. I know how to put it.”

However much and however tearfully Ivan Mironov begged Yevgeny Mikhailovich to acknowledge his coupon and the porter to confirm his words, both Yevgeny Mikhailovich and the porter stood their ground: they had never taken firewood from carts. And the policeman took Ivan Mironov, accused of forging the coupon, back to the station.

Only on the advice of a drunken scribe who was locked up with him, and by giving a five-ruble note to the precinct inspector, did Ivan Mironov get out from under guard, without the coupon and with seven rubles instead of the twenty-five he had had the day before. Ivan Mironov drank away three of those seven rubles and came home to his wife with a battered face and dead drunk.

His wife was pregnant, near her time, and ill. She began scolding her husband; he pushed her away; she began hitting him. Without answering, he lay down on his belly on the sleeping platform and wept loudly.

Only the next morning did his wife understand what had happened, and, believing her husband, she spent a long time cursing the robber of a gentleman who had cheated her Ivan. And Ivan, having sobered up, remembered what a workman he had been drinking with the day before had advised him, and decided to go to the lawyer and complain.

## VIII

The lawyer took the case, less for the money he might earn than because he believed Ivan and was outraged by the shameless way the peasant had been cheated.

Both sides appeared in court, and Vasily the porter was a witness. In court the same thing was repeated. Ivan Mironov spoke of God, of how we all have to die. Although Yevgeny Mikhailovich was tormented by his awareness of the nastiness and danger of what he was doing, he could no longer change his testimony now, and with an outwardly calm air he went on denying everything.

Vasily the porter received another ten rubles and, smiling, calmly maintained that he had never laid eyes on Ivan Mironov. And when he was brought to take the oath, though inwardly he was timid, outwardly he calmly repeated the words of the oath after the little old priest who had been summoned, swearing on the cross and the holy Gospel that he would tell the whole truth.

The case ended with the judge dismissing Ivan Mironov’s suit and ordering five rubles in court costs to be recovered from him, which Yevgeny Mikhailovich generously forgave him. As he let Ivan Mironov go, the judge gave him a lecture to the effect that in future he should be more careful about bringing accusations against respectable people and should be grateful that the court costs had been forgiven him and that he was not being prosecuted for slander, for which he would have spent some three months in prison.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 14 --> “My humble thanks,” said Ivan Mironov. He left the courtroom shaking his head and sighing.

All this seemed to have ended well for Yevgeny Mikhailovich and the porter Vasily. But it only seemed so. Something had happened that no one saw, but that was more important than everything people had seen.

Vasily was now in his third year of living in town since leaving the village. With every year he gave his father less and less, and he had not sent for his wife, having no need of her. Here in town he had as many women as he wanted, and not the likes of that slattern of his. With every year Vasily forgot more and more of the village law and grew more accustomed to the ways of town. There everything was coarse, gray, poor, and disorderly; here everything was refined, fine, clean, rich, everything in order. And he became more and more convinced that village people lived without understanding, like beasts in the forest, while here were the real people. He read books by good writers, novels, and went to performances at the People’s House. In the village you would not see such things even in a dream. In the village the old men say: live in lawful marriage with your wife, work, do not eat more than you need, do not dress for show; but here clever, educated people—which means they know the real laws—live for their own pleasure. And everything is fine. Until the business with the coupon, Vasily still could not believe that the gentry had no law about how to live. He kept thinking that he did not know their law, but that there was a law. But after the latest business with the coupon, and above all his false oath, which, despite his fear, had brought him nothing bad but, on the contrary, had brought him another ten rubles, Vasily became fully convinced that there were no laws at all and that one must live for one’s own pleasure. So he lived, and so he went on living. At first he only took a cut on the tenants’ purchases, but that was not enough for all his expenses, and wherever he could he began pilfering money and valuables from the tenants’ apartments, and he stole Yevgeny Mikhailovich’s purse. Yevgeny Mikhailovich exposed him, but did not take him to court; instead he dismissed him.

Vasily did not want to go home, and he stayed on in Moscow with his sweetheart, looking for a place. A cheap place turned up, as porter for a shopkeeper. Vasily took it, but the very next month he was caught stealing sacks. The owner did not report him, but beat Vasily and threw him out. After this incident no more places turned up, the money was spent on living, then the clothes began to go the same way, and it ended with nothing left but a torn jacket, trousers, and worn-out shoes. His sweetheart left him. But Vasily did not lose his lively, cheerful spirit, and, having waited for spring, he set out for home on foot.

## IX

Pyotr Nikolayevich Sventitsky was a small, stocky little man in dark glasses (his eyes were diseased; he was threatened with <!-- vol. 36, p. 15 --> complete blindness). As usual, he got up before dawn, drank a glass of tea, put on his short sheepskin coat with its fabric covering and lambskin trim, and went out to see to the estate.

Pyotr Nikolayevich had been a customs official and had acquired eighteen thousand rubles there. About twelve years earlier he had left the service, not entirely of his own free will, and bought the small estate of a young landowner who had squandered his fortune. Pyotr Nikolaich had married while still in the service. His wife was a penniless orphan from an old noble family, a large, full-bodied, handsome woman who had borne him no children. In all his affairs Pyotr Nikolaich was a thorough and persistent man. Knowing nothing about farming (he was the son of a Polish nobleman), he took up farming so well that in ten years the ruined estate of three hundred desyatinas had become a model one. All his buildings, from the house to the granary and the shed over the fire pump, were solid and substantial, roofed with iron and painted in good time. In the implement shed the carts, ard plows, plows, and harrows stood in order. The harness was greased. The horses were not large; nearly all were his own breeding, dun-colored, well fed, strong, and as like one another as could be. The threshing machine worked in a covered threshing barn, fodder was stored in a special shed, and the liquid manure ran into a paved pit. The cows too were of his own breeding, not large, but good milkers. The pigs were English. There was a poultry house, and hens of a particularly good laying breed. In the fruit garden the trees had been coated and new ones planted. Everywhere everything was businesslike, solid, clean, and in good repair. Pyotr Nikolaich took pleasure in his estate and was proud that he had achieved all this not by oppressing the peasants but, on the contrary, by strict justice toward them. Even among the nobility he held a middle position, rather liberal than conservative, and always defended the people against the defenders of serfdom. Be good to them, and they will be good. True, he did not let his workers’ lapses and mistakes pass, sometimes even pushed them himself, and demanded work, but then their quarters and food were the very best, their wages were always paid on time, and on holidays he treated them to vodka.

Stepping carefully over the thawing snow—it was February—Pyotr Nikolaich made his way past the stable for the working horses toward the hut where the workers lived. It was still dark, darker still because of the fog, but light showed in the windows of the workers’ hut. The workers were getting up. He meant to hurry them: according to the day’s assignment they were to go with six horses to the grove for the last loads of firewood.

“What’s this?” he thought, seeing the stable door open.

“Hey! Who’s here?”

No one answered. Pyotr Nikolaich went into the stable.

“Hey! Who’s here?”

Still no one answered. It was dark. It felt soft underfoot, and there was a smell of manure. A pair of young dun horses stood in a stall to the right of the door. Pyotr Nikolaich stretched out his hand: empty. He felt with his foot. Had she lain down? His foot met nothing. “Where could <!-- vol. 36, p. 16 --> they have led her?” he thought. They had not harnessed the horses: all the sleighs were still outside. Pyotr Nikolaich went out of the door and shouted loudly:

“Hey, Stepan!”

Stepan was the senior worker. He was just coming out of their quarters.

“Here!” Stepan answered cheerfully. “Is that you, Pyotr Nikolaich? The boys are coming right away.”

“Why is the stable unlocked?”

“The stable? Couldn’t say. Hey, Proshka! Bring the lantern!”

Proshka came running with the lantern. They went into the stable. Stepan understood at once.

“Thieves have been here, Pyotr Nikolaich. They knocked the lock off.”

“You’re lying?”

“They’ve taken them, the robbers. Mashka’s gone, Yastreb’s gone. Yastreb’s here. Pestriy’s gone. Krasavchik’s gone.”

Three horses were missing. Pyotr Nikolaich said nothing. He frowned and was breathing heavily.

“Oh, if only I could get my hands on him! Who was on watch?”

“Petka. Petka slept through it.”

Pyotr Nikolaich reported it to the police, to the district police officer and the land captain, and sent his own men out. The horses were not found.

“Filthy people!” Pyotr Nikolaich kept saying. “Look what they’ve done! Haven’t I done them good? Just you wait. Robbers, they’re all robbers. From now on I’ll treat you differently.”

## X

But the three dun horses had already reached their destinations. One, Mashka, had been sold to some Gypsies for eighteen rubles, another, Pestriy, had been exchanged with a peasant forty versts away, and Krasavchik had been driven until he could go no farther and then slaughtered. His hide had been sold for three rubles. The one who directed this whole business was Ivan Mironov. He had worked for Pyotr Nikolaich and knew Pyotr Nikolaich’s ways, and he had decided to get his money back. And he arranged the business.

After his misfortune with the forged coupon, Ivan Mironov had gone on drinking for a long time, and he would have drunk away everything if his wife had not hidden the horse collars, his clothes, and everything else that could be drunk away. All through his drinking, Ivan Mironov thought unceasingly not only of the man who had wronged him, but of all the gentry, great and small, who live only by fleecing the likes of us. Once Ivan Mironov was drinking with some peasants from near Podolsk. And on the way the peasants, drunk, told him how they had stolen a peasant’s horses. Ivan Mironov began to scold the horse thieves for wronging a peasant. “It’s a sin,” he said. “To a peasant his little horse is the same as a brother, and you leave him with nothing. If you’re going to take horses, take them from the gentry. Those dogs deserve it.” One thing led to another as they talked, and the Podolsk peasants said that stealing horses from the gentry

<!-- vol. 36, p. 17 -->

was tricky. You had to know the ways in, and without someone on the inside it could not be done. Then Ivan Mironov remembered Sventitsky, for whom he had worked as a hired man, remembered that when settling up Sventitsky had held back a ruble and a half for a broken kingpin, and remembered too the little dun horses he had worked with.

Ivan Mironov went over to Sventitsky’s as if to hire on, but only in order to look things over and find everything out. And having found out everything—that there was no watchman, that the horses were in loose boxes, in the stable—he brought in the thieves and did the whole job.

After dividing the takings with the Podolsk peasants, Ivan Mironov came home with five rubles. There was nothing to do at home: he had no horse. And from then on Ivan Mironov began associating with horse thieves and Gypsies.

## XI

Pyotr Nikolaich Sventitsky did everything he could to find the thief. The thing could not have been done without one of his own people. And so he began to suspect his own people and, having found out from the workers who had not spent that night at home, he learned that the one who had not was Proshka Nikolayev. Proshka was a young fellow, a soldier just back from military service, a handsome, deft fellow, whom Pyotr Nikolaich took on his drives in place of a coachman. The district police officer was a friend of Pyotr Nikolaich; he knew the district police chief, and the marshal of the nobility, and the land captain, and the examining magistrate. All these persons used to come to him on his name day and knew his tasty fruit liqueurs and salted mushrooms—porcini, honey mushrooms, and milk mushrooms. They all felt sorry for him and tried to help him.

“There you are, and you defend the peasants,” said the district police officer. “I was right when I said they’re worse than beasts. Without the whip and the stick you can’t do a thing with them. So you say it’s Proshka, the one who drives with you as coachman?”

“Yes, him.”

“Bring him here.”

They called Proshka in and began to question him:

“Where were you?”

Proshka shook back his hair, flashed his eyes.

“At home.”

“How can it be at home, when all the workers state that you weren’t there?”

“As you will.”

“It isn’t a matter of my will. And where were you?”

“At home.”

“Very well, then. Sotsky, take him to the station.”

“As you will.”

So Proshka never did say where he had been, and he did not say it because he had spent the night with his sweetheart, with Parasha, and had promised not to give her away, and he did not give her away. There was no evidence. And Proshka was let go. But Pyotr Nikolaich was sure that the whole thing was Prokofy’s doing, and he came to hate <!-- vol. 36, p. 18 --> him. Once, having taken Prokofy as his coachman, Pyotr Nikolaich sent him ahead to a relay stop. Proshka, as he always did, took two measures of oats at the coaching inn. He fed the horses one and a half measures and drank away the other half. Pyotr Nikolaich found out and lodged a complaint with the justice of the peace. The justice of the peace sentenced Proshka to three months in prison. Prokofy was proud. He thought himself above other people and was proud of himself. Prison humiliated him. He could no longer be proud in front of people, and he lost heart at once.

Proshka came home from prison embittered not so much against Pyotr Nikolaich as against the whole world.

Prokofy, as everyone said, went downhill after prison, began to be lazy about work, began to drink, and was soon caught stealing clothes from a townswoman and landed in prison again.

Of the horses, Pyotr Nikolaich learned only that the hide of a dun gelding had been found, which Pyotr Nikolaich recognized as Krasavchik’s hide. And this impunity of the thieves irritated Pyotr Nikolaich still more. He could not now see peasants or talk about them without malice, and wherever he could he tried to put the squeeze on them.

## XII

Once he had got rid of the coupon, Yevgeny Mikhailovich stopped thinking about it. But his wife, Maria Vasilyevna, could not forgive herself for having been taken in, nor her husband for the cruel words he had said to her, nor, above all, those two young scoundrels who had deceived her so cleverly.

From the very day she was deceived, she looked closely at all the schoolboys. Once she met Makhin but did not recognize him, because when he saw her he pulled such a face that it completely changed his features. But Mitya Smokovnikov she recognized at once, when she ran into him nose to nose on the sidewalk some two weeks after the incident. She let him pass and, turning around, followed after him. Having walked as far as his apartment and found out whose son he was, she went to the gymnasium the next day. In the front hall she met the religion teacher, Mikhail Vvedensky. He asked what she wanted. She said that she wished to see the headmaster.

“The headmaster is not here; he is unwell. Perhaps I can attend to it, or pass it on to him?”

Maria Vasilyevna decided to tell the religion teacher everything.

The religion teacher Vvedensky was a widower, a graduate of the Theological Academy, and a very proud man. Back in the previous year he had met Smokovnikov’s father at a gathering and had clashed with him in a conversation about faith, in which Smokovnikov had beaten him on every point and made a laughingstock of him. He had decided to pay particular attention to the son and, finding in him the same indifference to the Law of God as in his unbelieving father, he had begun to persecute him and had even failed him in an examination.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 19 --> When he learned from Maria Vasilyevna what young Smokovnikov had done, Vvedensky could not help feeling pleasure, finding in this case a confirmation of his suppositions about the immorality of people deprived of the guidance of the church. And he decided to make use of this case, as he tried to assure himself, to show the danger that threatens all who fall away from the church. In the depths of his soul, though, it was to take revenge on the proud and self-assured atheist.

“Yes, it is very sad, very sad,” said Father Mikhail Vvedensky, stroking with his hand the smooth sides of his pectoral cross. “I am very glad that you have handed the matter over to me; as a servant of the church, I will try not to leave the young man without guidance, but I will also try to make the admonition as gentle as possible.”

“Yes, I will do as befits my calling,” Father Mikhail said to himself, thinking that, having completely forgotten the father’s ill will toward him, he had in view only the good and the salvation of the youth.

The next day, at the lesson in the Law of God, Father Mikhail told the pupils the whole episode of the forged coupon and said that a schoolboy had done it.

“It is a bad, shameful act,” he said, “but denial is still worse. If, which I do not believe, one of you did this, it is better for him to repent than to hide.”

As he said this, Father Mikhail looked intently at Mitya Smokovnikov. The schoolboys, following his gaze, also turned to look at Smokovnikov. Mitya reddened, sweated, at last burst into tears and ran out of the classroom.

Mitya’s mother, learning of this, wormed the whole truth out of her son and ran to the photographic supplies shop. She paid the proprietress twelve rubles fifty kopeks and persuaded her to conceal the schoolboy’s name. Her son she told to deny everything and on no account to confess to his father.

And indeed, when Fyodor Mikhailovich learned what had happened at the gymnasium, he called his son in, and his son denied everything. Fyodor Mikhailovich drove to the headmaster and, having told him the whole affair, said that the religion teacher’s conduct was reprehensible in the highest degree and that he would not leave it at that. The headmaster invited the priest, and a heated exchange took place between him and Fyodor Mikhailovich.

“A stupid woman fastened an accusation on my son, then retracted her statement herself, and you could find nothing better than to slander an honest, truthful boy.”

“I slandered no one, and I will not allow you to speak to me like that. You forget my office.”

“I spit on your office.”

“Your perverse notions,” said the religion teacher, his chin trembling so that his sparse beard shook, “are known to the whole town.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 20 --> “Gentlemen, Father,” the headmaster tried to calm the disputants. But it was impossible to calm them.

“It is the duty of my office to concern myself with religious and moral education.”

“Stop pretending. Don’t I know that you believe in nothing at all?”

“I consider it beneath me to speak with such a gentleman as you,” said Father Mikhail. He was offended by Smokovnikov’s last words especially because he knew that they were just. He had gone through the full course of the Theological Academy and therefore had long since ceased to believe in what he professed and preached, and believed only that all people must force themselves to believe in what he forced himself to believe.

Smokovnikov was not so much outraged by the religion teacher’s action as he found it a good illustration of the clerical influence that is beginning to show itself among us, and he told everyone about the incident.

Father Vvedensky, for his part, seeing manifestations of entrenched nihilism and atheism not only in the young but also in the old generation, became more and more convinced of the necessity of fighting it. The more he condemned the unbelief of Smokovnikov and those like him, the more convinced he became of the firmness and immovability of his own faith, and the less need he felt to test it or to bring it into agreement with his life. His faith, acknowledged by the whole world around him, was for him the chief weapon in the struggle against those who denied it.

These thoughts, called up in him by the clash with Smokovnikov, together with the unpleasantness at the gymnasium that came of that clash—namely, a reprimand, a rebuke received from his superiors—made him take a decision that had long beckoned to him, ever since his wife’s death. The decision was to take monastic vows and to choose the very career that some of his fellow students from the Academy had followed, one of whom was already a bishop, and another an archimandrite filling a bishop’s vacancy.

Toward the end of the school year Vvedensky left the gymnasium, was tonsured a monk under the name of Misail, and very soon received the post of rector of a seminary in a Volga town.

## XIII

Meanwhile Vasily the porter was walking south along the high road.

By day he walked, and for the night the desyatsky took him to the lodging whose turn it was. Everywhere they gave him bread, and sometimes they even sat him down at the table for supper. In one village of Oryol Province, where he spent the night, he was told that a merchant who had leased a landowner’s orchard was looking for good young fellows as watchmen. Vasily was tired of begging, but he did not want to go home, and he went to the orchard merchant and hired himself out as a watchman for five rubles a month.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 21 --> Life in the hut, especially after the Grushovka apples began to ripen and the watchmen brought huge bundles of fresh straw, straight from the threshing machine, from the master’s threshing floor, was very pleasant for Vasily. Lie all day on the fresh, fragrant straw beside the heaps of windfall summer and winter apples, more fragrant even than the straw, keep an eye out in case boys have got in somewhere after apples, whistle and sing songs. And Vasily was a master at singing songs. And he had a good voice. Women and girls would come from the village for apples. Vasily would joke with them and give them, according to which one took his fancy, more or fewer apples for eggs or a few kopecks—and then lie down again; only go off for breakfast, dinner, supper.

Vasily had only one shirt, of pink cotton print, and even that was full of holes; there was nothing on his feet; but his body was strong and healthy, and when the pot of porridge was taken off the fire, Vasily ate enough for three, so that the old watchman could only marvel at him. At night Vasily did not sleep, and either whistled or called out now and then, and could see far in the darkness like a cat. Once some big lads from the village got in to shake down apples. Vasily crept up and threw himself on them; they meant to fight him off, but he flung them all aside, and one he brought to the hut and handed over to the owner.

Vasily’s first hut was in the far orchard, and his second hut, when the Grushovka was over, was forty paces from the manor house. And in this hut Vasily had an even merrier time. All day long Vasily saw how the gentlefolk and the young ladies played, went out driving, walked about, and in the evenings and at night played the piano, the violin, sang, danced. He saw how the young ladies sat on the windowsills with students and caressed one another, and then went off by themselves to walk in the dark lime avenues, where the moonlight came through only in stripes and patches. He saw servants running about with food and drink, and how the cooks, laundresses, stewards, gardeners, coachmen—all worked only to feed the gentlefolk, give them drink, and amuse them. Sometimes the young gentlefolk came to his hut too, and he picked out and handed them the best apples, juicy and red-sided, and the young ladies bit into them right there, crunching with their teeth, and praised them and said something—Vasily understood that it was about him—in French, and made him sing.

And Vasily admired this life, remembering his Moscow life, and the thought that it all came down to money sank deeper and deeper into his head.

And Vasily began to think more and more about how he might manage to grab a lot of money all at once. He began to recall how he had helped himself before, and decided that this was not the way to do it, that one must not, as before, snatch whatever lies about unguarded, but think it out in advance, find everything out, and do it cleanly, so as to leave no loose ends. By the Nativity of the Mother of God the last Antonovka apples had been picked. The owner had done well out of it, and he settled up with all the watchmen and with Vasily and rewarded them.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 22 --> Vasily got himself dressed—the young master had given him a jacket and a hat—and did not go home—it made him very sick to think of the coarse peasant life—but went back to the town with the drinking soldiers who had guarded the orchard with him. In the town he decided to break into and rob, at night, the very shop whose owner he had lived with, the one who had beaten him and driven him out without his pay. He knew all the ways in and where the money was; he set the soldier to keep watch, and he himself broke a window from the yard, climbed through, and took out all the money. The job was done skillfully, and no traces were found. He took out three hundred seventy rubles. Vasily gave a hundred rubles to his companion, and with the rest went off to another town and there caroused with male and female companions.

## XIV

Meanwhile Ivan Mironov had become a skillful, bold, and successful horse thief. Afimya, his wife, who had once scolded him for his bad doings, as she put it, was now pleased and proud of her husband, proud that he had a cloth-covered sheepskin coat and that she herself had a small shawl and a new fur coat.

Everyone in the village and the surrounding country knew that no horse theft took place without him, but people were afraid to testify against him, and whenever suspicion did fall on him, he came out clean and in the right. His latest theft had been from the night pasture at Kolotovka. When he could, Ivan Mironov picked whom to steal from, and he liked best to take from landowners and merchants. But with landowners and merchants it was harder. And so, when the landowners’ and merchants’ horses did not come to hand, he took from peasants too. That was how he had grabbed whatever horses came along from the night pasture at Kolotovka. It was not he himself who did the job, but a clever young fellow, Gerasim, whom he had put up to it. The peasants did not miss the horses until dawn, and then they rushed off to search the roads. The horses, though, were standing in a ravine in the Crown forest. Ivan Mironov meant to keep them there until the next night, and at night to make a dash of forty versts to an innkeeper he knew. Ivan Mironov went to see Gerasim in the forest, brought him some pie and vodka, and set off home by a forest path where he hoped to meet nobody. To his misfortune, he ran into a soldier who served as a forest guard.

“Been looking for mushrooms?” said the soldier.

“No, there aren’t any this year,” answered Ivan Mironov, pointing to the basket he had taken along just in case.

“Yes, it isn’t a mushroom summer this year,” the soldier said, “maybe they’ll come up during the fast,” and he walked on.

The soldier understood that something was not right here. Ivan Mironov had no reason to be walking about the Crown forest early in the morning. The soldier went back and began searching through the forest. Near the ravine he heard a horse snorting and went quietly toward the place the sound had come from. In the ravine the ground had been trampled, and there was horse dung.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 23 --> Farther on sat Gerasim, eating something, and two horses stood tied to a tree.

The soldier ran to the village and fetched the village elder, the sotsky, and two attesting witnesses. They came up from three sides to the place where Gerasim was and seized him. Geraska did not try to deny anything and, being drunk, confessed everything at once. He told how Ivan Mironov had got him drunk and put him up to it, and how Ivan had promised to come to the forest for the horses that day. The peasants left the horses and Gerasim in the forest and lay in ambush themselves, waiting for Ivan Mironov. When it grew dark, a whistle was heard. Gerasim answered. Ivan Mironov had barely begun coming down the hill when they fell on him and took him to the village. The next morning a crowd gathered in front of the village elder’s house.

Ivan Mironov was brought out and they began to question him. The first to question him was Stepan Pelageyushkin, a tall, slightly stooped, long-armed peasant with an aquiline nose and a gloomy expression. Stepan was a solitary peasant who had done his military service. He had only just separated from his father and begun to set himself up when his horse was stolen. After working a year in the mines, Stepan again got himself two horses. Both were stolen.

“Tell me where my horses are,” Stepan began, looking gloomily now at the ground, now into Ivan’s face, and turning pale with fury.

Ivan Mironov denied everything. Then Stepan struck him in the face and smashed his nose, and blood began to flow from it.

“Talk, or I’ll kill you!”

Ivan Mironov kept silent, bowing his head. Stepan struck with his long arm once, then again. Ivan still kept silent, only jerking his head back now this way, now that.

“All of you, beat him!” shouted the village elder.

And they all began to beat him. Ivan Mironov fell without a sound and shouted:

“Barbarians, devils, beat me to death. I’m not afraid of you.”

Then Stepan seized a stone from a stockpiled stack and smashed Ivan Mironov’s head.

## XV

The murderers of Ivan Mironov were tried. Stepan Pelageyushkin was among these murderers. He was charged more severely than the others, because everyone testified that he had smashed Ivan Mironov’s head with a stone. At the trial Stepan concealed nothing. He explained that when his last pair of horses had been stolen, he had reported it to the district police, and the trail could have been found among the Gypsies, but the district police officer had not even let him come before him and had made no search at all.

“What are we to do with a man like that? He ruined us.”

“Why did the others not hit him, but you did?” said the prosecutor.

“That isn’t true. Everybody hit him. The village decided to kill him. I only finished him off. Why torment him for nothing?”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 24 --> The judges were struck by the expression of complete calm with which Stepan told about his deed and about how they had beaten Ivan Mironov and how he had finished him off.

Stepan truly saw nothing terrible in this killing. In the service he had had to execute a soldier by shooting, and just as then, so too at the killing of Ivan Mironov, he had seen nothing terrible. They killed him, and that’s that. Today him, tomorrow me.

Stepan was sentenced lightly, to one year in prison. His peasant clothes were taken off him and put away under a number in the storehouse, and a prisoner’s robe and prison shoes were put on him.

Stepan had never had any respect for those in authority, but now he became fully convinced that all those in authority, all the gentlefolk, all of them, except the Tsar, who alone pitied the people and was just, all of them were robbers sucking the blood out of the people. The stories of the exiles and convicts with whom he became friendly in prison confirmed this view. One was being sent to hard labor for exposing the thieving of the authorities, another for striking an official when the official began wrongfully making an inventory of peasant property, a third for counterfeiting banknotes. Gentlefolk and merchants, whatever they did, got away with everything, but a poor peasant was sent off to the prisons for anything and everything, to feed the lice.

His wife visited him in prison. Life without him was hard enough for her already, and then her house burned down too, and she was completely ruined and took to begging with the children. His wife’s misfortunes embittered Stepan still more. In prison too he was vicious with everyone, and once he nearly hacked the cook to death with an axe, for which another year was added to his term. During that year he learned that his wife had died and that he no longer had a home…

When Stepan’s term was over, they called him to the storehouse, took down from a shelf the clothes he had come in, and gave them to him.

“So where am I to go now?” he said to the storekeeper as he dressed.

“Home, of course.”

“There’s no home. Looks as if I’ll have to take to the road. Rob people.”

“If you rob, you’ll land back with us.”

“Well, that’s as it turns out.”

And Stepan left. All the same, he headed toward home. There was nowhere else to go.

Before reaching home, he stopped for the night at an inn with a tavern that he knew.

The inn was kept by a fat tradesman from Vladimir Province. He knew Stepan. And he knew that Stepan had landed in prison through misfortune. And he let Stepan stay the night.

This rich tradesman had taken away a neighboring peasant’s wife and lived with her as with a servant and a wife.

Stepan knew the whole story: how the tradesman had wronged the peasant, how that nasty little woman had left her husband and had now grown fat and sat perspiring over her tea, and out of charity had treated Stepan to tea too. There were no travelers. Stepan was given a place for the night in the kitchen.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 25 --> Matryona cleared everything away and went into the front room. Stepan lay down on top of the stove but could not sleep, and kept crackling the splinters that were drying on the stove. He could not get the tradesman’s fat belly out of his head, sticking out from under the belt of his much-washed, faded cotton shirt. The thought kept coming into his head to slash that belly with a knife and let the fat out. And the little woman too. At one moment he told himself, “Well, to hell with them, I’ll leave tomorrow,” at another he remembered Ivan Mironov and again thought about the tradesman’s belly and Matryona’s white, perspiring throat. If he was going to kill, he would kill them both. The second cock crowed. If he meant to do it, he had to do it now, or else it would be light. He had noticed the knife that evening, and the axe. He climbed down from the stove, took the axe and the knife, and went out of the kitchen. Just as he came out, the latch clicked behind the door. The tradesman came out through the door. Stepan did not do it the way he had meant to. It did not come to the knife; he swung the axe and split the man’s head. The tradesman fell against the doorpost and to the ground.

Stepan went into the front room. Matryona jumped up and stood by the bed in nothing but her shift. Stepan killed her too with the same axe.

Then he lit a candle, took the money out of the desk, and left.

## XVI

In a district town, in his own house standing apart from the other buildings, there lived an old man, a former official and a drunkard, with his two daughters and his son-in-law. The married daughter drank too and led a bad life, while the elder, the widow Maria Semyonovna, a wrinkled, thin woman of fifty, alone supported them all: she had a pension of two hundred fifty rubles. The whole family lived on that money. And only Maria Semyonovna worked in the house. She looked after her weak, drunken old father and her sister’s child, and cooked, and washed. And, as always happens, it was onto her that every task that needed doing was piled, and she was the one all three of them scolded, and the son-in-law even beat her when he was drunk. She bore everything silently and meekly, and, again as always happens, the more work she had, the more she managed to do. She also helped the poor, denying herself and giving away her clothes, and helped care for the sick.

Once a lame, legless village tailor was working at Maria Semyonovna’s. He was altering a coat for the old man and covering a short sheepskin coat with cloth for Maria Semyonovna, for going to market in winter.

The lame tailor was an intelligent and observant man; his trade had shown him many different people, and because of his lameness he was always sitting and therefore inclined to think. After living a week at Maria Semyonovna’s, he could not marvel enough at her life. One day she came to him in the kitchen, where he was sewing, to wash out some towels, and got to talking with him about his life, how his brother had wronged him and how he had separated from him.

“I thought it would be better, but it’s all the same: want.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 26 --> “Better not to change,” said Maria Semyonovna. “Go on living the way you live.”

“That’s just what I marvel at in you, Maria Semyonovna: how you, all alone, all by yourself, bustle about in every direction for people. And as far as I can see, you get little good from them.”

Maria Semyonovna said nothing.

“Must be you got it from books that there’ll be a reward for it in the next world.”

“About that we don’t know,” said Maria Semyonovna, “only it is better to live this way.”

“And does it say that in books?”

“It says it in books too,” she said. And she read him the Sermon on the Mount from the Gospel. The tailor fell to thinking. And when he had settled up and gone home, he kept thinking about what he had seen at Maria Semyonovna’s, and what she had said and read to him.

## XVII

Pyotr Nikolaich changed toward the people, and the people changed toward him. Before a year had passed, they cut down twenty-seven oaks and burned the uninsured threshing barn and threshing floor. Pyotr Nikolaich decided that it was impossible to live with the people here.

At that same time the Liventsovs were looking for a manager for their estates, and the marshal of the nobility recommended Pyotr Nikolaich as the best farmer in the district. The Liventsov estates were immense and brought in no income at all, and the peasants made use of everything. Pyotr Nikolaich undertook to put everything in order and, having leased out his own estate, moved with his wife to a distant province on the Volga.

Pyotr Nikolaich had always loved order and legality, and now all the more he could not allow this wild, coarse people to take possession, contrary to the law, of property that did not belong to them. He was glad of the chance to teach them a lesson and set to work sternly. He had one peasant sentenced to prison for stealing timber, and another he beat with his own hands because the man had not pulled off the road and had not taken off his cap. About the meadows that were in dispute and that the peasants considered theirs, Pyotr Nikolaich announced to the peasants that if they let their cattle out onto them, he would impound the cattle.

Spring came, and the peasants, as they had done in previous years, let their cattle out onto the manor meadows. Pyotr Nikolaich gathered all the workers and ordered them to drive the cattle into the manor yard. The men were away plowing, and so the workers, despite the cries of the women, drove the cattle in. When they came back from work, the men gathered together and came to the manor yard to demand the cattle. Pyotr Nikolaich came out to them with a gun over his shoulder (he had just returned from riding round the estate) and announced to them that he would give back the cattle only on payment of fifty kopeks for each head of cattle and ten for each sheep.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 27 --> The men began shouting that the meadows were theirs, that both their fathers and their grandfathers had owned them, and that there was no such right as taking other people’s cattle.

“Give back the cattle, or things will go badly,” said an old man, advancing on Pyotr Nikolaich.

“What will go badly?” shouted Pyotr Nikolaich, all pale, stepping up to the old man.

“Give them back and keep from sin. You sponger.”

“What?” shouted Pyotr Nikolaich, and he struck the old man in the face.

“You’ve no right to hit people. Boys, take the cattle by force.”

The crowd pressed forward. Pyotr Nikolaich wanted to leave, but they would not let him. He began to force his way through. The gun went off and killed one of the peasants. A fierce brawl broke out. Pyotr Nikolaich was trampled down. And five minutes later they dragged his mutilated body into a ravine.

A military court was convened for the killers, and two of them were sentenced to be hanged.

## XVIII

In the village the tailor came from, five rich peasants rented from a landowner, for eleven hundred rubles, one hundred and five desyatinas of arable land, black as tar and rich, and let it out to their fellow peasants, some at eighteen rubles, some at fifteen. No land went for less than twelve. So the profit was good. The buyers themselves each took five desyatinas for themselves, and this land came to them for nothing. A partner of these peasants died, and they invited the lame tailor to come in with them as a partner.

When the tenants began to divide up the land, the tailor would not drink vodka, and when the talk turned to how much land to give to whom, the tailor said that everyone should be assessed equally, that they should not take anything extra from the tenants, but only what it came to.

“What do you mean?”

“Why, are we heathens, or what? That’s all very well for the gentry, but we’re peasants. It has to be done God’s way. Such is Christ’s law.”

“Then where is there such a law?”

“Why, in the book, in the Gospel. Come on Sunday, and I’ll read some and we’ll talk it over.”

And on Sunday not all of them came, but three came to the tailor, and he began to read to them.

He read five chapters of Matthew, and they began to talk it over. Everyone listened, but only one, Ivan Chuyev, accepted it. And he accepted it in such a way that he began to live God’s way in everything. And in his family they began to live that way too. He gave up the extra land and took only his own share.

And people began coming to the tailor and to Ivan, and began to understand, and understood, and gave up smoking, drinking, and swearing with foul words, and began to help one another. And they stopped going to church <!-- vol. 36, p. 28 --> and took their icons to the priest. And there came to be seventeen such households. Sixty-five souls in all. And the priest took fright and informed the bishop. The bishop thought about what to do and decided to send to the village Archimandrite Misail, who had been the religion teacher at the gymnasium.

## XIX

The bishop sat Misail down beside him and began to talk about the new things that had turned up in his diocese.

“It all comes from spiritual weakness and from ignorance. You are a learned man. I am relying on you. Go there, call them together, and explain it before the people.”

“If Your Grace gives his blessing, I will do my best,” said Father Misail. He was glad of this commission. Everything in which he could show that he believed gladdened him. And in converting others he most of all convinced himself that he believed.

“Do your best. I suffer greatly for my flock,” said the bishop, unhurriedly taking with his white, plump hands the glass of tea that the servant was handing him.

He turned to the servant. “What’s this, only the one jam? Bring another.” He went on with what he was saying to Misail: “It pains me very, very much.”

Misail was glad to make himself known. But, being a man of small means, he asked for money for the expenses of the journey and, fearing opposition from the coarse people, also asked for an order from the governor that the local police should assist him in case of need.

The bishop arranged everything for him, and Misail, having packed, with the help of his servant and his cook, a traveling provision chest and the food that one had to lay in when setting off for an out-of-the-way place, set out for his destination. Setting off on this official assignment, Misail experienced the pleasant feeling of being aware of the importance of his ministry and, along with it, of the end of all doubts about his faith, and, on the contrary, of complete certainty of its truth.

His thoughts were directed not at the essence of the faith—that was accepted as an axiom—but at refuting the objections that were made concerning its outward forms.

## XX

The village priest and his wife received Misail with great honor, and on the day after his arrival they gathered the people in the church. Misail, in a new silk cassock, with a pectoral cross and combed hair, went up onto the ambo; beside him stood the priest, a little way off the sextons and the singers, and at the side doors the police. The sectarians came too—in greasy, rough sheepskin coats.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 29 --> After the prayer service Misail preached a sermon, urging those who had fallen away to return to the bosom of mother church, threatening them with the torments of hell, and promising complete forgiveness to those who repented.

The sectarians were silent. When people began to question them, they answered.

Asked why they had fallen away, they answered that in the church wooden gods made by hands are worshipped, and that not only is this not indicated in Scripture, but the opposite is indicated in the prophecies. When Misail asked Chuyev whether it was true that they called the holy icons boards, Chuyev answered: “Why, turn over any icon you like, and you’ll see for yourself.” When they were asked why they did not recognize the priesthood, they answered that Scripture says, “Freely you received, and freely give,” but the priests hand out their grace only for money. To all Misail’s attempts to rest his case on holy Scripture the tailor and Ivan objected calmly but firmly, pointing to Scripture, of which they had a firm knowledge. Misail grew angry and threatened them with the secular power. To this the sectarians said that it is said: “They persecuted me—and they will persecute you.”

It came to nothing, and everything would have gone well, but the next day, at the liturgy, Misail preached a sermon on the perniciousness of those who lead others astray and on how they deserved every punishment. And among the people coming out of the church there began to be talk that it would be a good thing to teach the godless a lesson, so that they would not trouble the people. And that day, while Misail was having a bite of salmon and whitefish with the dean and an inspector who had come from town, a brawl broke out in the village. The Orthodox crowded around Chuyev’s hut and waited for them to come out, so as to beat them. There were about twenty sectarians, men and women. Misail’s sermon, and now the gathering of the Orthodox and their threatening talk, aroused in the sectarians an evil feeling that had not been there before. Evening came on; it was time for the women to milk the cows, but the Orthodox kept standing and waiting, and a lad who had started to come out they beat and drove back into the hut. They talked over what to do and did not agree.

The tailor said: one must endure and not defend oneself. But Chuyev said that if one endured like that, they would kill everyone. And, seizing a poker, he went out into the street. The Orthodox rushed at him.

“Come on, now, by the law of Moses,” he shouted. And he began to beat the Orthodox and knocked out one man’s eye; the rest jumped out of the hut and went back to their homes.

Chuyev was tried, and for leading others astray and for blasphemy he was sentenced to exile.

Father Misail, for his part, was given an award and was made an archimandrite.

## XXI

Two years before, there had come to St. Petersburg from the Don Cossack region, to attend the courses, a healthy, Eastern-looking, beautiful <!-- vol. 36, p. 30 --> girl, Turchaninova. In St. Petersburg this girl met the student Tyurin, the son of a land captain from Simbirsk Province, and fell in love with him; but she loved him not with an ordinary woman’s love, with the desire to become his wife and the mother of his children, but with a comrade’s love, fed chiefly by the same indignation and hatred, directed not only at the existing order but also at the people who represented it, and by the consciousness of their intellectual, educational, and moral superiority to those people.

She was capable at her studies, and easily remembered lectures and passed examinations, and in addition she devoured the newest books in enormous quantities. She was sure that her calling, too, lay not in bearing and raising children—she even looked on such a calling with disgust and contempt—but in destroying the existing order that bound the best powers of the people and in showing people the new way of life that was being shown to her by the newest European writers. Full-bodied, fair-skinned, rosy, beautiful, with brilliant black eyes and a big black braid, she aroused feelings in men which she did not want, and indeed was unable, to return—so wholly was she absorbed in her activity of agitation and talk. But all the same it was pleasant to her that she aroused these feelings, and so, though she did not dress up, she did not neglect her appearance. It was pleasant to her that she was attractive, and that in fact she could show how she despised what was so valued by other women. In her views on the means of struggle against the existing order she went farther than most of her comrades and her friend Tyurin, and she allowed that in the struggle all means are good and may be used, up to and including murder. And yet this same revolutionary, Katya Turchaninova, was at heart a very kind and self-sacrificing woman, who always spontaneously preferred another’s advantage, pleasure, and well-being to her own advantage, pleasure, and well-being, and who was always truly glad of a chance to do something pleasant for someone—a child, an old man, an animal.

Turchaninova spent the summer in a district town on the Volga, at the home of her friend, a village schoolteacher. Tyurin, too, was living with his father in the same district. The three of them, together with the district doctor, met often, exchanged books, argued, and were indignant. The Tyurins’ estate was next to the Liventsovs’ estate where Pyotr Nikolaich had gone to work as manager. As soon as Pyotr Nikolaich arrived and began putting things in order, young Tyurin, seeing the independent spirit of the Liventsov peasants and their firm intention to defend their rights, took an interest in them, and he often went to the village and talked with the peasants, expounding among them the theory of socialism in general and of the nationalization of land in particular.

When the murder of Pyotr Nikolaich took place and the court arrived, the circle of revolutionaries in the district town had strong <!-- vol. 36, p. 31 --> cause for indignation at the court and boldly expressed it. In court it came out that Tyurin had been going to the village and talking with the peasants. Tyurin’s house was searched, several revolutionary pamphlets were found, and the student was arrested and taken to St. Petersburg.

Turchaninova followed him and went to the prison for a visit, but she was not let in on an ordinary day and was admitted only on the general visiting day, when she saw Tyurin through two rows of bars. This visit increased her indignation still more. But what brought her indignation to its utmost limit was her talk with a handsome gendarme officer, who was evidently ready to show leniency if she accepted his proposals. This brought her to the last degree of outrage and malice against all persons in authority. She went to the chief of police to complain. The chief of police told her the same thing the gendarme had told her: that they could do nothing, that there was an order from the minister about it. She submitted a written petition to the minister asking for a visit; she was refused. Then she resolved on a desperate act and bought a revolver.

## XXII

The minister was receiving at his usual hour. He went round three petitioners, received a governor, and came up to a black-eyed, beautiful young woman in black, who stood holding a paper in her left hand. A caressing, lustful gleam lit up in the minister’s eyes at the sight of the beautiful petitioner, but, remembering his position, the minister put on a serious face.

“What can I do for you?” he said, coming up to her.

Without answering, she quickly drew her hand with the revolver from under her cape and, pointing it at the minister’s chest, fired, but missed.

The minister tried to seize her hand; she drew back and fired a second time. The minister ran off. She was seized. She was trembling and could not speak. And suddenly she burst out laughing hysterically. The minister had not even been wounded.

It was Turchaninova. She was put in the House of Preliminary Detention. The minister, for his part, having received congratulations and expressions of sympathy from the most highly placed persons and even from the Sovereign himself, appointed a commission to investigate the conspiracy of which this attempt had been the consequence.

There was, of course, no conspiracy at all; but the officials of the secret and regular police diligently set about tracing all the threads of the nonexistent conspiracy and conscientiously earned their salaries and upkeep: getting up early in the morning, in the dark, they conducted search after search, made lists of papers, books, read diaries, private letters, made extracts from them on fine paper in a fine hand, and many times interrogated <!-- vol. 36, p. 32 --> Turchaninova and arranged confrontations for her, wishing to find out from her the names of her accomplices.

At heart the minister was a kind man, and he very much pitied this healthy, beautiful Cossack girl, but he told himself that heavy state duties rested upon him, which he fulfilled, however hard they were for him. And when a former companion of his, a chamberlain acquainted with the Tyurins, met him at a court ball and began pleading with him for Tyurin and Turchaninova, the minister shrugged his shoulders, so that the red ribbon on his white waistcoat wrinkled, and said:

“Je ne demanderais pas mieux que de lâcher cette pauvre fillete, mais vous savez — le devoir.”[^1]

Meanwhile Turchaninova sat in the House of Preliminary Detention, and sometimes she calmly tapped messages to her comrades through the wall and read the books she was given, while at other times she suddenly fell into despair and fury, beat herself against the walls, shrieked, and laughed.

## XXIII

One day Maria Semyonovna collected her pension at the treasury, and on her way back she met a schoolteacher she knew.

“Well, Maria Semyonovna, got the treasury money?” he shouted to her from the other side of the street.

“I have,” answered Maria Semyonovna, “just enough to plug the holes.”

“Well, it’s a lot of money; you’ll plug the holes and there’ll be some left over,” said the teacher, and, saying goodbye, walked on.

“Goodbye,” said Maria Semyonovna. Looking at the teacher, she ran straight into a tall man with very long arms and a stern face.

But as she approached her house, she was surprised to see the same long-armed man again. Having seen her go into the house, he stood a while, turned, and went away.

At first Maria Semyonovna felt frightened, then sad. But when she went into the house and handed out the treats both to the old man and to her little scrofulous nephew Fedya, and petted Trezorka, who was squealing with joy, she felt good again, and, having given the money to her father, she set to the work that never ran out for her.

The man she had run into was Stepan.

From the inn where Stepan had killed the innkeeper he did not go into town. And, strange to say, the memory of the murder of the innkeeper was not only not unpleasant to him, but he recalled it several times a day. He liked to think that he <!-- vol. 36, p. 33 --> could do it so cleanly and skillfully that no one would find out or prevent him from doing it again, and to others as well. Sitting in a tavern over tea and vodka, he studied people always from the same point of view: how one could kill them. He went to spend the night with a man from his home region, a heavy-haul carter. The carter was not home. Stepan said he would wait, and sat talking with the man’s wife. Then, when she turned toward the stove, it came into his head to kill her. He was surprised, shook his head at himself, then drew the knife from his boot and, throwing her down, cut her throat. The children began screaming; he killed them too and left the town without staying the night. Outside the town, in a village, he went into a tavern and slept his fill there.

The next day he came back to the district town and in the street heard the conversation between Maria Semyonovna and the teacher. Her look frightened him, but all the same he decided to get into her house and take the money she had received. That night he broke the lock and went into the main room. The first to hear him was the younger, married daughter. She screamed. Stepan killed her at once with the knife. The son-in-law woke and grappled with him. He seized Stepan by the throat and struggled with him for a long time, but Stepan was stronger. And, having finished with the son-in-law, Stepan, agitated and excited by the struggle, went behind the partition. Behind the partition Maria Semyonovna was lying in bed, and, raising herself, she looked at Stepan with frightened, gentle eyes and crossed herself. Her look frightened Stepan again. He lowered his eyes.

“Where’s the money?” he said, without raising his eyes.

She was silent.

“Where’s the money?” said Stepan, showing her the knife.

“What are you doing? How can you do such a thing?” she said.

“Evidently I can.”

Stepan came up to her, getting ready to seize her by the hands so that she would not hinder him, but she did not raise her hands, did not resist, and only pressed them to her breast and sighed heavily and repeated:

“Oh, a great sin. What are you doing? Have pity on yourself. You are destroying other people’s souls, and your own still more... O-oh!” she cried out.

Stepan could no longer bear her voice and her look, and he slashed her across the throat with the knife. “Talking to the likes of you!” She sank back on the pillows and began to make a choking sound, soaking the pillow with blood. He turned away and went through the rooms, gathering things. Having taken what he needed, Stepan lit a cigarette, sat a while, brushed off his clothes, and went out. He thought he would get away with this murder too, as with the earlier ones, but before he reached his lodging for the night he suddenly felt such exhaustion that he could not move a single limb. He lay down in a ditch and lay there the rest of the night, all the next day, and the following night.

## PART TWO

## I

As he lay in the ditch, Stepan continually saw before him the gentle, thin, frightened face of Maria Semyonovna and heard her voice: “How can you do such a thing?” said that peculiar voice of hers, her lisping, pitiful voice. And again Stepan lived through everything he had done to her. And he grew frightened, and he shut his eyes and shook his hairy head to shake these thoughts and memories out of it. And for a moment he was free of the memories, but in their place there appeared to him first one black figure, then another, and after the other came still more black figures with red eyes, and they made faces, and all of them said one thing: “You’ve finished with her—now finish with yourself, or else we won’t give you any peace.” And he opened his eyes and again saw her and heard her voice, and he felt sorry for her, and disgusted and frightened at himself. And he shut his eyes again, and again the black figures.

Toward evening of the next day he got up and went to a tavern. He barely dragged himself to the tavern and began to drink. But however much he drank, he did not get drunk. He sat silently at a table and drank glass after glass. A police sergeant came into the tavern.

“Who are you?” the police sergeant asked him.

“Why, the very one. Yesterday I cut all their throats at Dobrotvorov’s.”

They tied him up and, after keeping him for a day at the district police officer’s quarters, sent him to the provincial town. The prison governor, recognizing in him his former prisoner, a brawler then and now a great criminal, received him sternly.

“Mind you, no mischief here with me,” the governor rasped, knitting his brows and thrusting out his lower jaw. “If I notice anything at all, I’ll flog you to death. You won’t run away from me.”

“Why should I run?” Stepan answered, lowering his eyes. “I gave myself up.”

“Well, none of your talk with me. And when your superiors speak to you, look them in the eye,” the governor shouted, and struck him under the jaw with his fist.

At that moment she appeared to Stepan again, and he heard her voice. He did not hear what the governor was saying to him.

“What?” he asked, coming to himself when he felt the blow on his face.

“Come on, come on—march, no use pretending.”

The governor expected rowdiness, dealings with the other prisoners, attempts to escape. But nothing of the kind happened. Whenever the guard or the governor himself looked through the peephole in his door, Stepan was sitting on the straw-stuffed sack with his head propped in his hands, and kept whispering something to himself. At the examining magistrate’s interrogations, too, he was unlike the other prisoners: he was absent-minded and did not listen to the questions; but when he did understand them, he was so truthful <!-- vol. 36, p. 35 --> that the magistrate, used to fighting the accused with skill and cunning, felt here something like what you feel when, in the dark, at the end of a staircase, you lift your foot for a step that isn’t there. Stepan described all his murders with knitted brows and his eyes fixed on one point, in the plainest, most matter-of-fact tone, trying to remember every detail: “Out he came,” Stepan said of the first murder, “barefoot, and stood in the doorway, so I whacked him once, and he started wheezing, and then I set about the woman straightaway,” and so on. When the prosecutor made his round of the prison cells, Stepan was asked whether he had any complaints and whether he needed anything. He answered that he needed nothing and that no one was mistreating him. The prosecutor, after walking a few steps down the stinking corridor, stopped and asked the governor who was accompanying him how this prisoner behaved.

“I can’t get over him,” the governor answered, pleased that Stepan had praised the way he was treated. “It’s his second month with us, and his conduct is exemplary. Only I’m afraid he may be planning something. A daring man, and of enormous strength.”

## II

During the first month in prison Stepan was tormented continually by one and the same thing. He saw the gray wall of his cell and heard the sounds of the prison—the hum below him in the common cell, the sentry’s footsteps along the corridor, the ticking of the clock—and at the same time he saw her, with her gentle look, which had conquered him even at their meeting in the street, and her thin, wrinkled neck, which he had cut, and he heard her tender, pitiful, lisping voice: “You are destroying other people’s souls, and your own. How can you do this?” Then the voice would die away, and those three would appear—the black figures. And they appeared all the same, whether his eyes were shut or open. With his eyes shut they appeared more distinctly. When Stepan opened his eyes, they merged with the doors and walls and gradually vanished. But then they would emerge again and come at him from three sides, making faces and repeating: finish it, finish it. You can make a noose, you can set a fire. And then a shudder would run through Stepan, and he would begin reciting the prayers he knew, the prayer to the Mother of God and the Our Father, and at first it seemed to help. While reciting the prayers he would begin to remember his life: he remembered his father, his mother, the village, the dog Volchok, his grandfather on the stove, the benches he used to slide down on with the other children; then he remembered the girls with their songs, then the horses, how they were stolen and how the horse thief was caught, how he finished him off with a stone. And the first prison came back to him, and how he got out, and he remembered the fat innkeeper, the carter’s wife, the children, and then again he remembered her. And he would grow hot, and, letting his prison coat slip from his shoulders, he would spring up from the sleeping platform and begin, like a beast in a cage, to pace rapidly back and forth across the short <!-- vol. 36, p. 36 --> cell, turning quickly at the sweating, damp walls. And again he recited prayers, but the prayers no longer helped.

One long autumn evening, when the wind whistled and droned in the chimneys, he ran about his cell until he was worn out and then sat down on his bunk. He felt that he could struggle no longer, that the black figures had overcome him, and he submitted to them. He had long been eyeing the vent of the stove. If you wrapped thin cords or thin strips of linen around it, it would not slip off. But it had to be cleverly arranged. And he set to work, and for two days he made linen strips from the sack he slept on (when the guard came in, he covered the bunk with his prison coat). He tied the strips together with knots and made them double, so that they would not break but would hold his body. While he was preparing all this, he was not tormented. When everything was ready, he made a running noose, put it around his neck, climbed onto the bed, and hanged himself. But just as his tongue began to stick out, the strips broke and he fell. The guard came in at the noise. The medical assistant was called, and Stepan was taken to the infirmary. The next day he had completely recovered, and he was taken out of the infirmary and put no longer in a separate cell but in the common one.

In the common cell he lived among twenty men as though he were alone. He saw no one and spoke to no one, and he was tormented just as before. It was especially hard for him when everyone was asleep and he was not, and he saw her as before and heard her voice, and then the black figures appeared again with their terrible eyes and taunted him.

Again, as before, he recited prayers, and, as before, they did not help.

Once, when she appeared to him again after he had prayed, he began to pray to her, to her dear soul, that she would release him and forgive him. And when, toward morning, he collapsed onto the flattened sack, he fell fast asleep, and in his sleep she came to him, with her thin, wrinkled, cut neck.

“Well, will you forgive me?”

She looked at him with her gentle look and said nothing.

“Will you forgive me?”

And so he asked her, up to three times. But still she said nothing. And he woke up. From then on it was easier for him, and he seemed to come to himself; he looked around him and for the first time began to draw closer to his cellmates and talk with them.

## III

In the same cell with Stepan were Vasily, who had again been caught stealing and sentenced to exile, and Chuyev, who had also been sentenced to exile in a settlement. Vasily was forever either singing songs in his fine voice or telling the others about his adventures.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 37 --> Chuyev, for his part, either worked, sewing something from clothing or linen, or read the Gospel and the Psalter.

Stepan asked him what he was being exiled for. Chuyev explained to him that he was being exiled for the true faith of Christ, because the priests, those deceivers, cannot stand the smell of people who live by the Gospel and expose them. And when Stepan asked Chuyev what the law of the Gospel consisted in, Chuyev explained to him that the law of the Gospel was not to pray to gods made by hands, but to worship in spirit and truth. And he told how they had learned this real faith from the legless tailor at the dividing of the land.

“Well, and what will there be for bad deeds?” Stepan asked.

“It’s all been said.”

And Chuyev read to him:

“When the son of man comes in his glory, and all the holy angels with him, then he will sit on the throne of his glory, and all the nations will be gathered before him; and he will separate them one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats, and he will set the sheep on his right side, and the goats on the left. “Then the king will say to those on his right side: ‘Come, you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the creation of the world: for I was hungry, and you gave me something to eat; I was thirsty, and you gave me drink; I was a wanderer, and you took me in; I was naked, and you clothed me; I was sick, and you visited me; I was in prison, and you came to me.’ “Then the righteous will say to him in answer: ‘Lord! When did we see you hungry and feed you, or thirsty and give you drink? When did we see you a wanderer and take you in, or naked and clothe you? When did we see you sick or in prison and come to you?’ And the king will say to them in answer: ‘Truly I tell you: as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me.’ “Then he will say also to those on the left: ‘Go from me, you accursed, into the everlasting fire prepared for the devil and his angels: for I was hungry, and you gave me nothing to eat; I was thirsty, and you gave me no drink; I was a wanderer, and you did not take me in; I was naked, and you did not clothe me; sick and in prison, and you did not visit me.’ “Then they too will say to him in answer: ‘Lord! When did we see you hungry, or thirsty, or a wanderer, or naked, or sick, or in prison, and did not serve you?’ Then he will say to them in answer: ‘Truly I tell you: as you did not do it to one of the least of these, you did not do it to me.’ And these will go into everlasting torment, and the righteous into everlasting life.” (Matthew XXV, 31–46.)

Vasily, who had sat down on the floor facing Chuyev and was listening to the reading, nodded his handsome head approvingly.

“That’s right,” he said decisively. “‘Go,’ he says, ‘you accursed, into everlasting torment’—you fed nobody, and guzzled it all yourselves. Serves them right. Come on, give it here, I’ll read a bit,” he added. He wanted to show off his reading.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 38 --> Stepan, who had been listening to the reading in silence, his shaggy head lowered, asked: “Well, and won’t there be any forgiveness?”

“Wait, be quiet,” Chuyev said to Vasily, who kept going on about how the rich had neither fed the wanderer nor visited him in prison. “Wait, will you,” Chuyev repeated, leafing through the Gospel. Having found what he was looking for, Chuyev smoothed out the pages with his large, strong hand, grown white in the prison.

“And they led with him—with Christ, that is,” Chuyev began, “two criminals also, to be put to death. And when they came to the place called the Skull, there they crucified him and the criminals, one on the right and the other on the left side.

“And Jesus said: ‘Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing.’... And the people stood and watched. And the rulers too scoffed along with them, saying: ‘He saved others; let him save himself, if he is the Christ, the chosen of God.’ The soldiers also mocked him, coming up and offering him vinegar and saying: ‘If you are the king of the Jews, save yourself.’ And there was an inscription over him, inscribed in Greek, Roman, and Hebrew words: ‘This is the king of the Jews.’ “One of the hanged criminals reviled him and said: ‘If you are the Christ, save yourself and us.’ But the other, on the contrary, checked him and said: ‘Do you not fear God, when you yourself are condemned to the same? And we are condemned justly, for we have received what our deeds deserved; but he has done nothing wrong.’ And he said to Jesus: ‘Remember me, Lord, when you come into your kingdom.’ And Jesus said to him: ‘Truly I tell you: this very day you will be with me in paradise.’” (Luke XXIII, 32–43.)

Stepan said nothing and sat lost in thought, as though listening, but he no longer heard anything of what Chuyev read after that.

“So that’s what the true faith consists in,” he thought. “Only those will be saved who fed the poor, gave them drink, and visited prisoners, and those who did not do this will go to hell. And yet the robber repented only on the cross, and even so he went to paradise.” He saw no contradiction here; on the contrary, the one confirmed the other: that the merciful would go to paradise and the unmerciful to hell meant that everyone must be merciful, and that Christ forgave the robber meant that Christ too was merciful. All this was completely new to Stepan; he only wondered why it had been hidden from him until now. And he spent all his free time with Chuyev, asking and listening. And as he listened, he understood. The general meaning of the whole teaching opened to him: that people are brothers and must love and pity one another, and then it will be good for everyone. And when he listened, he took in, as something forgotten and familiar, everything that confirmed the general meaning of this teaching, and let pass by his ears whatever did not confirm it, putting this down to his own lack of understanding.

And from that time Stepan became a different man.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 39 -->

## IV

Stepan Pelageyushkin had been docile before too, but lately he had been astonishing the governor and the guards and his fellow prisoners with the change that had taken place in him. Without being ordered, and out of turn, he did all the heaviest work, including cleaning out the latrine bucket. But in spite of this submissiveness of his, his fellow prisoners respected him and were afraid of him, knowing his firmness and his great physical strength, especially after the incident with two vagrants who had attacked him but whom he had fought off, breaking the arm of one of them. These vagrants had set about fleecing a young, well-off prisoner at gambling and had taken from him everything he had. Stepan stood up for him and took the winnings away from them. The vagrants began to curse him, then to beat him, but he got the better of them both. When the governor tried to find out what the quarrel was about, the vagrants declared that Pelageyushkin had started beating them. Stepan did not justify himself and submissively accepted the punishment, which consisted of three days in the punishment cell and a transfer to a solitary cell.

The solitary cell was hard for him because it had separated him from Chuyev and the Gospel, and besides, he was afraid that the visions of her and of the black figures would come back again. But there were no visions. His whole soul was filled with something new and joyful. He would have been glad of his solitude if he could read and had the Gospel. They would have given him the Gospel, but he could not read.

As a boy he had begun to learn his letters in the old way: az, buki, vedi; but, being slow to understand, he had not got beyond the alphabet, and at that time he could not understand syllables at all, and so he had remained illiterate. But now he decided to learn, and he asked a guard for a Gospel. The guard brought him one, and he set to work. He recognized the letters but could not put anything together. However hard he struggled to understand how words are put together out of letters, nothing came of it. He did not sleep at night, kept thinking, did not want to eat, and from misery such lice set upon him that he could not get rid of them.

Once the guard asked him: “Well, still haven’t got it?”

“No.”

“And do you know the Our Father?”

“I know it.”

“Well then, read that. Here it is.” And the guard showed him the Our Father in the Gospel.

Stepan began to read the Our Father, matching the familiar letters with the familiar sounds. And suddenly the mystery of putting letters together was revealed to him. And he began to read. It was a great joy. And from then on he began to read, and the meaning that emerged little by little from words put together with difficulty took on still greater significance.

Solitude now no longer weighed on Stepan but gladdened him.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 40 --> He was wholly taken up with his task and was not glad when, in order to free cells for newly arrived political prisoners, he was moved back into the common cell.

## V

Now it was no longer Chuyev but Stepan who often read the Gospel in the cell. And some of the prisoners sang filthy songs, while others listened to his reading and to his talk about what he had read. Two always listened to him in this way, silently and attentively: the convict, murderer, and executioner Makhorkin, and Vasily, who had been caught stealing and, while awaiting trial, was being held in the same prison. Makhorkin had twice carried out his duties during his time in prison, both times on trips elsewhere, because no people could be found who would carry out what the judges sentenced. The peasants who had killed Pyotr Nikolaich had been tried by a military court, and two of them had been sentenced to death by hanging.

Makhorkin was summoned to Penza to carry out his duty. In former times, on such occasions, he would at once write—he could read and write well—a paper to the governor, in which he explained that he had been dispatched to Penza to carry out his duties and therefore asked the head of the province to assign him the daily subsistence money due to him; but now, to the astonishment of the prison governor, he declared that he would not go and would no longer carry out the duties of an executioner.

“Forgotten the lash, have you?” shouted the prison governor.

“Well, if it’s the lash, then it’s the lash, but there’s no law for killing.”

“So what’s this, you picked that up from Pelageyushkin? A prison prophet has turned up. You just wait.”

## VI

Meanwhile Makhin, the schoolboy who had taught how to forge the coupon, had finished the gymnasium and the university course in the law faculty. Thanks to his success with women, with the former mistress of an old man who was a deputy minister, he had been appointed an examining magistrate while still quite young. He was a dishonest man, in debt, a seducer of women, a gambler; but he was an adroit, quick-witted man with a retentive memory, and he knew how to conduct cases well.

He was the examining magistrate in the district where Stepan Pelageyushkin was being tried. Already at the first interrogation Stepan surprised him with his answers, simple, truthful, and calm. Makhin felt unconsciously that this man standing before him in fetters and with a shaved head, whom two soldiers had brought in and were guarding and would take back under lock and key, that this was an entirely free man, standing morally, unattainably high above him.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 41 --> And therefore, while questioning him, he kept encouraging himself and spurring himself on so as not to be disconcerted and not to get muddled. What struck him was that Stepan spoke of his deeds as of something long past, committed not by him but by some other man.

“And you didn’t feel sorry for them?” Makhin asked.

“I didn’t. I didn’t understand then.”

“Well, and now?”

Stepan smiled sadly.

“Now, even if you burned me in the fire, I wouldn’t do that.”

“Why so?”

“Because I’ve understood that all people are brothers.”

“What, am I your brother too?”

“Of course.”

“How’s that? I’m a brother, and I’m sentencing you to hard labor?”

“From not understanding.”

“What is it I don’t understand?”

“You don’t understand, since you judge.”

“Well, let’s go on. Where did you go after that?…”

But what struck Makhin most of all was what he learned from the prison governor about Pelageyushkin’s influence on the executioner Makhorkin, who, at the risk of being punished, had refused to carry out his duty.

## VII

At an evening party at the Yeropkins’ there were two young ladies—wealthy girls of marriageable age—and Makhin was courting both of them. After the singing of romances, in which the very musical Makhin particularly distinguished himself—he both sang a fine second part and accompanied—he told, very accurately and in detail—he had an excellent memory—and with complete indifference, about the strange criminal who had converted the executioner. Makhin remembered everything so well and could relate it all precisely because he was always completely indifferent to the people he had to deal with. He did not enter, did not know how to enter, into the state of mind of other people, and that was just why he could remember so well everything that happened to people, what they did and said. But Pelageyushkin interested him. He did not enter into Stepan’s soul, but he could not help asking himself what was in his soul, and, finding no answer, but feeling that it was something interesting, he told the whole affair at the party: the corrupting of the executioner, and the governor’s stories of how strangely Pelageyushkin behaved, and how he read the Gospel, and what a strong influence he had on his fellow prisoners.

Makhin’s story interested everyone, but most of all the younger daughter, Liza Yeropkina, an eighteen-year-old girl who had just left the institute and had only just come to her senses after the <!-- vol. 36, p. 42 --> darkness and narrowness of the false conditions in which she had grown up, and who, as if she had just come up out of the water, was passionately breathing in the fresh air of life. She began questioning Makhin about the details and about how and why such a change had come about in Pelageyushkin. And Makhin told what he had heard from Stepan about the last murder, and how the meekness, the submissiveness, and the fearlessness of death of this very kind woman, the last one he had killed, had conquered him and opened his eyes, and how afterward the reading of the Gospel had completed the work.

For a long time that night Liza Yeropkina could not fall asleep. For several months a struggle had been going on in her between the life of society, into which her sister was drawing her, and her attraction to Makhin, joined with a desire to reform him. And now the latter had gained the upper hand. She had heard about the murdered woman before. But now, after this terrible death and Makhin’s story from Pelageyushkin’s own words, she had learned the story of Maria Semyonovna down to its details and was struck by everything she had learned about her.

Liza passionately wanted to be a Maria Semyonovna like that. She was rich and was afraid that Makhin was courting her for her money. And she decided to give away her estate, and she told Makhin so.

Makhin was glad of the chance to show his unselfishness and told Liza that he did not love her for her money, and this decision, magnanimous as it seemed to him, moved him himself. Meanwhile a struggle began between Liza and her mother (the estate had been her father’s), who would not allow her to give the estate away. And Makhin helped Liza. And the more he acted in this way, the more he understood the entirely different world of spiritual strivings, alien to him until then, which he saw in Liza.

## VIII

Everything had grown quiet in the cell. Stepan lay in his place on the bunks and was not yet asleep. Vasily came up to him and, tugging him by the leg, winked at him to get up and come out to him. Stepan slid down from the bunks and went over to Vasily.

“Well, brother,” said Vasily, “you be so good and help me out.”

“Help with what?”

“Why, I want to escape.”

And Vasily revealed to Stepan that he had everything ready for the escape.

“Tomorrow I’ll stir them up,” he pointed at the men lying there. “They’ll name me. They’ll move me to the upper cells, and up there I know what to do. Only you loosen the staple from the mortuary for me.”

“That can be done. Where will you go, then?”

“Why, wherever my eyes take me. Aren’t there plenty of bad people?”

“That’s so, brother, only it isn’t for us to judge them.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 43 --> “Well, what of it, am I some kind of soul-killer? I haven’t killed a single soul, and as for stealing? What’s bad about that? Don’t they rob the likes of us?”

“That’s their business. They’ll answer for it.”

“So why stand on ceremony with them? Look, I robbed a church. Who was hurt by that? Now I want to fix it so it’s not some little shop, but grab the treasury and give it away. Give it away to good people.”

At that moment one of the prisoners rose from the bunks and began to listen. Stepan and Vasily separated.

The next day Vasily did as he had meant to. He began complaining about the bread, that it was underbaked, and put all the prisoners up to calling the governor to them to lodge a complaint. The governor came, swore at them all, and, learning that Vasily was the instigator of the whole thing, ordered him to be put apart, in a solitary cell on the upper floor.

That was just what Vasily needed.

## IX

Vasily knew the upper cell they had put him in. He knew the floor in it, and as soon as he got in there, he began taking the floor apart. When it was possible to crawl under the floor, he took apart the ceiling boards and jumped down to the floor below, into the mortuary. That day one dead man was lying on the table in the mortuary. In this same mortuary the sacks for straw mattresses were stacked. Vasily knew this and had counted on this room. The staple in this room had been pulled out and put back in. Vasily went out of the door and went to the latrine that was being built at the end of the corridor. In this latrine there was a hole that went straight through from the third floor down to the lowest one, the basement. Having felt the door, Vasily went back to the mortuary, took the linen cloth off the dead man, who was cold as ice (he touched his hand as he took it off), then took the sacks and tied them together in knots so as to make a rope out of them. He carried this rope of sacks to the latrine; there he tied the rope to a crossbeam and climbed down it. The rope did not reach the floor. Whether it fell short by much or by little, he did not know, but there was nothing else to do: he hung from it and jumped. He bruised his feet, but he could walk. There were two windows in the basement. One could have crawled through, but iron gratings were set into them. They had to be broken out. With what? Vasily began to grope about. In the basement there were pieces of board lying around. He found one piece with a sharp end and began using it to pry out the bricks that held the gratings. He worked a long time. The cocks were already crowing for the second time, and the grating still held. At last one side came out. Vasily pushed the piece of board under it and heaved; the whole grating wrenched loose, but a brick fell and clattered. The sentries might hear. Vasily froze. All was quiet. He climbed into the window. He got out. He had to get away over the wall. In a corner of the yard there was an outbuilding. He had to climb onto this outbuilding and from it over <!-- vol. 36, p. 44 --> the wall. He must take the piece of board with him. Without it you won’t get up. Vasily climbed back in. Again he crawled out with the piece of board and froze, listening for where the sentry was. The sentry, as he had reckoned, was walking along the other side of the square yard. Vasily went up to the outbuilding, set the board against it, and began to climb. The board slipped, fell. Vasily was in his stockings. He took off the stockings so as to grip with his feet, set the board up again, sprang onto it, and caught hold of the gutter with his hand. “Little father, don’t come away, hold!” He seized the gutter, and now his knee is on the roof. The sentry is coming. Vasily lay down and froze. The sentry does not see him and moves off again. Vasily jumps up. The iron crackles under his feet. Another step, two, here is the wall. The wall is easy to reach with his hand. One hand, the other, he stretched out full length, and there he is on the wall. If only he doesn’t hurt himself jumping down. Vasily turns over, hangs by his hands, stretches down, lets go with one hand, the other. “Lord, bless!” On the ground. And the ground is soft. His legs are whole. And he runs.

In the suburb Malanya unlocks the door, and he crawls under the warm patchwork quilt, saturated with the smell of sweat.

## X

Pyotr Nikolaich’s wife was large, handsome, always calm, childless, plump like a barren cow. She saw from the window how they killed her husband and dragged him off somewhere into the field. The feeling of horror at the sight of this slaughter, which Natalya Ivanovna (that was the name of Pyotr Nikolaich’s widow) experienced, was, as always happens, so strong that it drowned out all other feelings in her. But when the whole crowd had disappeared behind the garden fence and the hum of voices had died down, and barefoot Malanya, the girl who waited on them, came running in with bulging eyes with the news, as though it were something joyful, that Pyotr Nikolaich had been killed and thrown into the ravine, another feeling began to stand out from behind the first feeling of horror: the feeling of joy at being freed from the despot whose eyes, covered by black glasses, had kept her in slavery for nineteen years. She herself was horrified at this feeling. She did not admit it even to herself, and still less did she voice it to anyone. When they washed the mutilated, yellow, hairy body and dressed it and laid it in the coffin, she was horrified, wept, and sobbed. When the examining magistrate for especially important cases arrived and questioned her as a witness, she saw there, in the magistrate’s quarters, two peasants in chains who had been found to be the chief culprits. One was already old, with a long fair beard in curls and a calm, stern, handsome face; the other was of Gypsy cast, not an old man, with shining black eyes and curly, tousled hair. She testified to what she knew and recognized in these very men those who had been the first to seize Pyotr Nikolaich by the arms. The peasant who looked like a <!-- vol. 36, p. 45 --> Gypsy, his eyes flashing and roving from under his moving brows, said reproachfully: “It’s a sin, ma’am! Oh, we all have to die.” Despite this, she did not feel the least bit sorry for them. On the contrary, during the investigation a hostile feeling rose up in her, and a desire to take revenge on her husband’s murderers.

But when, a month later, the case, which had been handed over to a military court, was decided with eight men sentenced to hard labor and two, the white-bearded old man and the swarthy young Gypsy, as they called him, sentenced to hanging, she felt something unpleasant. But this unpleasant doubt, under the influence of the solemnity of the court, soon passed. If the highest authorities recognize that it is necessary, then it follows that it is good.

The execution was to take place in the village. And on Sunday, having come back from the liturgy, Malanya, in a new dress and new shoes, reported to her mistress that they were building a gallows, and that the executioner from Moscow was expected by Wednesday, and that the families were wailing without stopping; it could be heard all over the village.

Natalya Ivanovna did not go out of the house, so as not to see either the gallows or the people, and she wished for one thing: that what had to be would be over as soon as possible. She thought only of herself, and not of the condemned men and their families.

## XI

On Tuesday a district police officer whom Natalya Ivanovna knew stopped by to see her. Natalya Ivanovna treated him to vodka and to salted mushrooms of her own making. The district police officer drank some vodka and had a bite to eat. He told her that the execution would not take place tomorrow after all.

“What? Why?”

“An amazing story. They couldn’t find an executioner. There was one in Moscow, and even he, my son was telling me, has read his fill of the Gospel and says: I can’t kill. He was sentenced to hard labor for murder himself, and now all of a sudden he can’t kill according to the law. They told him he would be flogged with the lash. Flog me, he says, but I can’t.”

Natalya Ivanovna suddenly flushed; she even broke into a sweat from her thoughts.

“And can’t they be forgiven now?”

“How can they be forgiven when they’ve been sentenced by a court? Only the Tsar can forgive.”

“But how will the Tsar know?”

“They have the right to petition for a pardon.”

“But they’re being executed because of me,” said foolish Natalya Ivanovna. “And I forgive them.”

The district police officer laughed.

“Well then, ask.”

“Can I?”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 46 --> “Of course you can.”

“But surely there’s no time now?”

“It can be done by telegram.”

“To the Tsar?”

“Well, you can send it to the Tsar too.”

The news that the executioner had refused and was ready to suffer rather than kill suddenly turned Natalya Ivanovna’s soul upside down. And the feeling of compassion and horror that had several times been asking to come out broke through and took hold of her.

“My dear Filipp Vasilyevich, write me a telegram. I want to ask the Tsar for a pardon.”

The district police officer shook his head.

“What if we catch it for this?”

“But I’m the one who answers for it. I won’t say anything about you.”

“What a kind woman,” thought the district police officer, “a good woman. If mine were like that, it would be paradise, not what it is now.”

And the district police officer wrote a telegram to the Tsar: “To His Imperial Majesty the Sovereign Emperor. The loyal subject of Your Imperial Majesty, the widow of Collegiate Assessor Pyotr Nikolayevich Sventitsky, who was killed by peasants, falling at the sacred feet (this place in the telegram especially pleased the district police officer who composed it) of Your Imperial Majesty, implores You to pardon the peasants so-and-so, of such-and-such province, district, volost, village, who have been sentenced to capital punishment.”

The telegram was sent by the district police officer himself. And Natalya Ivanovna felt glad and good at heart. It seemed to her that if she, the widow of the murdered man, forgave them and asked for them to be pardoned, the Tsar could not fail to pardon them.

## XII

Liza Yeropkina lived in an unceasing state of exaltation. The farther she went along the path of Christian life that had opened before her, the more certain she was that it was the true path, and the more joyful she felt in her soul.

She now had two most immediate aims. The first was to convert Makhin, or rather, as she put it to herself, to bring him back to himself, to his own good, beautiful nature. She loved him, and in the light of her love the divine in his soul, common to all people, was revealed to her; but in this principle of life common to all people she saw a goodness, tenderness, and loftiness peculiar to him alone. Her other aim was to stop being rich. She had wanted to free herself of her property in order to test Makhin, and then for herself, for her own soul—according to the word of the Gospel, she wanted to do it. At first she began to give things away, but her father stopped her, and, even more than her father, the crowd of petitioners who came flooding in, <!-- vol. 36, p. 47 --> in person and by letter. Then she decided to turn to an elder known for his holy life, so that he would take her money and do with it as he saw fit. On learning of this, her father got angry, and in a heated conversation with her he called her a madwoman, a mental case, and said that he would take measures to protect her, as a madwoman, from herself.

Her father’s angry, irritated tone passed to her, and before she could come to her senses, she burst into spiteful tears and said rude things to her father, calling him a despot and even a money-grubber.

She asked her father’s forgiveness; he said he was not angry, but she saw that he had been offended and in his heart had not forgiven her. She did not want to tell Makhin about it. Her sister, who was jealous of her over Makhin, had drawn completely away from her. She had no one to share her feeling with, no one before whom to repent.

“I must repent before God,” she said to herself. And since it was Lent, she decided to prepare for communion and at confession tell her confessor everything and ask his advice about how she should act from now on.

Not far from the town there was a monastery in which lived an elder who had become famous for his life, his teachings, and the prophecies and healings that were attributed to him.

The elder had received a letter from old Yeropkin, warning him of his daughter’s arrival and of her abnormal, agitated state, and expressing confidence that the elder would set her on the true path: that of the golden mean, of a good Christian life, without violating existing conditions.

Tired from receiving visitors, the elder received Liza and calmly began to impress on her moderation, submission to existing conditions, to her parents. Liza was silent, blushing and sweating. But when he had finished, she began to speak, with tears standing in her eyes, timidly at first, about how Christ said: “Leave father and mother and follow me”; then, growing more and more animated, she set out all her idea of how she understood Christianity. At first the elder smiled slightly and objected with his usual teachings, but then he fell silent and began to sigh, only repeating: “Oh, Lord.”

He said: “Well, all right, come to confession tomorrow.” And he blessed her with his wrinkled hand.

The next day he heard her confession and, without continuing the previous day’s conversation, let her go, briefly declining to take charge of her property.

The purity, the complete devotion to the will of God, and the ardor of this girl struck the elder. He had long wanted to renounce the world, but the monastery demanded his activity of him. This activity provided means for the monastery. And he consented, though he vaguely felt the whole untruth of his position. They were making him a saint, a miracle-worker, but he was a weak man, carried away by success. And the soul of this girl, which had opened to him, opened <!-- vol. 36, p. 48 --> his own soul to him as well. And he saw how far he was from what he wanted to be and what his heart drew him to.

Soon after Liza’s visit he shut himself up in seclusion, and only after three weeks did he come out into the church; he celebrated the service, and after the service he preached a sermon. In this sermon he reproached himself, and convicted the world of sin, and called it to repentance.

Every two weeks he preached sermons. And more and more people came to these sermons. And his fame as a preacher spread more and more. There was something special, bold, sincere in his sermons. And because of this he had such a strong effect on people.

## XIII

Meanwhile Vasily did everything as he wanted. With his comrades he crept in at night to Krasnopuzov, a rich man. He knew how miserly and depraved the man was, and he got into the bureau and took out thirty thousand in cash. And Vasily went on doing as he wanted. He even stopped drinking, and gave money to poor brides. He married them off, bought people out of debt, and kept himself hidden. And his only care was to give the money away well. He gave to the police as well. And they did not look for him.

His heart rejoiced. And they took him all the same. At the trial he laughed and boasted: the fat-belly’s money had just been lying there, he hadn’t even known how much he had, and I put it to work, I helped good people with it.

And his defense was so cheerful and good-natured that the jury nearly acquitted him. He was sentenced to exile.

He thanked them and told them in advance that he would get away.

## XIV

Sventitskaya’s telegram to the Tsar produced no effect whatever. In the Commission of Petitions they had at first decided not even to report it to the Tsar. But later, when the Sventitsky case came up at breakfast at the Sovereign’s, the director who was breakfasting with the Sovereign reported the telegram from the murdered man’s wife.

One of the ladies of the imperial family said: “C’est très gentil de sa part.”[^2]

But the Sovereign sighed, shrugged his epauletted shoulders, and said: “The law.” And he held out his glass, into which a chamber footman was pouring sparkling Moselle. Everyone pretended to be struck by the wisdom of the word the Sovereign had spoken. And there was no more talk of the telegram. And the two peasants—the old one and the young one—were hanged with the help of a cruel murderer who practiced bestiality, a Tatar executioner brought in from Kazan.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 49 --> The old woman wanted to dress the body of her old man in a white shirt, white footcloths, and new peasant boots, but she was not allowed to. And both of them were buried in one pit outside the cemetery fence.

Once the Sovereign’s mother, the old Empress, said to her son: “Princess Sofia Vladimirovna has been telling me that he is an amazing preacher. Faites le venir. Il peut prêcher à la cathédrale.”[^3]

“No, better here with us,” said the Sovereign. And he ordered the elder Isidor to be invited.

All the people of general’s rank gathered in the palace church. A new and extraordinary preacher was an event.

A little old man came out, gray-haired and thin, and looked around at them all: “In the name of the father and the son and the holy spirit.” And he began.

At first it went well, but the further it went, the worse. “Il devenait de plus en plus agressif,”[^4] as the Empress said afterward. He thundered against everyone. He spoke about executions. And he ascribed the necessity of executions to bad government. Can one really kill people in a Christian country?

Everyone exchanged glances, and all of them were occupied only with the impropriety and with how unpleasant this was for the Sovereign, but no one showed it. When Isidor said “Amen,” the metropolitan came up to him and asked him to come to his rooms.

After a talk with the metropolitan and the Ober-Procurator, the little old man was sent back at once to a monastery. But not to his own, rather to the one at Suzdal, where Father Mikhail was superior and commandant.

## XV

Everyone pretended that nothing unpleasant had come of Isidor’s sermon, and no one mentioned it. And it seemed to the Tsar that the elder’s words had left no trace in him. But a couple of times in the course of the day he remembered the execution of the peasants for whose pardon Sventitskaya had asked by telegram. In the daytime there was a parade, then a drive out to the promenade, then a reception of ministers, then dinner, in the evening the theater. As usual, the Tsar fell asleep as soon as his head touched the pillow. In the night a terrible dream woke him: In a field stood gallows, and corpses were swinging on them, and the corpses were sticking out their tongues, and the tongues stretched farther and farther. And someone was shouting: “Your work, your work.” The Tsar woke up in a sweat and began to think. For the first time he began to think about the responsibility that lay on him, and all the little old man’s words came back to him...

<!-- vol. 36, p. 50 --> But he saw the human being in himself only from far off, and could not give himself up to the simple demands of a human being because of the demands made on the Tsar from all sides. And he did not have the strength to recognize the demands of a human being as more binding than the demands of the Tsar.

## XVI

Having served his second term in the jail, Prokofy, that lively, proud, dandyish fellow, came out of it a completely finished man. When sober, he sat and did nothing, and however much his father cursed him, he ate bread and did not work, and, more than that, he was always trying to sneak something off to the tavern to get a drink. He sat, coughed, hawked, and spat. The doctor he went to listened to his chest and shook his head.

“What you need, brother, is what you haven’t got.”

“That’s well known. You always do.”

“Drink milk, don’t smoke.”

“It’s the fast now as it is, and there’s no cow either.”

Once in spring he did not sleep all night; he was in anguish, he wanted a drink. There was nothing at home to grab. He put on his cap and went out. He went along the street and came to the priests’ houses. At the sexton’s a harrow is standing outside, leaning against the wattle fence. Prokofy went up, heaved the harrow onto his back, and carried it to Petrovna’s, to the tavern. “Maybe she’ll give me a little bottle.” He had hardly gone off when the sexton came out onto the porch. It’s already quite light—he sees Prokofy carrying his harrow.

“Hey, what are you up to?”

People came out, seized Prokofy, and put him in the lockup. The justice of the peace sentenced him to eleven months in prison.

It was autumn. Prokofy was transferred to the hospital. He coughed and was tearing his whole chest apart. And he could not get warm. Those who were somewhat stronger still did not shiver. But Prokofy shivered day and night. The governor was pushing hard to save firewood and did not heat the hospital until November. Prokofy suffered sorely in body, but worst of all he suffered in spirit. Everything was repugnant to him, and he hated everyone: the sexton, and the governor for not heating, and the guard, and the man in the next cot with the swollen red lip. He came to hate also the new convict who had been brought in among them. This convict was Stepan. He had come down with erysipelas on his head, and he was transferred to the hospital and put next to Prokofy. At first Prokofy came to hate him, but then he came to love him so much that he only waited for the time when he could talk with him. Only after a talk with him did the anguish in Prokofy’s heart quiet down.

Stepan always told everyone about his last murder, and how it had affected him.

“Not that she cried out or anything,” he would say, “but just: here, cut. Pity not me, she says, but yourself.”

“Well, of course, it’s a terrible thing to destroy a soul. Once I even <!-- vol. 36, p. 51 --> set about slaughtering a ram, and I wasn’t glad of it myself. But I never destroyed anybody, so what did they destroy me for, the villains? I never did anyone harm...”

“Well, all that will be counted to your credit.”

“Where?”

“What do you mean, where? What about God?”

“Somehow there’s no sign of Him. I don’t believe, brother. I think you die—and grass grows. And that’s all.”

“And how do you figure it? How many souls I destroyed, and she, poor dear, did nothing but help people. What, do you think it will be the same for me and her? No, wait...”

“So you think when you die, the soul stays?”

“How else? That’s certain.”

It was hard for Prokofy to die; he was suffocating. But in the last hour it suddenly became easy. He called Stepan.

“Well, brother, goodbye. My death has come, it seems. I was afraid, and now it’s nothing. Only I want it quicker.”

And Prokofy died in the hospital.

## XVII

Meanwhile Yevgeny Mikhailovich’s affairs went worse and worse. The shop was mortgaged. Trade was slack. Another shop had opened in town, and the interest was being demanded. He had to borrow again at interest. And it ended with the shop and all the stock being put up for sale. Yevgeny Mikhailovich and his wife rushed about everywhere and could nowhere get the four hundred rubles that were needed to save the business.

There was a small hope in the merchant Krasnopuzov, whose mistress was acquainted with Yevgeny Mikhailovich’s wife. But now it was known all over town that a huge sum of money had been stolen from Krasnopuzov. People said half a million had been stolen.

“And who was it that stole it?” Yevgeny Mikhailovich’s wife was saying. “Vasily, our former porter. They say he’s throwing that money around now, and the police have been bought.”

“He was a scoundrel,” said Yevgeny Mikhailovich. “How easily he went in for perjury that time. I never would have thought it.”

“They say he came into our yard. The cook said it was him. She says he has married off fourteen poor brides.”

“Oh, they’ll make anything up.”

At that moment some strange elderly man in a cassinet jacket came into the shop.

“What do you want?”

“A letter for you.”

“From whom?”

“It’s written there.”

“What, doesn’t it need an answer? Wait, then.”

<!-- vol. 36, p. 52 --> “I can’t.”

And the strange man, having handed over the envelope, hurriedly left.

“Odd!”

Yevgeny Mikhailovich tore open the thick envelope and could not believe his eyes: Hundred-ruble notes. Four. What was this? And with them an illiterate letter to Yevgeny Mikhailovich: “In the Gospel it says, do good for evil. You done me a lot of evil with the coupon, and I wronged the little peasant something terrible, but here I am pittying you. Here, take 4 catherines and remember your porter Vasily.”

“No, this is astonishing,” Yevgeny Mikhailovich kept saying. He said it both to his wife and to himself. And whenever he remembered it or talked about it to his wife, tears came into his eyes, and there was joy in his soul.

## XVIII

In the Suzdal prison fourteen persons of the clergy were held, all of them chiefly for departing from Orthodoxy. Isidor too was sent there. Father Mikhail received Isidor according to the paper and, without talking with him, ordered him put in a separate cell, as an important criminal. In the third week of Isidor’s stay in the prison, Father Mikhail was making the rounds of the inmates. On entering Isidor’s cell, he asked whether he needed anything.

“I need a great deal; I can’t say it in front of people. Give me a chance to talk with you alone.”

They glanced at each other, and Mikhail understood that he had nothing to fear. He ordered Isidor brought to his own cell and, when they were left alone, said:

“Well, speak.”

Isidor fell on his knees.

“Brother!” said Isidor. “What are you doing? Have pity on yourself. Why, there is no villain worse than you; you have desecrated everything holy...”

A month later Mikhail submitted papers for the release, as penitents, not only of Isidor but of seven others as well. And he himself asked to retire to a monastery.

## XIX

Ten years passed.

Mitya Smokovnikov had finished his course at a technical school and was an engineer with a large salary at the gold fields in Siberia. He had to travel around his section. The director suggested that he take the convict Stepan Pelageyushkin.

<!-- vol. 36, p. 53 --> “A convict? Isn’t it dangerous?”

“With him it’s not dangerous. He’s a holy man. Ask anyone you like.”

“But what is he in for?”

The director smiled.

“He killed six souls, and he’s a holy man. I’ll answer for him.”

And so Mitya Smokovnikov took Stepan, a bald, thin, sunburned man, and set out with him.

On the road Stepan looked after Smokovnikov the way he looked after everyone wherever he could, as after his own child. And on the road he told him his whole story. And how and why and by what he lives now.

And a remarkable thing. Mitya Smokovnikov, who had lived until then only for drink, food, cards, wine, women, began for the first time to think about life. And these thoughts did not leave him, but unfolded his soul further and further. He was offered a post where the advantages were great. He refused. And he decided to use what he had to buy an estate, marry, and serve the people as best he could.

## XX

He did just that. But first he went to his father. He had been on unpleasant terms with him over the new family his father had started. Now, though, he decided to draw close to his father. And he did just that. And his father was surprised and laughed at him. And then he himself stopped attacking him. And he remembered many, many occasions when he had been in the wrong toward him.
