## *XIII. ON PRAYER.

Only older, married people who have no constant relations with the society youth of the present age can fail to know that most of this youth believes in nothing, and be surprised that I divide them into categories; but apart from these respectable persons, everyone will agree with me that unbelief has put down deep roots in the contemporary young generation of the upper circle and has spread so widely that it is frightening to think of the fate awaiting our fatherland, if it is true that in the course of education the upper class leads the lower ones.—

There are three grounds of unbelief: reasoning, vanity, and weakness; corresponding to this division there are also three classes of unbelievers—reasoners, the vain, and the weak.—To the first class belong people endowed with a strong mind and great energy. They feel such an irresistible need to bring everything under the inexorable laws of reason that they cannot help refuting the laws of religion founded on faith and revelation.—Why were they given the ability to refute these laws by thought? Why must they pass through the painful state of doubt and uncertainty? These are questions that it is not given to man to answer.—All he can say is that they are less guilty than unhappy—they unwillingly, more than unwillingly—with horror see what had been their support being destroyed; but an unknown, irresistible force draws them toward destruction.—Unbelievers of this class have existed always and everywhere; and, for the most part in old age, when with the years energy and the need for reflection diminish, they return with repentance to religion.—To the third class belong all those who, through weakness of mind and will and through imitation, submit to the opinion of the majority, cease to observe the dogmas of the Christian religion, and finally cease to believe in it altogether.—The number of unbelievers; <!-- vol. 1, p. 248 --> of all classes is so great [that] they make up the majority among the young.—How is a young boy to stand against public opinion—he is afraid inwardly, feels that he is doing wrong—he would gladly cross himself, but people are looking at him, he is threatened by the most immediate danger—the danger that acts most strongly on a young soul—ridicule, the bad opinion of people whom he has chosen as his models, with whom he cannot help being in constant relations.—

All the guilt of these people consists in youth, weakness, and vanity.—If fate were to cast them among virtuous people, they would be virtuous—they fell among unbelievers and became the same.—
