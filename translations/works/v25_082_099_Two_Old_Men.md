# TWO OLD MEN

> John 4:19. The woman says to him: Lord! I see that you are a prophet.

> 20. Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, and you say that the place where people ought to worship is in Jerusalem.

> 21. Jesus says to her: Believe me, the time is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the father.

> 22. You do not know what you bow down to, and we know what we bow down to, for salvation is from the Jews.

> 23. But the time will come, and has already come, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth; for such are the worshipers the Father seeks for himself.

## I

Two old men made up their minds to go and pray to God in old Jerusalem. One was a rich peasant; his name was Yefim Tarasych Shevelev. The other was a man who was not rich, Yelisey Bodrov.

Yefim was a staid peasant; he did not drink vodka, did not smoke tobacco or take snuff, had never in all his life sworn a foul word, and he was a strict and firm man. Yefim Tarasych had served two terms as village elder and had come out of office with no shortage charged against him. His family was large: two sons and a married grandson, and they all lived together. In appearance he was a hale, bearded, upright peasant, and only in his sixties had gray begun to show in his beard. Yelisey was a little old man, neither rich nor poor; in former days he used to go about doing carpentry work, but in his old age he had begun to live at home and kept bees. One son went away to earn money, the other was at home. Yelisey was a good-natured and cheerful man. He would drink vodka, too, and took snuff, and liked to sing songs, but he was a meek man and lived on friendly terms with his household and with his neighbors.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 83 --> In appearance Yelisey was a short little peasant, darkish, with a curly little beard and, like his saint, the prophet Elisha, bald over his whole head.

The old men had long ago made a vow and agreed to go together, but Tarasych never had the time: his affairs never let up. As soon as one thing was ending, another was starting: now he was marrying off his grandson, now he was waiting for his younger son to come back from the army, and then he had taken it into his head to build a new cottage.

Once, on a holiday, the old men met and sat down on some logs.

“Well then,” said Yelisey, “when are we going to go and fulfill our vow?” Yefim made a wry face.

“We’ll have to wait a while,” he said. “This year has turned out hard for me. I took it into my head to build this cottage; I thought I’d put a hundred or so into it, and it’s already climbing toward three hundred. And even so I haven’t finished it. It’ll have to be summer, I can see. In summer, God willing, we’ll go without fail.”

“To my mind,” said Yelisey, “there’s nothing to put it off for; we must go now. Spring is the very time.”

“The time is right enough, but the work has been begun; how can I drop it?”

“Haven’t you anyone? Your son will see to things.”

“See to them, how? My eldest isn’t to be relied on—he drinks.”

“When we die, old friend, they’ll live without us. Your son has to learn, too.”

“That’s so, but still I’d like to get the work done under my own eye.”

“Ah, my dear man! You’ll never get every job done. Just the other day the women at my house were washing and cleaning up for the holiday. This needed doing, and that, and they couldn’t get to everything. And my eldest daughter-in-law, a clever woman, says: ‘Thank goodness,’ she says, ‘the holiday is coming and doesn’t wait for us; otherwise,’ she says, ‘however much we did, we’d never get it all done.’”

Tarasych grew thoughtful.

“I’ve spent a lot of money on this building,” he said, “and one can’t set out on the road empty-handed either. It’s no small money—a hundred rubles.”

Yelisey laughed.

“Don’t sin, old friend,” he said. “You have ten times what I have, and you talk about money. Just say when to set out—I haven’t got it, but I will have.”

Tarasych grinned, too.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 84 --> “Look what a rich man has turned up!” he said. “Where will you get it, then?”

“I’ll scrape around at home and gather something; and whatever’s short, I’ll let my neighbor have ten or so hives from the stand. He’s been asking for a long time.”

“If the swarming turns out good, you’ll be sorry.”

“Sorry?! No, old friend! Never in my life have I been sorry about anything but my sins. There is nothing more precious than the soul.”

“That’s so, but still it’s not right when things at home aren’t seen to.”

“And when things in our soul aren’t seen to, that will be worse. We made a vow—let’s go! Truly, let’s go.”

## II

And Yelisey talked his companion into it. Yefim thought and thought, and the next morning he came to Yelisey.

“Well then, let’s go,” he said. “You speak the truth. God has power over death and life. While we’re alive and have the strength, we must go.”

A week or so later the old men were ready.

Tarasych had money at home. He took a hundred rubles for himself for the road and left two hundred with his old wife.

Yelisey got ready, too: he sold his neighbor ten hives from the stand, and the increase, however much came from the ten hives, also to the neighbor. For all of it he got seventy rubles. The other thirty rubles he gathered up from everyone in the house, sweeping it clean. His old wife gave her last money, which she had been saving for her funeral; his daughter-in-law gave hers.

Yefim Tarasych gave his eldest son orders about everything: how many meadows to take for mowing and where, and where to cart the manure, and how to finish the cottage and roof it. He thought over every job and gave orders for all of it. But Yelisey only told his old wife to set the young swarms from the sold hives aside separately and give them to the neighbor without cheating; about the household affairs he would not even talk: the work itself, he said, would show what needed doing and how. You’re the masters yourselves; you’ll do what is best for yourselves.

The old men got ready. They baked flat cakes at home, sewed bags, cut new foot-cloths, put on new soft boots, took spare bast shoes, and set off. Their households saw them past the village fence, they said their goodbyes, and the old men went on their way.

Yelisey set out in a cheerful spirit, and as soon as he had gone away from the village, he forgot all his affairs. His only thought was how to please his companion on the road, how not to say a rough word to anyone, how <!-- vol. 25, p. 85 --> to reach the place and come back home in peace and love. Yelisey walks along the road and keeps either whispering a prayer to himself or repeating from memory the saints’ lives that he knows. And when he meets someone on the way or comes to a place for the night, he tries to treat everyone as kindly as he can and to say a godly word. He walks and rejoices. There was one thing Yelisey could not do. He had meant to give up taking snuff and had left his snuffbox at home, but he grew dreary. On the road a man gave him some. And every now and then he would fall behind his companion, so as not to lead him into sin, and take a pinch.

Yefim Tarasych, too, walks well, firmly; he does nothing bad and says nothing idle, but there is no lightness in his soul. The cares of home will not go out of his head. He keeps recalling what is being done at home. Has he forgotten to give his son some order, and is his son doing it right? If he sees along the road that people are planting potatoes or carting manure, he thinks: Is my son doing it as I ordered? He felt he could just turn back and show them everything and do it himself.

## III

The old men walked for five weeks; they wore out the bast shoes from home and had begun buying new ones, and they came into the Ukrainian country. From home onward they had paid for their night’s lodging and their dinner, but when they came among the Ukrainians, people began vying with one another to call them in. They would take them in, and feed them, and take no money, and even fill their bags with bread for the road, and sometimes with flat cakes. In this free way the old men went some seven hundred versts; they crossed one more province and came to a place where the harvest had failed. People did take them in, and took no money for the night, but they stopped feeding them. And they did not give bread everywhere; sometimes they could not get it even for money. The year before, the people told them, nothing had grown. Those who had been rich were ruined and had sold everything; those who had lived middling well had come down to nothing; and as for the poor, they had either gone away altogether, or were going begging, or were scraping along at home somehow. In winter they had eaten chaff or goosefoot.

Once the old men spent the night in a little town; they bought some fifteen pounds of bread, spent the night, and set out before daybreak, to get farther on before the heat. They walked some ten versts and came to a little river; they sat down, scooped water into a cup, soaked a little bread, ate, and changed their footwear. They sat a while and rested. Yelisey took out his snuff horn. Yefim Tarasych shook his head at him.

“How can you not give up such filth!” he said.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 86 --> Yelisey waved his hand.

“Sin has got the better of me,” he said. “What can you do!”

They got up and went on. They walked some ten versts more. They came to a big village and went right through it. And by now it had grown hot. Yelisey was worn out; he wanted both to rest and to have a drink, but Tarasych does not stop. Tarasych was the stronger at walking, and it was rather hard for Yelisey to keep up with him.

“I could do with a drink,” he said.

“Well, have a drink. I don’t want any.”

Yelisey stopped.

“Don’t wait for me,” he said. “I’ll just run into that little cottage there and get a drink. I’ll catch up with you in no time.”

“All right,” he said. And Yefim Tarasych went on ahead along the road alone, and Yelisey turned toward the little cottage.

Yelisey went up to the little cottage. It was a small cottage, plastered with clay; black at the bottom, white at the top, but the clay had already peeled; plainly it had not been plastered for a long time; and the roof was stripped on one side. The way into the cottage was from the yard. Yelisey went into the yard; he sees a man lying by the earth bank around the cottage, beardless, thin, his shirt tucked into his trousers, in the Ukrainian way. The man had evidently lain down in the cool shade, but the sun had come round right onto him. And he lies there and is not asleep. Yelisey called to him and asked for a drink; the man did not respond. “Either he’s sick, or he’s unfriendly,” thought Yelisey, and went up to the door. He hears a child crying in the cottage. Yelisey knocked with the ring. “People of the house!” No one answers. Yelisey knocked again on the door with his staff. “Christian folk!” No one stirs. “Servants of God!” No one responds. Yelisey was about to go away, but he hears someone behind the door, as if groaning. “Could some trouble have come upon these people? I must take a look!” And Yelisey went into the cottage.

## IV

Yelisey turned the ring: it was not locked. He opened the door and went through the little entry. The door into the cottage stood unlocked. To the left, the stove; straight ahead, the front corner; in the corner, the icon shelf and a table; behind the table, a bench; on the bench an old woman sits in nothing but her shift, bareheaded, with her head laid on the table, and beside her a boy, thin, all as if made of wax, but with a fat belly, is tugging the old woman by the sleeve and bawling at the top of his voice, begging for something. Yelisey went into the cottage. In the cottage the air was heavy. He looks: behind the stove a woman is lying on a bed. She lies <!-- vol. 25, p. 87 --> face down and does not look up; she only wheezes, and now stretches out a leg, now draws it up. And she is thrown from side to side, and it is from her that the heavy smell comes—evidently she is soiling herself, and there is no one to clean her up. The old woman raised her head and saw a man.

“What,” she said, “do you need? What do you need? There’s nothing here, good man.”

Yelisey understood what she was saying and went up to her.

“I’m a servant of God,” he said. “I came in for a drink.”

“There’s none, I tell you, none. There’s nothing here to take. Go on your way.”

Yelisey began asking questions. “What, is there no one well here, nobody at all, to clean the woman up?”

“Why, there’s nobody; the man is dying out in the yard, and we’re in here.”

The boy had fallen quiet when he saw a stranger, but as soon as the old woman began to speak, he seized her by the sleeve again: “Bread, Granny! Bread!” And he began to cry again.

Yelisey was just about to question the old woman when a peasant staggered into the cottage, made his way along the wall, and meant to sit down on the bench, but did not reach it and collapsed in the corner by the threshold. And he did not try to get up; he began to speak. He tears out one word at a time: he says one, gets his breath, says another.

“And sickness,” he said, “came on us, hungry. Look, they’re dying of hunger!” The peasant pointed with his head at the boy and began to cry.

Yelisey shook the bag on his shoulders, freed his arms, threw the bag down on the floor, then lifted it onto the bench and began to untie it. He untied it, took out bread and a knife, cut a slice, and held it out to the peasant. The peasant did not take it but pointed to the boy and to the girl: give it to them, he meant. Yelisey held it out to the boy. The boy smelled the bread, stretched out, seized the slice with both little hands, and buried his nose in it. Another child, a girl, crept out from behind the stove and stared at the bread. Yelisey gave her some, too. He cut one more piece and gave it to the old woman. The old woman took it, too, and began to chew.

“If only someone would bring water,” she said. “My lips are parched. I meant,” she said, “—yesterday or today, I don’t remember anymore—to fetch some; I fell, I didn’t get there, and the bucket was left lying there, unless somebody has taken it.”

Yelisey asked where their well was. The old woman explained. Yelisey went, found the bucket, brought water, and gave the people a drink. The children ate some more bread with water, and the old woman ate, too, but the peasant would not eat. “My soul,” he said, “won’t take it.” The woman did not get up at all and did not come to herself; she only tossed about on the bed. Yelisey went to the shop in the village and bought millet, salt, <!-- vol. 25, p. 88 --> flour, and oil. He hunted up a little axe, chopped some wood, and began to heat the stove. The girl began to help him. Yelisey cooked a soup and a porridge and fed the people.

## V

The peasant ate a little, and the old woman ate, and the girl and the little one licked the whole bowl clean and tumbled down to sleep with their arms around each other.

The peasant and the old woman began to tell how all this had come about with them.

“Even before that,” they said, “we didn’t live well-off, and then nothing grew, and from the autumn on we began eating up what we had. We ate it all up and began asking from our neighbors and from kind people. At first they gave, and then they began refusing. Some would have been glad to give, but they had nothing. And it got so we were ashamed to ask, too: we owe everybody—money, and flour, and bread. I looked for work,” said the peasant, “and there is no work. Everywhere people are pushing to get work just for their food. You work one day, and then you go about two days like that, looking for work. The old woman and the girl began going far off to beg. Alms were poor; nobody has bread. Still, we fed ourselves somehow; we thought we’d hold out like that till the new harvest. But from spring on they stopped giving altogether, and then the sickness came on us, too. Things got very bad. One day we eat, and two days we don’t. We began eating grass. And whether from the grass or just like that, the sickness came on the wife. The wife took to her bed, and I,” said the peasant, “have no strength. And there’s nothing to get well on.”

“I struggled alone,” said the old woman, “but without food I wore out my strength and grew weak. The girl grew weak, too, and lost her nerve. We sent her to the neighbors—she wouldn’t go. She crept into a corner and won’t go. A neighbor woman looked in the day before yesterday, but she saw that we were hungry and sick, and turned round and went away. Her own husband has gone off, and she has nothing to feed her little children with. So this is how we lay here—waiting for death.”

Yelisey heard out what they said and gave up the idea of going after his companion that same day, and he spent the night there. In the morning Yelisey got up and set to work about the house as if he himself were the master. He kneaded bread with the old woman and heated the stove. He went round the neighbors with the girl to get what was needed. Whatever he looks for, there is nothing; everything has been eaten up: nothing for the household and nothing in the way of clothes. And Yelisey began laying in what was needed: some he would make himself, some he would buy. So Yelisey stayed one day, stayed another, and stayed a third. The little one recovered, began walking along the bench, and kept nestling up to Yelisey. And the girl <!-- vol. 25, p. 89 --> cheered up altogether and helped with everything. She keeps running after Yelisey: “Granddad! Granddaddy!” The old woman got up, too, and went over to a neighbor woman. And the peasant began walking about, holding on to the wall. Only the wife still lay there, and even she came to on the third day and began asking for food. “Well,” thought Yelisey, “I didn’t expect to waste so much time; now I’ll go.”

## VI

On the fourth day the breaking of the fast came round, and Yelisey thinks: “Well, let me break the fast with these people; I’ll buy them a few things for the holiday, and toward evening I’ll go.” Yelisey went to the village again and bought milk, white flour, and lard. He and the old woman cooked and baked, and in the morning Yelisey went to the liturgy, came back, and broke the fast with the people. That day the wife got up, too, and began to move about. And the peasant shaved, put on a clean shirt—the old woman had washed it—and went to the village to a rich peasant to ask for mercy. Both the meadow and the plowland had been pledged to the rich peasant, so he went to ask whether he would not give back the meadow and the plowland until the new harvest. Toward evening the master came back downcast and began to cry. The rich peasant had not shown mercy; he said: “Bring the money.”

Yelisey fell to thinking again. “How,” he thinks, “are they to live now? People will go out to mow, and they have nothing: the meadow is pledged. The rye will ripen, people will set about getting it in (and how well it has come up, the dear thing!), and they have nothing even to wait for: their desyatina has been sold to the rich peasant. If I go away, they’ll sink just the same again.” And Yelisey’s thoughts were split in two, and he did not leave that evening; he put it off till morning. He went to sleep out in the yard. He prayed, lay down, and cannot fall asleep: he must go—he had already spent so much money and time as it was—and he is sorry for the people. “You can’t give to everyone, it seems. I meant to bring them a little water and hand them each a slice of bread, and look where it has got to. Now it’s—buy back the meadow and the plowland. And buy back the plowland—then buy a cow for the children and a horse for the peasant to cart his sheaves. You’ve got yourself tangled up, it seems, brother Yelisey Kuzmich. You’ve come loose from your anchor and you can’t make sense of it!” Yelisey got up, took his coat from under his head, unrolled it, got out his snuff horn, took a pinch; he thought he would clear his thoughts, but no: he thought and thought and thought of nothing. He must go, and he is sorry for the people. And how to manage, he does not know. He rolled up the coat under his head and lay down again. He lay and lay; the cocks had already crowed, and he was quite falling asleep. Suddenly it was as if someone woke him. He sees <!-- vol. 25, p. 90 --> himself as if he were fully dressed, with his bag and his staff, and he has to pass through the gate, but the gate is opened only enough for one to squeeze through. And he goes through the gate and catches his bag on one side; he tries to unhook it and catches his foot-cloth on the other side, and the foot-cloth comes undone. He begins to unhook it, and it is not caught on the wattle fence: it is the girl holding on, crying: “Granddad, Granddaddy, bread!” He looked at his leg, and the little one is holding on to the foot-cloth, and the old woman and the peasant are looking out of the window. Yelisey woke and began talking to himself out loud. “Tomorrow,” he says, “I’ll buy back the plowland and the meadow, and I’ll buy a horse, and flour till the new harvest, and I’ll buy a cow for the children. Or else you go seeking Christ over the sea and lose him in yourself. These people must be set on their feet!” And Yelisey slept till morning. Yelisey woke early. He went to the rich peasant and bought back the rye, and paid the money for the meadow, too. He bought a scythe—that had been sold, too—and brought it home. He sent the peasant to mow, and he himself went round among the peasants: at the tavern keeper’s he found a horse and cart for sale. He bargained, bought them, bought a sack of flour, too, put it on the cart, and went to buy a cow. Yelisey is walking along and overtakes two Ukrainian women. The women are walking and chattering between themselves. And Yelisey hears that the women are talking in their own way, but he makes out that they are talking about him.

“See, at first they didn’t know who he was; they thought: a simple man. He came in, they say, for a drink, and there he stayed and lived. What, what hasn’t he bought them! I saw myself how the other day he bought a horse and cart from the tavern keeper. No, it seems there really are such people in the world. We must go and have a look.”

Yelisey heard this, understood that they were praising him, and did not go to buy the cow. He went back to the tavern keeper and paid the money for the horse. He harnessed it and drove with the flour to the cottage. He drove up to the gate, stopped, and got down from the cart. The people of the house saw the horse and wondered. And they think that he has bought the horse for them, but they do not dare to say so. The master came out and opened the gate.

“Where,” he said, “did you get the horse, Grandfather?”

“Why, I bought it,” he said. “It came my way cheap. Cut some grass, now, to put in the box for it for the night. And take the sack down, too.”

The master unharnessed the horse, carried the sack into the storehouse, mowed an armful of grass, and put it in the box. They lay down to sleep. Yelisey lay down outside and had carried his bag out there in the evening. All the people fell asleep. Yelisey got up, tied up his bag, put on his shoes, put on his coat, and set off on the road after Yefim.

## VII

Yelisey had gone some five versts. It began to grow light. He sat down under a tree, untied his bag, and began to count. He counted: there were seventeen rubles twenty kopecks left. “Well,” he thinks, “with this you won’t get across the sea! And to collect in Christ’s name—there might be more sin in that. My old friend Yefim will get there even on his own, and he’ll set up a candle for me. And as for me, it seems, the vow will stay on me till I die. Thank goodness, the Master is merciful—he will wait.”

Yelisey got up, shook his bag into place on his shoulders, and went back. Only he went right round that village, so that the people would not see him. And Yelisey soon got home. Going there it had seemed hard; at times he had dragged himself after Yefim beyond his strength; but when he went back, God so granted it that he walks and knows no weariness. He walks as if at play, swinging his staff, and covers seventy versts a day.

Yelisey came home. They had already finished in the fields. His household were glad of their old man and began asking him questions: how and what, why he had fallen behind his companion, why he had not got there and had come back home? Yelisey would not tell.

“Why, God did not bring me there,” he said. “I lost my money on the road and fell behind my companion. So I didn’t go on. Forgive me, for Christ’s sake!”

And he gave his old wife the rest of the money. Yelisey asked about the household affairs: everything was well, all the work had been done, nothing had been let slip in the household, and they were all living in peace and harmony.

That same day Yefim’s people heard, too, that Yelisey had come back, and came to ask about their old man. And Yelisey told them the same.

“Your old man,” he said, “went on in fine health; we parted,” he said, “three days before St. Peter’s Day; I meant to catch up with him, but then such things came up: I lost my money and had nothing to go on with, so I came back.”

People wondered: how could a sensible man have acted so foolishly—set off and not got there, and only spent his money? They wondered and forgot. And Yelisey forgot. He set to work about the house: with his son he got in firewood for the winter, with the women he threshed the grain, he covered the sheds, put the bees away, and gave the neighbor the ten hives of bees with their increase. His old wife wanted to hide how many swarms had come from the sold hives, but Yelisey knew himself which were barren and which had swarmed, and instead of ten he gave the neighbor seventeen. Yelisey got everything in order, sent his son off to earn money, and himself sat down for the winter to plait bast shoes and hollow out log hives.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 92 -->

## VIII

All that day, while Yelisey stayed in the cottage with the sick people, Yefim waited for his companion. He went a short way off and sat down. He waited and waited, dozed, woke, sat some more—no companion. He looked his eyes out. The sun had already gone behind the trees—no Yelisey. “Could he have gone past me,” he thinks, “or ridden past (someone giving him a lift), and not noticed me while I was asleep? But he couldn’t have missed seeing me. On the steppe you can see far. If I go back,” he thinks, “he’ll go on ahead. We’ll get separated, and that’s worse still. I’ll go on ahead; we’ll meet at the night’s lodging.” He came to a village and asked the village constable that, if such and such a little old man came, he should be taken to that same cottage. Yelisey did not come to the night’s lodging. Yefim went on farther and asked everyone: had they not seen a little bald old man? No one had. Yefim wondered at it and went on alone. “We’ll meet,” he thinks, “somewhere in Odessa, and on the ship,” and he stopped thinking about it.

On the way he fell in with a pilgrim. The pilgrim, in a monk’s cap and a cassock and with long hair, had been to Athos and was going to Jerusalem for the second time. They met at a night’s lodging, got to talking, and went on together.

They got to Odessa all right. They waited three days and nights for a ship. Many pilgrims were waiting. They were from different parts. Again Yefim asked around about Yelisey—no one had seen him.

Yefim got himself a passport for abroad—it came to five rubles. He paid forty rubles for the passage there and back and bought bread and herrings for the road. They loaded the ship, ferried the pilgrims out, and Tarasych got on, too, with the pilgrim. They raised the anchors, cast off, and sailed out to sea. For one day they sailed well; toward evening a wind rose, rain began, the ship began to pitch and to be washed with water. The people were thrown into turmoil, the women began to wail, and some of the weaker men began running about the ship, looking for a place. Fear came over Yefim, too, only he did not show it: just as he had sat down on the floor when he came aboard, next to some old men from Tambov, so he sat all night and all the next day; they only held on to their bags and said nothing. On the third day it grew calm. On the fifth day they put in at Tsargrad. Those pilgrims who went ashore went to see the church of Sophia the Wisdom, which the Turks now possess; Tarasych did not go ashore; he sat it out on the ship. He only bought a white roll. They lay there a day and a night, then sailed on again by sea. They stopped again at the city of Smyrna, at another city, Alexandria, and sailed <!-- vol. 25, p. 93 --> safely to the city of Jaffa. At Jaffa all the pilgrims go ashore: seventy versts on foot to Jerusalem. The people took fright at the landing, too: the ship is high, and from the ship people are thrown down into boats, and the boat rocks; any moment someone may not land in the boat but miss it; a couple of people got a soaking, but all landed safely. They landed and went on foot; on the third day, by dinnertime, they reached Jerusalem. They put up outside the city at the Russian hostel, had their passports registered, had dinner, and went round the holy places with the pilgrim. To the Lord’s Sepulchre itself there was no admittance yet. They went to the patriarch’s monastery; all the worshipers were gathered there, and the female sex and the male sex were seated separately. They were told to take off their shoes and sit in a circle. A monk came out with a towel and began washing everyone’s feet; he would wash, wipe, and kiss, and so he went round them all. He wiped Yefim’s feet and kissed them. They stood through vespers and matins, prayed, set up candles, and handed in lists of names to be remembered for their parents. Here they were fed, too, and offered wine. In the morning they went to the cell of Mary of Egypt, where she worked out her salvation. They set up candles and had a prayer service sung. From there they went to the monastery of Abraham. They saw the garden of Sabek—the place where Abraham meant to slay his son for God. Then they went to the place where Christ appeared to Mary Magdalene, and to the church of James, the brother of the Lord. The pilgrim showed them all the places and everywhere told them how much money ought to be given where. By dinnertime they came back to the hostel and ate. And just as they were beginning to settle down to sleep, the pilgrim began to moan and to turn over his clothes, rummaging.

“Someone’s pulled out my purse with the money,” he said. “There were twenty-three rubles,” he said, “two ten-ruble notes and three in small change.”

The pilgrim grieved and grieved; nothing could be done—they lay down to sleep.

## IX

Yefim lay down to sleep, and temptation came upon him. “Nobody pulled out the pilgrim’s money,” he thinks; “he hadn’t any, I should think. Nowhere did he give anything. He told me to give, and himself gave nothing, and he even took a ruble from me.”

Yefim would think so and then begin to reproach himself: “Who am I,” he says, “to judge a man? I’m sinning. I won’t think about it.” But as soon as he forgets himself, again he begins to recall how sharp-eyed the pilgrim is about money and how unconvincingly he says that his purse was pulled out. “He never had any money,” he thinks. “It’s all a blind.”

In the morning they got up and went to the early liturgy in the great church <!-- vol. 25, p. 94 --> of the Resurrection, at the Lord’s Sepulchre. The pilgrim does not leave Yefim; he goes along with him.

They came to the church. A great many people—pilgrims and worshipers, both Russians and people of every nation, Greeks, and Armenians, and Turks, and Syrians—had gathered. Yefim came in through the Holy Gates with the people. A monk led them. He led them past the Turkish guard to the place where the Savior was taken down from the cross and anointed and where nine great candlesticks burn. He showed and told them everything. Yefim set up a candle there. Then the monks led Yefim to the right, up the steps to Golgotha, to the place where the cross stood; there Yefim prayed. Then they showed Yefim the cleft where the earth had split open down to the nether world; then they showed the place where they nailed Christ’s hands and feet to the cross; then they showed Adam’s tomb, where Christ’s blood flowed onto his bones. Then they came to the stone where Christ sat when they put the crown of thorns on him; then to the pillar to which they bound Christ when they beat him. Then Yefim saw the stone with two holes for Christ’s feet. They meant to show something more, but the people began to hurry: everyone hastened to the very cave of the Lord’s Sepulchre. Someone else’s service had ended there, and the Orthodox liturgy had begun. Yefim went with the people to the cave.

He wanted to shake off the pilgrim—in his thoughts he keeps sinning against the pilgrim—but the pilgrim does not leave him and went with him to the liturgy at the Lord’s Sepulchre, too. They wanted to stand nearer, but did not make it in time. The people were packed so tight that there was no getting through either forward or back. Yefim stands, looks ahead, prays, and every now and then feels whether his purse is there. His thoughts are double: first he thinks—the pilgrim is deceiving him; second he thinks—if he didn’t deceive, and it really was pulled out, then I’d better see the same thing doesn’t happen to me.

## X

So Yefim stands, prays, and looks ahead into the chapel where the Sepulchre itself is and above the Sepulchre thirty-six lamps burn. Yefim stands looking over people’s heads—what a wonder! Right under the lamps, where the fire of grace burns, in front of everyone, he sees a little old man standing in a coat of coarse homespun, his bald pate gleaming over his whole head, like Yelisey Bodrov’s. “He’s like Yelisey,” he thinks. “But it can’t be him! He couldn’t have got here before me. The ship before ours left <!-- vol. 25, p. 95 --> a week earlier. He couldn’t have got ahead. And he wasn’t on our ship. I saw all the pilgrims.”

Just as Yefim thought this, the little old man began to pray and bowed three times: once forward to God, and then to the Orthodox congregation on both sides. And as the little old man turned his head to the right side, Yefim recognized him. It is he himself, Bodrov—the darkish curly beard, and the gray on his cheeks, and the eyebrows, and the eyes, and the nose, and his whole face. It is he himself, Yelisey Bodrov.

Yefim was glad that he had found his companion, and he wondered how Yelisey had got there ahead of him.

“Well done, Bodrov,” he thinks, “see where he’s squeezed his way to, up front! He must have fallen in with someone who got him through. Let me find him at the way out; I’ll drop my pilgrim in the monk’s cap and go about with him; maybe he’ll get me up front, too.”

And Yefim kept watching so as not to lose Yelisey. But the liturgy ended, the people began to sway, went up to kiss the holy things, pressed together, and squeezed Yefim off to one side. Again fear came over him: what if, he thinks, they pull my purse out? Yefim pressed his purse with his hand and began pushing his way through, if only to get out into open space. He got out into open space and walked and walked, searched and searched for Yelisey, there and in the church. There in the church, in the cells, he saw a great many people of every kind: some eat right there, and drink wine, and sleep, and read. And there is no Yelisey anywhere. Yefim went back to the hostel—he had not found his companion. That evening the pilgrim did not come. He had vanished, and had not given back the ruble. Yefim was left alone.

The next day Yefim went again to the Lord’s Sepulchre with an old man from Tambov; he had come on the ship with him. He tried to squeeze through to the front, but again they pushed him aside, and he stood by a pillar and prays. He looked ahead—again under the lamps, at the very Sepulchre of the Lord, in the foremost place, stands Yelisey, his arms spread wide like a priest at the altar, and his bald pate gleaming over his whole head. “Well,” thinks Yefim, “this time I won’t let him slip.” He started pushing his way through to the front. He pushed his way through—no Yelisey. He must have gone away. And on the third day he looks again at the Lord’s Sepulchre—in the holiest place stands Yelisey, in full view, his arms spread wide, looking upward, as if he sees something above himself. And his bald pate shines with light over his whole head. “Well,” thinks Yefim, “this time I won’t let him slip; I’ll go and stand at the way out. There we can’t miss each other.” Yefim went out and stood and stood; he stood there half the day: all the people went past—no Yelisey.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 96 --> Yefim stayed six weeks in Jerusalem and went everywhere: to Bethlehem, and to Bethany, and to the Jordan, and he had a seal put on a new shirt at the Lord’s Sepulchre, so as to be buried in it, and he took water from the Jordan in a little flask, and took earth, and candles lit with the fire of grace, and in eight places he had names written down for remembrance; he spent all his money, keeping only enough to get home. And Yefim set off back toward home. He reached Jaffa, boarded a ship, sailed to Odessa, and went home on foot.

## XI

Yefim travels alone by the same way. As he began to draw near home, the care came over him again of how they are living at home without him. “In a year,” he thinks, “much water flows away. You spend your whole life getting a household together, and it doesn’t take long to ruin it. How has his son run things without him, how did the spring open up, how did the cattle get through the winter, did they finish the cottage properly?” Yefim came to the place where, the year before last, he had parted from Yelisey. The people could not be recognized. Where the year before last the people had been in want, now everyone lives in sufficiency. The fields had yielded well. The people had recovered and forgotten their former grief. Toward evening Yefim comes up to that very village where, the year before last, Yelisey had fallen behind. He had only just entered the village when a girl in a white shift jumped out from behind a cottage.

“Granddad! Granddaddy! Come in to us!”

Yefim meant to go on, but the girl would not let him; she caught hold of the skirt of his coat and pulls him toward the cottage, laughing all the while.

A woman, too, came out onto the porch with a boy, and she beckons, too:

“Come in, Grandfather, have some supper—you’ll spend the night.”

Yefim went in. “By the way,” he thinks, “I’ll ask about Yelisey: I do believe it was this very cottage he went into that time for a drink.”

Yefim went in; the woman took his bag off him, gave him water to wash with, and sat him down at the table. She got out milk, dumplings, porridge—and set them on the table. Tarasych thanked them and praised the people for making pilgrims welcome. The woman shook her head.

“We,” she said, “cannot help making pilgrims welcome. It was from a pilgrim that we learned life. We lived, we forgot God, and God punished us so that we were all just waiting for death. Last summer it came to this, that we were all lying here—nothing to eat, and sick. And we would have died, but God sent us just such a little old man as you. He came in in the middle of the day for a drink, saw us, took pity on us, and stayed with <!-- vol. 25, p. 97 --> us. And he gave us drink, and fed us, and set us on our feet, and bought back the land, and bought a horse and cart and left it with us.”

The old woman came into the cottage and interrupted the woman.

“And we ourselves don’t know,” she said, “whether he was a man or an angel of God. He loved everyone, he pitied everyone, and he went away without telling, and who to pray to God for, we don’t know. I can see it now: I’m lying here waiting for death, I look—in comes a little old man, nothing much to look at, a bit bald, for a drink of water. And I even thought, sinner that I am: what are they always tramping about for? And look what he did! As soon as he saw us, off with his bag at once; he set it down right here on this spot and untied it.”

The girl, too, put in her word.

“No, Granny,” she said, “first he put the bag down here, in the middle of the cottage, and then he moved it onto the bench.”

And they began to argue and to recall all his words and deeds: where he sat, and where he slept, and what he did, and what he said to whom.

Toward night the master, the peasant, came home with the horse, and he, too, began telling about Yelisey and how he had lived with them.

“If he hadn’t come to us,” he said, “we would all have died in our sins. We were dying in despair, grumbling against God and against people. But he set us on our feet, and through him we came to know God, and came to believe in good people. Christ save him! Before, we lived like cattle; he made people of us.”

The people fed Yefim and gave him drink, put him to bed, and lay down themselves.

Yefim lies and does not sleep, and Yelisey will not go out of his head, how he had seen him in Jerusalem three times in the foremost place.

“So that’s where,” he thinks, “he got ahead of me! Whether my labors have been accepted or not, him the Lord has accepted.”

In the morning the people said goodbye to Yefim, packed some little pies for him for the road, and went off to work, and Yefim—on his way.

## XII

Yefim was away exactly a year. He came back home with the spring.

He came home toward evening. His son was not at home: he was at the tavern. The son came home tipsy, and Yefim began to question him. From everything he saw that the lad had gone to pieces without him. He had spent all the money badly and let the work slide. His father began to rebuke him. The son began to be rude.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 98 --> “You should have done the work yourself,” he said, “instead of going off on your walk and carrying all the money away with you, too, and now you call me to account.”

The old man flew into a rage and beat his son.

In the morning Yefim Tarasych went out to the village elder to speak about his son, and he is walking past Yelisey’s yard. Yelisey’s old wife is standing on the porch and greets him:

“Good health to you, old friend,” she said. “Did you make the journey in good health, my dear?”

Yefim Tarasych stopped.

“Thank God,” he said, “I made it; I lost your old man, but I hear he came back home.”

And the old woman started talking—she was one who liked a chat.

“He came back,” she said, “our breadwinner, came back long ago. Soon after the Dormition, I believe. And how glad we were that God brought him! It’s dreary for us without him. Not that there’s much work in him now—his years have gone. But still he’s the head, and it’s more cheerful for us. And how glad the lad was! ‘Without him,’ he says, ‘it’s like being without light in your eye.’ It’s dreary for us without him, my dear; we love him, oh, how we cherish him.”

“Well, is he at home now, then?”

“At home, dear man, at the apiary, taking in the swarms. ‘A fine swarming,’ he says. God has given the bee such strength that the old man can’t remember the like. ‘God gives not according to our sins,’ he says. Go in, my dear; oh, how glad he’ll be.”

Yefim went through the entry, through the yard, to the apiary to Yelisey. He went into the apiary and looks—there stands Yelisey, without a net, without gloves, in his gray coat, under a little birch tree, his arms spread wide, looking upward, and his bald pate gleams over his whole head, as he had stood in Jerusalem at the Lord’s Sepulchre; and above him, as in Jerusalem, through the little birch, the sun plays, burning like live coals, and around his head the golden little bees have twined themselves into a crown; they circle and do not sting him. Yefim stopped.

Yelisey’s old wife called to her husband.

“Our old friend,” she said, “has come!”

Yelisey looked round, was glad, and went to meet his old friend, gently picking the bees out of his beard.

“Good health to you, old friend, good health to you, my dear man... Did you have a good journey?”

“My feet made the journey, and I’ve brought you a little water from the river Jordan. Come in and take it; but whether the Lord accepted the labors...”

“Well, thank God; Christ save you.”

Yefim was silent a while.

<!-- vol. 25, p. 99 --> “I was there with my feet, but whether I was there in my soul, or someone else...”

“God’s affair, old friend, God’s affair.”

“On the way back I also went into the cottage where you fell behind...”

Yelisey took fright and began to hurry.

“God’s affair, old friend, God’s affair. Well, come into the house, why don’t you—I’ll bring you a little honey.”

And Yelisey smoothed the talk over and began talking about household matters.

Yefim sighed and did not remind Yelisey about the people in the cottage or about having seen him in Jerusalem. And he understood that in the world, until death, God has bidden each one to fulfill his own vow—by love and good deeds.
