## \* VII. [THE RADIANT RESURRECTION OF CHRIST]

On Holy Saturday I spent the evening at a friend's. We became so absorbed in conversation that we did not notice when eleven struck.

“Well—are you definitely going, or not?” asked my friend's young wife as she came in.

“Is it really absolutely necessary to go?” he answered, smiling.

I got up and took my hat.

“Where do you want to go?” he asked. “And are you really going?”

“Of course! To the university, and I am going to dress right now. Please, let us go! Marie and I promised to be there. Really, what sort of thing is it not to meet such a feast in church? I hope even you will go to church?” she added, turning to me.[^1]

“No, I am going to sleep,” I answered. “I do not feel well either, and besides......”

“Well, as you like; you are not my husband. But you, please, Seryozha....”

I shook hands with the two spouses, put on my coat, and went out into the street. Passing through the hall and entryway, I noticed that the floors had been freshly polished, the locks cleaned, and the footman who handed me my coat had pomade in his hair and was especially smoothly shaved. His boots shone and creaked unusually. In the[^2] From above <!-- vol. 5, p. 231 --> there fell either melting snow or a light rain. The conversation with my acquaintance, which his wife had interrupted, occupied me strongly. I walked quickly, mechanically turning from lane to lane in the direction of home and noticing nothing that was happening around me. On Nikitskaya, at an intersection, I nearly collided with two women who, like me, were going around a puddle. “They are already going to the vigil,” I thought, noticing in the half-light of the streetlamp their white skirts beneath their raised dresses and their new shoes stepping with particular care over the glossy wet stones of the street. A little farther on I met a soldier-clerk in a new cap and greatcoat with his wife, whose skirts rustled. The holiday seemed to breathe upon me from that sound, and especially from the sight of the silk kerchief on her head and another new red handkerchief in the hand she swung evenly.

“We should have brought the umbrella, Mikhail Yefremovich,” she said.

Farther on still I met two officials in hats with an umbrella, then an old man with a stick. Two ladies with children. Several droshkies dragged past, dipping into watery ruts; two carriages, their springs rattling and lamps shining, drove along the street.
