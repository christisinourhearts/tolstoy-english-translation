## **THE GOSPEL IN BRIEF**

## PREFACE

This brief exposition of the Gospel is an extract from a large work that lies in manuscript and cannot be printed in Russia.

The work consists of 4 parts.

1) An exposition of that course of my personal life and of my thoughts which led me to the conviction that truth is to be found in the Christian teaching.

2) An investigation of the Christian teaching according to the interpretations of the church in general, of the apostles, of the councils, and of the so-called fathers of the church, and proofs of the falsity of these interpretations.

3) An investigation of the Christian teaching not according to these interpretations, but only according to what has come down to us of the teaching of Christ, ascribed to him and written down in the Gospels; a translation of the four Gospels and their combination into one.

4) An exposition of the real meaning of the Christian teaching, of the reasons why it was distorted, and of the consequences that the preaching of it must have.

This brief exposition of the Gospel is an abridgment of the third part. The combination of the four Gospels was made by me according to the meaning of the teaching. In making this combination I hardly had to depart from the order in which the Gospels are set out, so that in my combination there are not more but rather fewer transpositions of Gospel verses than in most of the concordances known to me and in our Grechulevich four-Gospel harmony.

In the Gospel of John, in my combination, there are no transpositions at all; it is all set out in the same order as in the original.

The division of the Gospel into 12 or into 6 chapters (joining the chapters two into one) flowed of itself from the meaning of the teaching.

<!-- vol. 24, p. 802 --> Here is the meaning of these words:

1) Man is the son of an infinite source, the son of this father not by the flesh, but by the spirit.

2) And therefore man must serve this source in spirit.

3) The life of all people has a divine source. It alone is holy.

4) And therefore man must serve this source in the life of all people. This is the will of the father.

5) Only service to the will of the father of life gives true, that is, rational life.

6) And therefore the satisfaction of one's own will is not necessary for true life.

7) Temporal, fleshly life is food for true life—material for rational life.

8) And therefore true life is outside time; it is only in the present.

9) The deception of life in time—of the life of the past and of the future—hides from people the true life, of the present.

10) And therefore man must strive to destroy the deception of the temporal life of the past and the future.

11) True life is not only life outside time—of the present—but is also life outside personality—life common to all people—and it is expressed by love.

12) And therefore he who lives in the present by the common life of all people is united with the father—the source and foundation of life.

Every two chapters have between them a connection of effect and cause. Besides the 12 chapters, there have been joined to the exposition: an introduction from the 1st chapter of John, in which the writer speaks for himself about the meaning of the whole teaching, and a conclusion from an epistle of the same writer (written, probably, before the Gospels), containing the general conclusion of all that precedes.

The introduction and the conclusion do not make up an essential part of the teaching. They are only general views of the whole teaching. Although both the introduction and the conclusion could have been left out without loss to the meaning of the teaching (all the more since both these parts are written in the person of John, and not of Jesus), I have kept them because, given a simple and rational understanding of the teaching of Christ, these parts, confirming one another and the whole teaching in opposition to the strange interpretations of the church, provide the simplest indication of the meaning that ought to be given to the teaching.

<!-- vol. 24, p. 803 --> At the beginning of each chapter, besides a brief definition of its content, I have also set out the words of the prayer that Jesus taught his disciples to pray, corresponding to each chapter.

Having finished my work, I found, to my surprise and joy, that the so-called Lord's Prayer is nothing other than the whole teaching of Jesus expressed in the most compressed form, in the very order in which I had arranged the chapters, and that each expression of the prayer corresponds to the meaning and order of the chapters.

In the extended exposition of the 3rd part, which is in manuscript, the Gospel according to the four evangelists is translated and set out entire, without the slightest omissions. In the present exposition, however, the following verses have been left out: the conception, the birth of John the Baptist, his imprisonment and death, the birth of Jesus, his genealogy, the flight with his mother into Egypt, Jesus' miracles in Cana and Capernaum, the casting out of devils, the walking on the sea, the withering <!-- vol. 24, p. 804 --> of the fig tree, the healing of the sick, the raising of the dead, the resurrection of Christ himself, and the references to prophecies fulfilled in the life of Christ.

These verses have been left out of the present brief exposition because, containing no teaching, but describing only events that took place before Jesus' preaching, during it, and after it, they add nothing and only complicate and burden the exposition. These verses, however they may be understood, contain neither contradiction of the teaching nor confirmation of it. The only significance of these verses for Christianity was that, to one who did not believe in the divinity of Jesus, they proved it. But for a man who understands how unconvincing the account of the miracles is and who, moreover, does not doubt the divinity of Jesus because of his very teaching, these verses fall away of themselves through their uselessness.

In the large exposition every departure from the usual translation, every inserted explanation, every omission is explained and proved by comparison of the different variants of the Gospels, by contexts, by philological and other considerations. In this brief exposition all these proofs and refutations of the false understanding of the church, as well as the detailed notes with references, are left out on the ground that, however exact and correct the reasonings about each separate passage may be, these reasonings cannot convince anyone of the truth of the understanding of the meaning of the teaching. The proofs of the truth of the understanding lie not in reasonings about separate passages, but in the unity, clarity, simplicity, and completeness of the teaching and in its agreement with the inner feeling of every person who seeks the truth.

With regard in general to all the departures in my exposition from the text accepted by the churches, the reader must not forget that the notion so habitual to us—that the Gospels, all four of them, with all their verses and letters, are sacred books—is on the one hand the crudest error and on the other the crudest and most harmful deception. The reader must remember that Jesus himself never wrote any book, as Plato, Philo, or Marcus Aurelius did; that he never even, as Socrates did, handed on his teaching to literate and educated people, but spoke to those illiterate people whom he met in life; and that only long after his death did people realize that what he had said was very important and that it would not be a bad thing to write down <!-- vol. 24, p. 805 --> something of what he had said and done, and almost 100 years later they began to write down what they had heard about him. The reader must remember that there were very, very many such writings, that many were lost, many were very poor, and that Christians made use of all of them and little by little selected what seemed to them better and more sensible; that in choosing these best Gospels the churches, as the proverb has it, "you can't pick a cudgel without a crook in it," were bound to take in, in what they cut out of the whole enormous literature about Christ, a good deal of crook as well; that there are many passages in the canonical Gospels just as poor as in the rejected apocryphal ones, and that in the apocryphal ones there is something good. The reader must remember that the teaching of Christ may be sacred, but a certain number of verses and letters can in no way be sacred, and such-and-such books cannot become sacred from the first line to the last merely because people say that they are sacred. Only our Russian reader from among educated people, thanks to the Russian censorship, can ignore the 100-year labor of historical criticism and naively say that the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, and Luke, just as they are, were written by the evangelists, each one separately and entire. The reader must remember that to say this in the year 1880, ignoring everything that science has worked out on this subject, is the same as it would have been in the last century to speak of the sun turning around the earth. The reader must remember that the synoptic Gospels, as they have come down to us, are the fruit of slow accretion through copying and adding to, and through the considerations of thousands of different human minds and hands, and in no way works of the inspiration of the holy spirit upon the evangelists. That the ascription of the Gospels to the apostles is a fable that not only does not withstand criticism but has not even any foundation except the wish of pious people that it should be so. The Gospels were selected, added to, and interpreted over centuries; all the Gospels of the 4th century that have come down to us are written in continuous script, without punctuation marks, and therefore even after the 4th and 5th centuries were subject to the most varied readings, and such variant readings of the Gospel books are counted up to fifty thousand. All this the reader must remember, so as not to slip into the view habitual to us that the Gospels, just as they are understood now, came to us in exactly that form from the holy spirit. The reader must remember that it is not only not reprehensible to throw out <!-- vol. 24, p. 806 --> unnecessary passages from the Gospels and to illuminate some passages by others, but that, on the contrary, it is reprehensible and godless not to do this, and to consider a certain number of verses and letters sacred.

Only people who do not seek the truth and do not love the teaching of Christ could have established such a view of the Gospels.

On the other hand, I ask the reader of my exposition of the Gospel to remember that, if I do not look on the Gospels as sacred books that have come down to us from heaven from the holy spirit, neither do I look on the Gospels merely as monuments of the history of religious literature. I understand both the theological and the historical view of the Gospels, but I look at them differently, and therefore I ask the reader, in reading my exposition, not to slip into either the church view of the Gospels or the historical view that has lately become habitual to educated people, a view I did not hold and which I find equally incomplete. I look on *Christianity* not as an exclusive divine revelation, not as a historical phenomenon; I look on *Christianity* as a teaching that gives meaning to life. I was led to Christianity not by theological, not by historical investigations, but by this: at fifty years of age, having asked myself and all the sages of my circle what I am and what the meaning of my life is, and having received the answer: you are a chance cohesion of particles, there is no meaning in life, and life itself is evil—I was led by this, that, having received such an answer, I fell into despair and wanted to kill myself; but remembering that formerly, in childhood, when I believed, life had a meaning for me, and that the believing people around me—the majority of people, those not corrupted by wealth—believe and live a real life, I came to doubt the truthfulness of the answer given to me by the wisdom of the people of my circle, and I tried to understand the answer that Christianity gives to people who live a real life; and I began to study Christianity, and to study in the Christian teaching that which guides the life of people. I began to study that Christianity whose application I saw in life, and began to compare this application with its source. The source of the Christian teaching was the Gospels, and in the Gospels I found the explanation of that meaning which guided the life of all the people who live a real life. But, studying Christianity, I found, side by side with this source of the pure water of life, mud and slime unlawfully joined to it, which alone had hidden its purity from me; side by side with the lofty <!-- vol. 24, p. 807 --> Christian teaching I found, bound up with it, an alien and hideous Jewish and church teaching. I was in the position of a man who had received a sack of stinking mud and only after long struggle and labor had found that in this sack, heaped with mud, there really lay priceless pearls; who had understood that he was not to blame for his disgust at the stinking mud, and that the people who had gathered and kept these pearls together with the mud were not only not to blame but worthy of love and respect; but who still does not know what to do with the treasures he has found mixed with the mud. I was in a tormenting state until I became convinced that the pearls had not grown together with the mud and can be cleaned.

I had not known the light, I had thought that there is no truth in life, but having become convinced that people live only by this light, I began to seek its source and found it in the Gospels, in spite of the false interpretations of the churches. And, having reached this source of light, I was blinded by it and received full answers to the questions about the meaning of my life and of the life of other people, answers that fully agree with all the answers of other peoples known to me and, in my view, surpass them all.

I was seeking an answer to the question of life, not to a theological or historical question, and therefore it was entirely the same to me whether Jesus Christ is God or not God, and from whom the holy spirit proceeded, and so on, and it was equally unimportant and unnecessary for me to know when and by whom each Gospel and each parable was written, and whether or not it can be ascribed to Christ. What was important to me was that light which for 1800 years has illuminated mankind and which illuminated and illuminates me; but what to call the source of this light, and what its materials are, and by whom it was kindled, was all the same to me.

And I began to look closely into this light and to throw away everything that was contrary to it, and the further I went along this path, the more indubitable the difference between truth and falsehood became for me. At the beginning of my work I still had doubts, there were attempts at artificial explanations, but the longer I went on, the firmer and clearer the matter became, and the more indubitable the truth. I was in the position of a man who is putting together a statue broken into pieces. At first there may still be doubt whether this piece is part of a leg or of an arm, but when the legs have been put together, then the piece surely does not belong to a leg, and when, besides this, <!-- vol. 24, p. 808 --> this piece fits with another side piece and coincides with the lower piece along all the lines of the break, then there can no longer be any doubt. This I experienced as I moved forward in my work, and if I am not mad, the reader too will have to experience this feeling in reading the large exposition of the Gospel, where every proposition is confirmed at one and the same time by philological considerations, and by variants, and by contexts, and by agreement with the basic thought.

The preface could have ended there, if the Gospels were books just now discovered, if the teaching of Christ had not been subjected to 1800 years of false interpretation. But now, to understand the true teaching of Jesus as he himself could have understood it, it is necessary to be aware of the chief causes of the false interpretations that distorted the teaching, and of the chief methods of these false interpretations. The chief cause of those false interpretations, which have so disfigured the teaching of Christ that it is hard to see it under their thick layer, consists in this: that from the time of Paul, who, not having properly understood the teaching of Christ, and not even knowing it in the form in which it was afterward expressed in the Gospel of Matthew, joined it to the teaching about the Pharisaic tradition and therefore to all the teachings of the Old Testament. Paul is usually considered the apostle of the gentiles—the apostle of the Protestants. And he was such outwardly, in relation to circumcision, for example. But the teaching about tradition, about the connection of the Old Testament with the New, was brought into Christianity by Paul, and it was precisely this teaching about tradition, this principle of tradition, that was the chief cause of the distortion of the Christian teaching and of the failure to understand it.

From the time of Paul there begins the Christian Talmud, which is called the church, and the teaching of Christ becomes not a teaching that is unique, divine, and complete, [but] one of the links in a chain of revelation that began with the beginning of the world and continues in the church to this day.

These false interpreters call Jesus God; but the recognition of him as God does not make them attach more significance to the words and teaching ascribed to God than to the words of the Pentateuch, the psalms, the Acts of the apostles, the Epistles, the Apocalypse, and even the decrees of the councils and the writings of the fathers of the church.

These false interpreters do not admit any understanding of the teaching of Jesus Christ other than one that would be in agreement with all the preceding and subsequent revelation, so that their aim is not to explain the meaning of Christ's preaching, but only <!-- vol. 24, p. 809 --> to find the least contradictory meaning for all the most impossibly discordant writings: of the Pentateuch, the psalms, the Gospels, the Epistles, the Acts, that is, of everything that is considered holy scripture.

It is obvious that, with such a view of the teaching of Christ, an understanding of it is unthinkable. From this same false view flow also the countless disagreements in the understanding of the Gospel.

Of such explanations, which aim not at truth but at the reconciliation of the irreconcilable, that is, of the writings of the Old and New Testaments, there can obviously be a countless number, and so indeed there are. So that, in order to recognize a given reconciliation as true, only external means remain: miracles, the descent of the holy spirit.

They have reconciled and do reconcile, each in his own way; but each, in his reconciliation, asserts that his reconciliation is the continuing revelation of the holy spirit. Such are the epistles of Paul, and the decrees of the councils, which begin with the formula: "It has pleased us and the holy spirit." Such too are the decrees of popes, of synods, of the Khlysty, and of all the false interpreters who assert that the holy spirit speaks through their mouths. All of them use one and the same crude method of asserting the truth of their reconciliation: by claiming that their reconciliation is not the fruit of their own thoughts but the affirmation of the holy spirit.

Without going into an analysis of these faiths themselves, each of which calls itself the true one, one cannot help seeing that in the method common to all of them—of recognizing an enormous quantity of the so-called writings of the Old and New Testaments as equally sacred—there lies an insurmountable barrier, set up by themselves, to the understanding of the teaching of Christ; and also that from this error flows the very possibility, and even the necessity, of endlessly various hostile sects.

Only the reconciliation of an enormous number of revelations can be endlessly various; the interpretation of the teaching of one person, revered as God, cannot give rise to sects. The teaching of God, who came down to earth to teach people, cannot, by the very purpose of God's coming down to earth, be understood in different ways. If God came down to earth to reveal the truth to people, then the least he could do was to reveal the truth so that everyone would understand it; if he did not do this, then he was not God; and if divine truths are such that even God could not <!-- vol. 24, p. 810 --> make them comprehensible to people, then people can in no way do it.

If Jesus is not God but a great man, then his teaching can still less give rise to sects. The teaching of a great man is great only because it expresses comprehensibly and clearly what others expressed incomprehensibly and unclearly.

What is incomprehensible in the teaching of a great man is not great either, and the teaching of a great man cannot give rise to sects. The teaching of a great man is great only because it unites everyone in one truth for all. Everyone has always understood the teaching of Socrates in the same way. Only an interpretation that asserts that it is the revelation of the holy spirit, that it is the one true interpretation, that all the rest are lies—only such an interpretation gives rise to hatred and to the so-called sects. However much sectarians of every confession may say that they do not condemn another confession, that they pray for it to be joined to them, and that they bear it no hatred—this is untrue. Never has a single assertion of any dogma whatever, beginning with Arius, flowed from anything other than the condemnation of the opposite dogma as a lie. And the declaration that the expression of such-and-such a dogma is a divine expression, of the holy spirit, is the highest degree of pride and stupidity: of the highest pride because nothing prouder can be said than that the words I have spoken were spoken through me by God himself, and of the highest stupidity because nothing stupider can be said than, in answer to a man's assertion that God speaks through his mouth, to say: no, not through yours, but through my mouth God speaks, and he speaks the complete opposite of what your God speaks. And yet this is precisely what all the churches say, and from this alone flow and have flowed all the sects and all that evil which has been done and is being done in the world in the name of faiths. But besides this external evil of sects that such interpretation produces, it has also another important inner defect, which gives all these sects an unclear, indefinite, and dishonest character.

This defect consists in this: that all these sects, having recognized as the last revelation that of the holy spirit, which descended upon the apostles and passed and is passing to the supposedly chosen—these false interpreters nowhere express directly, definitely, and finally what this revelation of the holy spirit consists in, and yet on this supposedly <!-- vol. 24, p. 811 --> continuing revelation they base their faith and call it Christ's.

All the sectarians who recognize the revelation of the holy spirit, like the Mohammedans, recognize three revelations: the Mohammedans—those of Moses, Jesus, and Mohammed; the churchmen—those of Moses, Jesus, and the holy spirit. But according to the Mohammedan faith Mohammed is the last prophet, the one who explained the meaning of the revelation of Moses and of Jesus, and he is the last revelation, which explained all that preceded it, and this revelation every true believer has before him. But it is not so with the church faith. Like the Mohammedan faith, it recognizes three revelations: that of Moses, that of Jesus, and that of the holy spirit; but it does not call itself by the name of the last revelation—the holy-spirit faith—but asserts that the foundation of its faith is the teaching of Christ. So that they preach their own teaching, but ascribe the authority of this teaching to Christ.

The holy-spirit sectarians, who recognize as the last revelation, the one that explains all that preceded it, some Paul, some these councils, some those, some the popes, some the patriarchs, some private revelations of the holy spirit, ought to have said so and called their faith by the name of the one who had the last revelation; and if the last revelation is the fathers, or the epistle of the Eastern patriarchs, or papal decrees, or the Syllabus, or the catechism of Luther or of Philaret, then they ought to say so and to call their faith so, because the last revelation, which explains all that preceded it, always will be the chief revelation. But they do not do this, and instead, while preaching teachings most alien to Christ, they assert that Christ preached these teachings. So that, according to their teaching, it comes out that Christ declared that he had redeemed by his blood the human race fallen in Adam; that God is a trinity; that the holy spirit came down upon the apostles and passed through the laying on of hands to the priesthood; that seven sacraments are necessary for salvation; that communion must be in two kinds, and so on. It comes out that all this is the teaching of Christ, when in the teaching of Jesus there is not a single hint of any of this. These false teachers ought to call their teaching and their faith the teaching and faith of the holy spirit, and not Christ's, because only that faith can be called Christ's faith which is the revelation of Christ that has come down to us in the Gospels, and which recognizes it as the last revelation, as it must recognize it according to the words of Christ: and acknowledge no one as teacher except Christ. It would seem that this is so simple that it would not be worth speaking of, but, strange as it is to say, <!-- vol. 24, p. 812 --> no one until now has yet attempted, on the one hand, to separate the teaching of Christ from the artificial reconciliation of it, justified by nothing, with the Old Testament, and from those arbitrary additions to his teaching which were made and are made in the name of the holy spirit. And strangely, in this error two extreme camps agree: the churchmen and the freethinking historians of Christianity. The first, the churchmen, calling Jesus the second person of the trinity, understand his teaching in no other way than in connection with those supposed revelations of the third person which they find in the Old Testament, in the catholic epistles, in the decrees of the fathers, and they preach the strangest faiths, asserting that these faiths are Christ's. The others, not recognizing Jesus as God, likewise understand his teaching not as it could have been preached by him, but as it is understood by Paul and his other interpreters. Recognizing Jesus not as God but as a man, these interpreters deprive Jesus of the most lawful human right—to answer for his own words, and not for the false interpreters of his words. In trying to explain the teaching of Jesus, these learned interpreters foist on Jesus what he never thought of saying. The representatives of this school of interpreters, beginning with the most popular of them, Renan, without having taken the trouble to separate what Christ himself taught from what his interpreters have pinned on him, without having taken the trouble to understand this teaching any more deeply than the churchmen, try to understand the meaning of the appearance of Jesus and of the spread of his teaching from the events of Jesus' life and the conditions of his time.

And yet it would seem that historians, of all people, could in no way make this mistake. The problem they have to solve consists in the following: 1800 years ago some beggar appeared and said something. He was flogged and hanged, and everyone forgot about him, as millions of such cases have been forgotten, and for some 200 years the world heard nothing about him. But it turns out that someone remembered what he had said and told it to another, to a third. Further and further, and now billions of people, clever and stupid, learned and illiterate, cannot rid themselves of the thought that this man, only this man, was God. How is this astonishing phenomenon to be explained? The churchmen say that this came about because Jesus really was God. And then everything is understandable. But if he was not God, then how is it to be explained that precisely this simple man is recognized by everyone as God?

<!-- vol. 24, p. 813 --> And the scholars of this school diligently search out all the details of the conditions of this man's life, not noticing that, however many details they might find (in reality they have found exactly nothing besides what is in Flavius Josephus and in the Gospels), even if they were to reconstruct the whole life of Jesus down to the smallest details and learn what Jesus ate when and where he spent the night, the question why he, precisely he, had such influence on people would still remain without an answer. The answer lies not in what environment Jesus was born in, who brought him up, and so on, and still less in what was going on in Rome, or in the people's being inclined to superstition, and so on, but only in what this man preached that was so special that it made people single him out from all others and recognize him as God then and now. It would seem that if you want to understand this, the first thing to be done is to try to understand the teaching of this man, and to understand, it goes without saying, precisely his teaching, and not those crude interpretations of his teachings which spread and are spreading after him. And this is precisely what they do not do. These learned historians of Christianity were so glad to have understood that Jesus was not God, and they so want to prove that his teaching is not divine and therefore not binding, that, forgetting that the more they prove that he was a simple man and that his teaching is not divine, the further they will be from an understanding of the question that occupies them, they strain all their forces to prove that he was a simple man and that therefore his teaching is not Divine. To see this astonishing error clearly, it is enough to recall Renan and his disciples. Havet—he naively asserts that *Jesus Christi n'avait rien de chritien*. And Souris—he proves with delight that Jesus Christ was a very coarse and stupid man.

The point is not to prove that Jesus was not God and that therefore his teaching is not divine, and not to prove that he was not a Catholic, but to understand what that teaching consisted in which was so lofty and so dear to people that people recognized and recognize the preacher of this teaching as God. That is what I have tried to do; and for myself, at least, I have done it. And that is what I offer to my brothers.

If the reader belongs to the enormous majority of educated people who were brought up in the church faith but have renounced it because of its incongruities with common sense and <!-- vol. 24, p. 814 --> conscience (whether such a person has kept love and respect for the spirit of the Christian teaching, or, as the proverb says, "angry at the fleas, into the stove with the fur coat," considers all Christianity a harmful superstition), I ask such a reader to remember that what repels him, and what seems to him superstition, is not the teaching of Christ; that Christ cannot be guilty of the hideous tradition that has been woven into his teaching and passed off as Christianity; one must study only the teaching of Christ, as it has come down to us, that is, those words and actions which are ascribed to Christ and which have a teaching significance. Reading my exposition, such a reader will be convinced that Christianity is not only not a mixture of the lofty and the low, not only not a superstition, but is the most strict, pure, and complete metaphysical and ethical teaching, above which human reason has not risen to this day, and within the circle of which, without being aware of it, all the highest human activity moves: political, scientific, poetic, and philosophical. If the reader belongs to that insignificant minority of educated people who hold to the church faith, professing it not for external ends but for inner peace, I ask such a reader, before reading, to decide in his soul the question of what is dearer to him: peace of soul or truth? ## Chapter II GOD IS SPIRIT

### AND THEREFORE MAN MUST WORK NOT FOR THE FLESH, BUT FOR THE SPIRIT. (WHO ART IN THE HEAVENS.)

The Jews who considered themselves Orthodox revered an external God, a fleshly creator. According to their teaching, this external God had made an agreement with them. By the agreement God promised the Jews to help them, and the Jews promised to revere him, and the chief condition of the agreement was the keeping of the sabbath. Jesus rejected the keeping of the sabbath. He said: the sabbath is a human institution. A living man in his spirit is more important than all external rites. The observance of the rite of the sabbath, like all external worship of God, contains a deception in itself. It is impossible to do nothing on the sabbath. A man must always do good deeds, and if the sabbath hinders the doing of a good deed, then it means that the sabbath is a lie.

Another condition of the agreement with God, as the Orthodox Jews held, was not to associate with unbelievers. To this Jesus said that what God wants from people is not sacrifices, but love among themselves.

They held the rules about washing and purification to be a further condition of the agreement. And to this Jesus said that God does not require external cleanness, but requires only mercy and love toward people. And here Jesus said that external rites are harmful and that church tradition itself is evil. Church tradition causes people to cast aside the most important deeds of love, as, for example, love for mother and father, and to justify this by church tradition.

About everything external, about all the rules of the former law that defined the cases in which a man is defiled, Jesus said: know, all of you, that nothing can defile a man from outside; a man is defiled only by what he thinks. ## Chapter III THE SOURCE OF UNDERSTANDING

### FROM THE SPIRIT OF THE FATHER HAS COME THE LIFE OF ALL PEOPLE. (HALLOWED BE THY NAME.)

#### CONTENT OF CHAPTER III

Jesus' disciples ask Jesus what that kingdom of God which he preaches consists in. Jesus answers: the kingdom of God that I preach is the same one that <!-- vol. 24, p. 832 --> John preached. It consists in this: that all people, in spite of any misfortunes of the flesh, can be blessed.

And Jesus says to the people: John was the first to preach to the people a kingdom of God not in the external world, but in the soul of people; the Orthodox went to listen to him but understood nothing, because they understand only what they themselves invent about an external God, and they preach their inventions and are surprised that no one listens to them. But John preached the truth of the kingdom of God within people, and therefore he did more than anyone. He brought it about that from his time the law and the prophets and all external worship of God became unnecessary. From the time of his teaching it was revealed that the kingdom of God is in the soul of people and that every man, according to his strength, can be in the kingdom, in the will of God the father.

To the question of when the kingdom of God will come, Jesus says that the kingdom of God is invisible and is not found in the external, but is in the souls of people. The beginning and end of everything is in the soul of man.

And explaining the meaning of the kingdom of God, Jesus says: Every man, besides his fleshly life, besides his conception, which he understands, from a fleshly father in the womb of a fleshly mother, is conscious in himself of a spirit that is free, rational, and independent of the flesh. It is this spirit, infinite and come forth from the infinite, that is the source of all and that which we call God. We know it only within ourselves. This spirit is the source of our life, and it must be set above everything; we must live by it. Having made this spirit the foundation of life, we receive true, infinite life. That father spirit who sent this spirit into people could not have sent it in order to deceive people, so that people, conscious of an infinite life within themselves, should lose it. If this infinite spirit is in man, then it must give infinite life. And therefore a man who places his life in this spirit has infinite life. A man who does not place his life in this spirit does not have life. People can choose life and death for themselves. Life is in the spirit, death in the flesh. The life of the spirit is good, light; the life of the flesh is evil, darkness. To believe in the spirit means to do deeds of good; not to believe means to do deeds of evil. Good is life, evil is death. God, the external creator, the source of all sources, we do not know. All that we can imagine about him is that he has sown the spirit in people, and sown it as a sower sows, everywhere, not picking out the ground, and the seed that has fallen on good ground grows, and on unsuitable ground it perishes. Only the spirit gives life to people, <!-- vol. 24, p. 833 --> and it depends on people whether to keep it or to lose it. For the spirit, evil does not exist. Evil is a semblance of life. There is only the living and the not living. Evil is the not living. Such is the conception of the whole world of people; but for each man there is the consciousness of the kingdom of heaven in his soul. Each man can, of his own free will, enter it or not enter it. To enter it one must believe in the life of the spirit. He who believes in the life of the spirit has infinite life.

Matt. XI, 2, 3. After this John's disciples came to ask Jesus whether he was the one John speaks of; whether he is opening the kingdom of God and whether he is renewing people by the spirit?

4. Jesus answers and says: look, listen, and tell John whether the kingdom of God has come and whether people are being renewed by the spirit. Tell him how I preach the kingdom of God.

5. In the prophecies it is said that when the kingdom of God comes, all people will be blessed. Well, then, tell him that my kingdom of God is such that the poor are blessed.

6. And that everyone who understands me becomes blessed.

7. And having sent John's disciples away, Jesus began to speak to the people about what kingdom of God John had proclaimed. He said: when you went to John in the wilderness to be baptized, what did you go out to see? The Orthodox lawyers went too, but they did not understand what John proclaimed. And they counted him as nothing.

16. This breed, the Orthodox lawyers, consider to be true only what they themselves invent and hear from one another, and that law which they themselves have invented.

18. But what John said, what I say, they do not hear and do not understand. Of what John said, they understood only that he fasted in the wilderness, and they say: there is a devil in him.

19. Of what I say, they have understood only that I do not fast, and they say: he eats and drinks with tax farmers and debauchees; he is their friend.

17. They are like children in the street, chattering with one another and wondering that no one listens to them.

19. And their wisdom can be seen from their deeds.

8. If you had wanted to look at a man dressed up in rich clothes, why, those men live here, in palaces.

9. So what was it you had not seen in the wilderness? Do you think you went because John was a prophet just like the others? <!-- vol. 24, p. 834 --> Do not think that. John was not a prophet like the others; he was greater than all the prophets. They foretold what may be. He proclaimed to people what is: that the kingdom of God both was and is on earth.

11. Truly I tell you: no man has been born greater than John. He announced the kingdom of God on earth, and therefore he is higher than all.

Luke XVI, 16. The law and the prophets—all this was needed until John. But from John until this time it is being proclaimed that the kingdom of God is on earth, and that whoever makes an effort enters it.

Luke XVII, 20. And the Orthodox came to Jesus and began to ask him how, then, and when the kingdom of God will come. And he answered them: the kingdom of God that I preach is not such as the former prophets preached. They said that God will come with various visible manifestations, but I speak of a kingdom of God whose coming cannot be seen with the eyes.

23. And if they say to you: look, it has come, or it will come, or look, it is here—do not believe them. The kingdom of God is not in time or in any place.

24. It is like lightning—both here, and there, and everywhere.

21. And it has neither time nor place, because the kingdom of God, the one that I preach, is within you.

John III, 1, 2. After this one of the Orthodox, from among the Jewish rulers, Nicodemus, came to Jesus at night and says: you do not tell us to keep the sabbaths, you do not tell us to keep cleanness, you do not tell us to offer sacrifices or to fast; you have done away with the temple; of God you say that he is spirit, and of the kingdom of God you say that it is within us. What sort of kingdom of God is this?

3. And Jesus answered him: understand this: if a man is conceived from heaven, then there must be something heavenly in him.

4. Nicodemus did not understand this and said: how can a man, if he was conceived from the flesh of a father and has grown old, climb into his mother's belly again and be conceived from the beginning?

5. And Jesus answered him: understand what I am saying. I am saying that a man, besides the flesh, is conceived also from the spirit, and therefore every man is conceived from flesh and spirit, and therefore the kingdom of heaven can be in him.

6. From the flesh—flesh. Spirit cannot be born of the flesh: spirit can come only from spirit.

<!-- vol. 24, p. 835 --> 8. Spirit is that which lives in you, and lives freely and rationally, and that of which you know neither the beginning nor the end, and every man feels this in himself.

7. And therefore, why were you surprised that I told you we must be conceived from heaven?

9. Nicodemus said: all the same, I do not believe that this could be so.

10. Then Jesus said to him: what sort of teacher are you, if you do not understand this!

11. Understand that I am not interpreting some kind of mysteries; I am interpreting what we all know, I am affirming what we all see.

12. How will you believe in what is in heaven, if you do not believe in what is on earth, what is in yourself.

13. Why, no one has been in heaven, but there is only, on earth, man, who has come down from heaven and is himself heavenly.

14. It is this very heavenly son in man that must be raised up, so that everyone may believe in him and not perish, but have heavenly life.

16. Why, it was not for the ruin of people, but for the good of people, that God gave people his son, the same as himself. He gave him, after all, so that everyone may believe in him and not perish, but have infinite life.

17. Why, it was not in order to destroy the world of people that he brought forth his son—life—into the world of people, but he brought forth his son—life—so that the world of people might be alive by him.

18. Whoever places his life in him does not die, but whoever does not place his life in him destroys himself by not having relied on what is life.

19. Division (death) consists precisely in this: that life has come into the world, but people themselves go away from life. Life is the light of people. The light has come into the world, but people have preferred darkness to the light and do not go to the light.

20. And therefore whoever does what is bad does not go to the light, so that his deeds are not seen, and he deprives himself of life.

21. But whoever lives in truth goes to the light, so that his deeds are in the light, and he has life and is united with God.

The kingdom of God must be understood not as you think, that the kingdom of God will come for all people at some time and in some place, but thus: that in the whole world always some people, those who rely on the heavenly son of man, <!-- vol. 24, p. 836 --> become sons of the kingdom, and others, who do not rely on him, are destroyed. The father of that spirit which is in man is the father only of those who recognize themselves as his sons. And therefore for him there exist only those who have kept in themselves what he gave them.

Matt. XIII, 3. And after this Jesus began to explain to the people what the kingdom of God is, and he explained it in parables. He said: the father spirit sows the life of understanding in the world, just as a master sows seeds on his field.

4. He sows over the whole field, not picking out which falls where. And so some grains fall on the road, and the birds fly down and peck them up.

5. And others fall on stones, and on the stones, though they sprout, they wither, because there is nowhere to take root.

7. And still others fall in the wormwood, and the wormwood chokes the grain, and the ear comes up but does not fill.

8. And others fall on good ground; these come up and make up for the lost grains, and form ears and fill; and one ear yields a hundredfold, another sixtyfold, another thirtyfold.

So too God has scattered the spirit in people; in some it is lost, and in others it bears a hundredfold. It is these people who make up the kingdom of God.

Mark IV, 26. So the kingdom of God is not such as you think, that God will come to reign over you. God has only sown the spirit, and the kingdom of God will be in those who keep it.

27. God does not rule people, but, like a master, throws the seeds into the ground and himself does not think about them.

28. The seeds swell by themselves, sprout, come up green, form the stalk and the ear, and fill the grain.

29. And only when it has ripened does the master send the sickles to reap the field. So God too has given the world his son—the spirit—and the spirit grows by itself in the world, and the sons of the spirit make up the kingdom of God.

Matt. XIII, 33. As a peasant woman, having put leaven into the kneading trough, mixes it with flour and no longer turns it, but waits for it to rise and swell by itself.

As long as people live, God does not enter into their life; he has given the spirit into the world, and the spirit lives by itself in people and makes up the kingdom of God. For the spirit there is neither death nor evil. Death and evil are for the flesh, not for the spirit.

<!-- vol. 24, p. 837 --> XIII, 24. Here is what the kingdom of God may be likened to: a master sowed good seeds on his field. The master is the spirit, the father; the field is the world; the good seeds are the sons of the kingdom of God.

25. Now the master lay down to sleep, and an enemy came and sowed cockle on the field. The enemy is temptation; the cockle is the sons of temptation.

27, 28. Now the workmen came to the master and say: or did you sow bad seed? A lot of cockle has come up in your field. Send us, we will weed it out.

29. But the master says: no need; or else you will start weeding out the cockle and trample the wheat as well.

30. Let them grow together. The harvest will come, then I will tell the reapers to pick out the cockle, and I will burn it, and the wheat I will put away in the barn.

The harvest is the end of human life, and the reapers are the heavenly powers. And they will burn the cockle, and the wheat will be cleaned and gathered. So too at the end of life all that was the deception of time will vanish, and there will remain only the real life—in the spirit. For the spirit of the father there is no evil. The spirit keeps what it needs, and what is not from it does not exist for it.

47. The kingdom of God is like a dragnet. The net is drawn through the sea and catches every kind of fish.

48. And then, when they pull it out, they pick out the worthless ones and throw them into the sea. So it will be at the end of the age as well: the heavenly power will pick out the good, and the bad will be thrown away.

All these parables, you see, I speak because there are some, like you, my disciples, who alone understand what the kingdom of God consists in; who understand that the kingdom of God is within each man, who understand how to enter it; and others do not understand this. The others look and do not see, and listen and do not understand.

15. Because their heart has grown fat. So I speak these parables in two ways, to both the one and the other. To those I speak of God, of what his kingdom is for God, and they can understand this. But to you I speak of what the kingdom of God is for you—the one that is within you.

18. And you, see that you understand the parable of the sower properly. For you the parable means this.

23. And on good ground—this is he who has understood the meaning of the kingdom and has taken it into his heart. This one bears fruit, one a hundredfold, one sixtyfold, one thirtyfold.

12. For whoever holds, to him much will be given; and whoever does not hold, from him the last he has will be taken away.

Luke VIII, 18. And therefore see how you understand the parables. Understand them so as not to give way to deceptions, offenses, and cares, but so as to bring forth fruit thirtyfold, sixtyfold, and a hundredfold.

Matt. XIII, 31. The kingdom of heaven in the soul grows out of nothing, but gives everything. It is like a birch seed, the smallest of grains; when it grows up, it is bigger than all the trees, and the birds of the sky build their nests in them.
