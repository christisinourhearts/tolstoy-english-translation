# MASTER AND MAN

## I

This was in the seventies, on the day after the winter feast of St. Nicholas. There was a holiday in the parish, and the village innkeeper, Vasily Andreich Brekhunov, a merchant of the second guild, could not go away: he had to be in church—he was the church warden—and at home he had to receive and entertain relatives and acquaintances. But now the last guests had left, and Vasily Andreich began getting ready to drive at once to the neighboring landowner to buy from him a grove he had long been bargaining over. Vasily Andreich was in a hurry to go so that the town merchants would not snatch this profitable purchase away from him. The young landowner was asking ten thousand for the grove only because Vasily Andreich was offering seven for it. And seven thousand was only one third of the grove's real value. Vasily Andreich might have bargained down even further, since the forest lay in his district, and between him and the village merchants of the county an order had long been established by which one merchant did not raise prices in another's district; but Vasily Andreich had learned that timber dealers from the provincial town meant to come and bargain for the Goryachkino grove, and he decided to go at once and settle the business with the landowner. And so, as soon as the holiday was over, he took his own 700 rubles out of the chest, added to them 2,300 of the church's money that he had on hand, so as to make 3,000 rubles, and, having carefully counted them over and packed them into his wallet, got ready to go.

The workman Nikita, the only one of Vasily Andreich's workmen not drunk that day, ran to harness. Nikita was not drunk that day because he was a drunkard, and now, since the eve of the fast, during which he had drunk away his coat and leather boots, <!-- vol. 29, p. 4 --> he had sworn off drinking and was now in his second month without drinking; he was not drinking now either, despite the temptation of the vodka being drunk everywhere in the first two days of the holiday.

Nikita was a fifty-year-old peasant from a nearby village, "no householder," as people said of him, who had lived the greater part of his life not at home but in service with others. Everywhere he was valued for his industry, his deftness and strength at work, and above all for his kind, pleasant character; but nowhere did he stay long, because twice a year, or even more often, he went on a drinking bout, and then, besides drinking away everything he had on him, he also became violent and quarrelsome. Vasily Andreich, too, had driven him away several times, but afterward took him back again, valuing his honesty, his love of animals, and, above all, his cheapness. Vasily Andreich paid Nikita not the 80 rubles that such a workman was worth, but some 40 rubles, which he handed out to him without any reckoning, in small sums, and even then mostly not in money but in goods from the shop at a high price.

Nikita's wife, Marfa, once a handsome, lively woman, kept house at home with a half-grown boy and two girls and did not ask Nikita to come and live at home, first, because for some twenty years now she had been living with a cooper, a peasant from another village, who lodged in their house; and second, because, although she ordered her husband about as she liked when he was sober, she feared him like fire when he got drunk. Once, having got drunk at home, Nikita, probably to pay his wife back for all his sober meekness, broke open her chest, took out her most precious clothes, and, taking an axe, chopped all her sarafans and dresses into small shreds on the chopping block. All the wages Nikita earned were handed over to his wife, and Nikita did not object to this. So now too, two days before the holiday, Marfa had come to Vasily Andreich and taken from him white flour, tea, sugar, and an eighth-pail of vodka, three rubles' worth in all, and had taken another five rubles in money besides, and had thanked him for it as for a special favor, whereas, even at the very cheapest rate, Vasily Andreich owed some 20 rubles.

"Did you and I ever make any agreements?" Vasily Andreich would say to Nikita. "If you need it, take it; you'll work it off. With me it's not like with other people: wait, and reckonings, and fines. We go by honor. You serve me, and I don't abandon you."

And, saying this, Vasily Andreich was sincerely convinced that he was doing Nikita good: so persuasively could he talk, and so thoroughly did all the people dependent on his money, beginning with Nikita, <!-- vol. 29, p. 5 --> support him in this conviction that he was not cheating them but doing them good.

"Why, I understand, Vasily Andreich; I think I serve you, I try, as for my own father. I understand very well," Nikita would answer, understanding very well that Vasily Andreich was cheating him, but feeling at the same time that there was no use even trying to clear up his accounts with him, and that he had to live while there was no other place, and take what he was given.

Now, having received his master's order to harness, Nikita, cheerfully and willingly as always, with the brisk, light step of his goose-stepping legs, went into the shed, took down from a nail there a heavy leather bridle with a tassel and, rattling the rings of the bit, went to the closed stable in which, by itself, stood the horse that Vasily Andreich had ordered to be harnessed.

"What, have you been lonely, lonely, little fool?" Nikita said, answering the faint whinny of greeting with which he was met by the stallion standing alone in the little stall—a well-built stallion of middle height, somewhat sloping in the rump, dark bay, mealy-muzzled. "Now, now! You'll get there in time; let me water you first," he said, speaking to the horse exactly as one speaks to creatures who understand words, and, having wiped with the skirt of his coat the fat back with a groove down the middle, chafed and covered with dust, he put the bridle on the stallion's handsome young head, freed his ears and forelock and, throwing off the halter, led him to water.

Carefully picking his way out of the stable, which was deep in dung, Mukhorty frisked and kicked up, pretending he wanted to strike with his hind leg at Nikita, who was trotting with him to the well.

"Play, play, you rascal!" Nikita kept saying; he knew the care with which Mukhorty flung up his hind leg only so as to touch his greasy sheepskin coat but not to strike it, and he particularly loved this trick.

Having drunk the icy water, the horse sighed, moving his wet, firm lips, from whose whiskers transparent drops dripped into the trough, and stood still as if lost in thought; then suddenly he snorted loudly.

"If you don't want it, you don't need it; so we'll know; don't ask for more later," said Nikita, explaining his conduct to Mukhorty quite seriously and thoroughly; and again he ran to the shed, tugging by the rein the merry young horse, who was kicking up and breaking wind all across the yard.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 6 --> None of the workmen were there; there was only one outsider, the cook's husband, who had come for the holiday.

"Go and ask, dear soul," Nikita said to him, "which sleigh he says to harness: the wide one or the little 'un?"

The cook's husband went into the iron-roofed house on its high foundation and soon came back with the news that the order was to harness the little one. Nikita meanwhile had already put on the collar, tied on the saddle-pad studded with nails, and, carrying the light painted shaft-bow in one hand and leading the horse with the other, was approaching the two sleighs standing under the shed.

"The little one it is, then," he said, and led into the shafts the clever horse, who all the while pretended he wanted to bite him, and with the help of the cook's husband began to harness.

When everything was almost ready and only the reins remained to be put on, Nikita sent the cook's husband to the shed for straw and to the barn for sackcloth.

"There, that's fine. Now, now, don't bristle!" Nikita was saying as he pressed down in the sleigh the freshly threshed oat straw the cook's husband had brought. "And now let's spread the bit of coarse cloth like this, and the sackcloth on top. Like that, like that, and it'll be good sitting," he said, doing what he said—tucking the sackcloth in over the straw on all sides around the seat.

"Thanks, dear soul," Nikita said to the cook's husband. "Two make quicker work." And, sorting out the leather reins, which had a ring at the joined end, Nikita sat down on the driver's ledge and started the good horse, who was eager to go, across the frozen dung of the yard toward the gate.

"Uncle Mikit, Uncle, oh, Uncle!" a seven-year-old boy in a little black sheepskin coat, new white felt boots, and a warm cap shouted behind him in a thin little voice, having run hurriedly out of the entry into the yard. "Let me sit in," he begged, buttoning his little coat as he ran.

"Well, well, run, little dove," said Nikita, and, stopping, he set in the sleigh the master's pale, thin little boy, who was beaming with joy, and drove out into the street.

It was about three o'clock. It was frosty—about 10 degrees—overcast and windy. Half the sky was covered by a low dark cloud. But in the yard it was still. In the street the wind was more noticeable: snow was sweeping off the roof of the neighboring shed, and at the corner, by the bathhouse, it was whirling. Nikita had barely driven out of the gate and turned the horse toward the porch <!-- vol. 29, p. 7 --> when Vasily Andreich too, with a cigarette in his mouth, in a cloth-covered sheepskin coat girded tight and low with a sash, came out of the entry onto the high porch, its snow trodden hard and squeaking under his leather-trimmed felt boots, and stopped. Drawing on the end of his cigarette, he threw it under his feet and stepped on it and, letting the smoke out through his mustache and glancing sideways at the horse driving out, began tucking the corners of his coat collar, fur side in, on both sides of his ruddy face, clean-shaven except for the mustache, so that the fur would not get damp from his breath.

"See what a rogue he is—he's there already!" he said, catching sight of his little son in the sleigh. Vasily Andreich was excited by the wine he had drunk with his guests, and therefore even more pleased than usual with everything that belonged to him and everything he did. The sight of his son, whom in his thoughts he always called his heir, now gave him great pleasure; he looked at him, screwing up his eyes and baring his long teeth.

Wrapped about the head and shoulders in a woollen shawl so that only her eyes could be seen, Vasily Andreich's wife, pregnant, pale and thin, stood behind him in the entry, seeing him off.

"Really, you ought to take Nikita," she said, timidly stepping out from behind the door.

Vasily Andreich made no answer, and at her words, which were evidently unpleasant to him, he frowned angrily and spat.

"You'll be traveling with money," his wife went on in the same plaintive voice. "And the weather might get up, really, by God."

"What, don't I know the road, that I absolutely need a guide?" Vasily Andreich said with that unnatural tension of the lips with which he usually spoke to sellers and buyers, pronouncing each syllable with particular distinctness.

"Well, really, you ought to take him. I beg you by God!" his wife repeated, wrapping her shawl over to the other side.

"She's stuck to me like a bathhouse leaf... Well, where am I to take him?"

"Well, Vasily Andreich, I'm ready," Nikita said cheerfully. "Only they should feed the horses without me," he added, turning to the mistress.

"I'll see to it, Nikitushka; I'll tell Semyon," said the mistress.

"So what, are we going, Vasily Andreich?" said Nikita, waiting.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 8 --> "Well, it seems we must humor the old woman. Only if you're going, go put on some warmer diplomat," Vasily Andreich brought out, smiling again and winking at Nikita's sheepskin coat, which was torn under the arms and in the back, frayed into a fringe at the hem, greasy and matted, and had seen everything.

"Hey, dear soul, come out and hold the horse!" Nikita shouted into the yard to the cook's husband.

"I will, I will!" squeaked the boy, pulling his chilled red little hands out of his pockets and clutching the cold leather reins with them.

"Only don't spend too long prettying up that diplomat of yours—be quick!" shouted Vasily Andreich, grinning at Nikita.

"In one breath, Vasily Andreich, sir," said Nikita, and, quickly flashing the turned-in toes of his old felt boots, which were patched with felt soles, he ran into the yard and to the workmen's hut.

"Come on, Arinushka, give me my coat off the stove—I'm going with the master!" said Nikita, running into the hut and taking his sash down from the nail.

The workwoman, who had had a good sleep after dinner and was now setting the samovar for her husband, met Nikita cheerfully and, infected by his haste, began bustling about as quickly as he did, took down from the stove the poor, worn cloth kaftan that was drying there, and began hastily shaking it out and rubbing it soft.

"Now you'll have room to go strolling with your man," Nikita said to the cook; out of good-natured courtesy he always said something or other to a person when he was left alone with him face to face.

And, passing the narrow, matted little sash around himself, he drew in his belly, lean as it was, and pulled the sash tight over his sheepskin coat with all his might.

"There now," he said after this, addressing no longer the cook but the sash, as he tucked its ends into his belt, "you won't slip out like that"; and, raising and lowering his shoulders to free up his arms, he put the coat on over it, again straining his back so that his arms would be free, beat it down under the arms, and took his mittens from the shelf. "Well, there, that's fine."

"You ought to change your footwear, Stepanych," said the cook, "your boots are bad."

Nikita stopped, as though remembering.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 9 --> "Ought to... Well, it'll do as it is; it's not far!"

And he ran out into the yard.

"Won't you be cold, Nikitushka?" said the mistress when he came up to the sleigh.

"Cold, what cold? It's quite warm," answered Nikita, arranging the straw in the front of the sleigh so as to cover his feet with it, and tucking under the straw the whip, which was not needed for a good horse.

Vasily Andreich was already sitting in the sleigh, filling almost the whole curved back of the sleigh with his back, dressed in two fur coats, and at once, taking the reins, he started the horse. Nikita, as they moved, settled himself in front on the left side and stuck one leg out.

## II

The good stallion, with a light creak of the runners, moved the sleigh off and set out at a brisk pace along the frosty road, beaten smooth through the settlement.

"What are you hanging on there for? Give me the whip, Mikita!" shouted Vasily Andreich, evidently delighted with his heir, who had perched himself behind on the runners. "I'll give it to you! Run to your mama, you son of a bitch!"

The boy jumped off. Mukhorty quickened his amble and, his spleen grunting, broke into a trot.

Kresty, where Vasily Andreich's house stood, consisted of six houses. As soon as they had driven past the last hut, the blacksmith's, they at once noticed that the wind was much stronger than they had thought. The road could hardly be seen any more. The track of the runners was at once swept over, and the road could be told only by its being higher than the rest of the ground. Over the whole field the snow was whirling, and the line where earth meets sky could not be seen. The Telyatino forest, always clearly visible, now only now and then showed dimly black through the snow dust. The wind blew from the left side, stubbornly turning the mane on Mukhorty's arched, well-fed neck over to one side, and blowing aside his fluffy tail, tied up in a simple knot. The long collar of Nikita, who sat on the windward side, pressed against his face and nose.

"She can't go at her real pace, it's snowy," said Vasily Andreich, proud of his good horse. "Once I went to Pashutino with this very one, and he got me there in half an hour."

"Wha'?" asked Nikita, who had not heard because of his collar.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 10 --> "To Pashutino, I say, I got there in half an hour," shouted Vasily Andreich.

"No need to say it—a good horse!" said Nikita.

They fell silent. But Vasily Andreich wanted to talk.

"Well, I suppose you told your missus not to give the cooper any drink?" Vasily Andreich began in the same loud voice, so sure that it must be flattering to Nikita to talk with such an important and clever man as himself, and so pleased with his joke, that it never entered his head that this conversation might be unpleasant to Nikita.

Again Nikita did not hear the sound of his master's words, which the wind carried away.

Vasily Andreich repeated his joke about the cooper in his loud, distinct voice.

"God be with them, Vasily Andreich; I don't go into those matters. So long as she doesn't wrong the boy—otherwise God be with her."

"That's so," said Vasily Andreich. "Well, and what about it—are you going to buy a horse by spring?" he began a new subject of conversation.

"Why, there's no getting out of it," answered Nikita, turning back the collar of his kaftan and leaning over toward his master.

Now the conversation was interesting to Nikita, and he wanted to hear everything.

"The lad's grown up; he has to plow himself, and up to now we've always hired," he said.

"Well, then, take the rumpless one; I won't put a high price on him!" shouted Vasily Andreich, feeling excited and in consequence falling upon the occupation he loved, which absorbed all his mental powers—horse-dealing.

"Or else you give me fifteen rubles or so and I'll buy one at the horse market," said Nikita, who knew that the outside price of the rumpless horse Vasily Andreich wanted to palm off on him was some seven rubles, and that Vasily Andreich, having given him this horse, would reckon it at some twenty-five rubles, and then you would not see money from him for half a year.

"It's a good horse. I wish for you as for myself. On my conscience. Brekhunov wrongs no man. Let me lose what's mine, rather than do like others. On my honor," he shouted in that voice of his with which he talked his sellers and buyers round. "A real horse!"

<!-- vol. 29, p. 11 --> "Just so," said Nikita, sighing, and, convinced that there was nothing more to listen to, he let go of his collar, which at once covered his ear and face.

For half an hour they drove in silence. The wind blew through Nikita's side and arm, where his coat was torn.

He hunched himself and breathed into the collar that covered his mouth, and all of him was not cold.

"Well, what do you think—shall we go by Karamyshevo, or straight?" asked Vasily Andreich.

The way by Karamyshevo was along a busier road, well marked with good stakes in two rows, but it was longer. Straight was nearer, but the road was little traveled, and there were no stakes, or else poor ones, snowed over.

Nikita thought a little.

"By Karamyshevo, though it's a bit farther, is better for driving," he said.

"But straight we only have to cross the little hollow without going astray, and then it's good through the forest," said Vasily Andreich, who wanted to go straight.

"As you will," said Nikita, and again let go of his collar.

Vasily Andreich did just that and, after driving half a verst, turned left by a tall oak branch swaying in the wind, with dry leaves hanging on here and there.

After the turn the wind was almost against them. And snow began falling from above. Vasily Andreich drove, puffed out his cheeks, and blew his breath down into his mustache. Nikita dozed.

They drove on like this in silence for about ten minutes. Suddenly Vasily Andreich said something.

"Wha'?" asked Nikita, opening his eyes.

Vasily Andreich did not answer, and kept twisting about, looking behind and ahead in front of the horse. The horse, its coat curled with sweat at the groin and on the neck, was going at a walk.

"Wha' is it, I say?" Nikita repeated.

"Wha', wha'!" Vasily Andreich mimicked him angrily. "Can't see the stakes! We must have gone astray!"

"Then stop, and I'll look for the road," said Nikita, and, jumping lightly out of the sleigh and getting the whip from under the straw, he went off to the left, on the side where he had been sitting.

The snow this year was not deep, so that one could drive anywhere, but still here and there it was knee-deep and got into Nikita's <!-- vol. 29, p. 12 --> boot. Nikita walked about, feeling with his feet and the whip, but there was no road anywhere.

"Well?" said Vasily Andreich when Nikita came up to the sleigh again.

"There's no road on this side. Have to go walk around on the other side."

"There's something showing black ahead there; go and have a look," said Vasily Andreich.

Nikita went there too, came up to what was showing black—it was earth that had blown from the bare winter-crop fields over the snow and had colored the snow black. Having walked about on the right as well, Nikita came back to the sleigh, beat the snow off himself, shook it out of his boot, and got into the sleigh.

"We have to go to the right," he said decisively. "The wind was on my left side, and now it's right in my mug. Go to the right!" he said decisively.

Vasily Andreich listened to him and bore to the right. But still there was no road. They drove on like this for some time. The wind did not lessen, and snow began falling.

"Well, Vasily Andreich, it seems we've gone right astray," Nikita said suddenly, as though with pleasure. "What's this?" he said, pointing to black potato tops sticking out from under the snow.

Vasily Andreich stopped the horse, who was already sweating and heaving his steep sides heavily.

"Well, what?" he asked.

"Why, we're on the Zakharovka field. See where we've got to!"

"You're lying?" Vasily Andreich responded.

"I'm not lying, Vasily Andreich, I'm telling the truth," said Nikita. "You can hear it by the sleigh, too—we're driving over a potato field; and there are the heaps where they carted the tops. The Zakharovka estate field."

"See where we've gone astray!" said Vasily Andreich. "What's to be done?"

"Why, we have to keep straight on, that's all; we'll come out somewhere," said Nikita. "If not to Zakharovka, then we'll come out at the master's farmstead."

Vasily Andreich obeyed and let the horse go as Nikita said. They drove like this rather a long time. Sometimes they came out onto bare winter-crop fields, and the sleigh rattled over the frozen clods. Sometimes they came out onto stubble, now winter, now spring, through which <!-- vol. 29, p. 13 --> wormwood and straws could be seen from under the snow, swaying in the wind; sometimes they drove into deep, everywhere equally white, level snow, above which nothing could be seen any longer.

Snow was falling from above and sometimes rising from below. The horse was evidently worn out; its coat was all curled and frosted with sweat, and it was going at a walk. Suddenly it broke through and sank into a gully or ditch. Vasily Andreich wanted to stop, but Nikita shouted at him:

"Why hold him! We've got in—we have to get out. Go on, darling! Go on! Go on, my dear!" he shouted in a cheerful voice at the horse, jumping out of the sleigh and sinking into the ditch himself.

The horse plunged and at once scrambled out onto a frozen bank. Evidently it was a dug ditch.

"Where are we, then?" said Vasily Andreich.

"We'll find out!" answered Nikita. "Just keep going; we'll come out somewhere."

"But that must be the Goryachkino forest?" said Vasily Andreich, pointing to something black that had appeared through the snow ahead of them.

"We'll drive up and see what sort of forest it is," said Nikita.

Nikita saw that from the side of the black something dry, long willow leaves were flying, and so he knew that it was not a forest but a dwelling place, but he did not want to say so. And indeed, they had not driven even ten sazhens past the ditch when trees clearly showed black before them and some new, dreary sound was heard. Nikita had guessed right: it was not a forest but a row of tall willows with leaves still fluttering on them here and there. The willows had evidently been planted along the ditch of a threshing floor. Having come up to the willows, which droned drearily in the wind, the horse suddenly raised its forelegs above the sleigh, climbed with its hind legs too onto a rise, turned left, and stopped sinking knee-deep in snow. This was a road.

"Here we are," said Nikita, "but who knows where."

The horse, without straying, went along the snowed-over road, and they had not driven forty sazhens along it when the straight strip of the wattle fence of a drying barn showed black, under a roof thickly covered with snow, from which snow kept pouring without ceasing. Past the drying barn the road turned with the wind, and they drove into a snowdrift. But ahead could be seen a lane between two houses, so that evidently the drift had been blown <!-- vol. 29, p. 14 --> across the road, and they had to drive over it. And indeed, having crossed the drift, they came out into a street. At the end house frozen washing hung on a line, flapping desperately in the wind: shirts, one red, one white, drawers, foot-cloths, and a skirt. The white shirt tore about especially desperately, waving its sleeves.

"See, a lazy woman, or else she's dying—didn't take in her washing for the holiday," said Nikita, looking at the flapping shirts.

## III

At the beginning of the street it was still windy, and the road could be made out, but in the middle of the village it became quiet, warm, and cheerful. At one yard a dog was barking; at another a woman, her coat pulled over her head, came running from somewhere and went into the door of a hut, stopping on the threshold to look at the travelers. From the middle of the village came the songs of girls.

In the village, it seemed, there was less wind, and snow, and frost.

"Why, this is Grishkino," said Vasily Andreich.

"That's what it is," answered Nikita.

And indeed it was Grishkino. It turned out that they had gone astray to the left and had driven some eight versts not quite in the direction they needed, but all the same they had moved closer to their destination. From Grishkino to Goryachkino was some five versts.

In the middle of the village they came upon a tall man walking down the middle of the street.

"Who's driving?" this man shouted, stopping the horse, and, at once recognizing Vasily Andreich, he took hold of a shaft and, moving his hands along it, reached the sleigh and sat down on the driver's ledge.

It was the peasant Isai, an acquaintance of Vasily Andreich's, known in the district as the foremost horse thief.

"Ah! Vasily Andreich! Where's God carrying you off to?" said Isai, enveloping Nikita in the smell of the vodka he had drunk.

"Why, we were going to Goryachkino."

"Look where you've got to! You should have gone by Malakhovo."

"It's not enough that we should have; we didn't hit it," said Vasily Andreich, stopping the horse.

"A good little horse," said Isai, looking the horse over and, with an accustomed movement, tightening the loosened knot of its thick tied-up tail right up to the dock.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 15 --> "Well, then, staying the night, maybe?"

"No, brother, we absolutely have to go on."

"Must need to, then. And who's this? Ah! Nikita Stepanych!"

"Who else?" answered Nikita. "But how, dear soul, could we not go astray here again?"

"How could you go astray here! Turn back, straight along the street, and then, when you come out, keep straight on. Don't bear left. You'll come out on the high road, and then—to the right."

"Where's the turn off the high road? By the summer road or by the winter one?" asked Nikita.

"By the winter one. Right as you come out there are bushes, and opposite the bushes there's another stake, a big one, oak, a leafy one—that's where it is."

Vasily Andreich turned the horse back and drove through the settlement.

"Or else stay the night!" Isai shouted behind them.

But Vasily Andreich did not answer him and kept urging the horse on; five versts of level road, two of them through forest, seemed easy to drive, especially since the wind seemed to have dropped and the snow was stopping.

Having driven again along the street, over the road beaten smooth and dark here and there with fresh dung, and having passed the yard with the washing, where the white shirt had already torn loose and hung by one frozen sleeve, they came again to the terribly droning willows and again found themselves in the open field. The snowstorm not only had not died down but seemed to have grown even stronger. The road was all snowed over, and one could tell one had not gone astray only by the stakes. But even the stakes ahead were hard to make out, because the wind was against them.

Vasily Andreich screwed up his eyes, bent his head, and peered at the stakes, but mostly he let the horse go, relying on it. And the horse indeed did not go astray, but went on, turning now right, now left, along the windings of the road, which it sensed under its feet, so that although the snow from above had grown heavier and the wind had grown stronger, the stakes continued to be visible, now on the right, now on the left.

So they drove for about ten minutes, when suddenly, straight in front of the horse, something black appeared, moving in the slanting net of the wind-driven snow. These were fellow travelers. ## IV

The household into which Vasily Andreich had driven was one of the richest in the village. The family held five allotments and rented more land besides from outside. In the yard there were six horses, three cows, two heifers, some twenty sheep. All the members of the household numbered 22 souls: four married sons, six grandsons, of whom only Petrukha was married, two great-grandchildren, three orphans, and four daughters-in-law with their children. This was one of the rare households still remaining undivided; but in it too the muffled inner work of discord had already begun, starting, as always, among the women, which must inevitably soon lead to a division. Two sons lived in Moscow as water carriers; one was in the army. At home now were the old man, the old woman, the second son, who managed the household, and the eldest son, who had come <!-- vol. 29, p. 20 --> from Moscow for the holiday, and all the women and children; besides the household there was also a guest, a neighbor and godfather.

Over the table in the hut hung a lamp with a shade on top, brightly lighting beneath it the tea things, a bottle of vodka, things to eat, and the brick walls, hung with icons in the corner of honor and with pictures on both sides of them. In the first place at the table sat Vasily Andreich in nothing but his black sheepskin coat, sucking his frozen mustache and looking round at the people and the hut with his bulging, hawklike eyes. Besides Vasily Andreich, there sat at the table the bald, white-bearded old master in a white homespun shirt; beside him, in a fine cotton print shirt, with a huge back and shoulders, the son who had come from Moscow for the holiday, and also another son, broad-shouldered—the elder brother who managed the house—and a lean, red-haired peasant, the neighbor.

The men, having drunk and eaten, were just about to have tea, and the samovar was already humming, standing on the floor by the stove. Children could be seen on the sleeping shelf and on the stove. A woman sat on the bunk over a cradle. The little old mistress, whose face was covered in every direction with fine little wrinkles that puckered even her lips, was looking after Vasily Andreich.

At the moment Nikita was coming into the hut, she had poured vodka into a little glass of thick glass and was offering it to the guest.

"Don't take it amiss, Vasily Andreich, you can't refuse, you must drink to the holiday," she was saying. "Drink it, my dear."

The sight and smell of vodka, especially now when he was chilled and worn out, greatly troubled Nikita. He frowned and, having shaken the snow off his cap and kaftan, stood facing the icons and, as though not seeing anyone, crossed himself three times and bowed to the icons; then, turning to the old master, he bowed first to him, then to all who were at the table, then to the women standing by the stove, and, saying "Happy holiday," began to take off his things, not looking at the table.

"Well, you're all frosted over, Uncle," said the elder brother, looking at Nikita's face, eyes, and beard, all furred with snow.

Nikita took off his kaftan, shook it again, hung it by the stove, and came up to the table. He too was offered vodka. There was a moment of agonizing struggle: he very nearly took the little glass and tossed the fragrant, clear liquid into his mouth; but he glanced at Vasily Andreich, remembered his vow, remembered the boots he had drunk away, remembered the cooper, remembered the boy, for whom he had promised to buy a horse by spring, sighed, and declined.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 21 --> "I don't drink, thank you kindly," he said, frowning, and sat down on the bench by the second window.

"Why's that?" said the elder brother.

"I don't drink, and that's all," said Nikita, not raising his eyes, squinting down at his thin mustache and beard and thawing the icicles from them.

"It's not good for him," said Vasily Andreich, following the little glass he had drunk with a bite of a ring-roll.

"Well, then, some tea," said the kind little old woman. "I'd think you're frozen, poor dear. What are you women fiddling with the samovar for?"

"It's ready," answered a young wife, and, having fanned with an apron the covered samovar, which was boiling over, she carried it with difficulty, lifted it, and set it down on the table with a thump.

Meanwhile Vasily Andreich was telling how they had gone astray, how they had twice come back to the same village, how they had wandered, how they had met the drunken men. "From here a small child could drive to Molchanovka; only you have to hit the turn off the high road right—there's a bush you can see there. And you didn't get as far as that!" said the neighbor.

"You'd go in the morning—that would be the loveliest thing," the old man confirmed.

"Can't, brother—business!" said Vasily Andreich. "Lose an hour, and you won't make it up in a year," he added, remembering the grove and the merchants who might outbid him for this purchase. "We'll get there, won't we?" he turned to Nikita.

"If only we don't go astray again," Nikita said gloomily.

Nikita was gloomy because he wanted vodka passionately, and the one thing that could quench this desire was tea, and tea had not yet been offered to him.

"But we only have to get as far as the turn, and then we won't go astray; it's through the forest right up to the place," said Vasily Andreich.

Nikita said nothing, but only shook his head and, carefully pouring the tea out into the saucer, began warming his hands in the steam, with their fingers always swollen from work. Then, biting off a tiny bit of sugar, he bowed to his hosts and said:

He drank glass after glass, and he was getting warmer and warmer, and more and more comfortable. Petrukha, in his fur coat, was standing with his horse in the middle of the yard and, smiling, reciting verses from Paulson. He was saying: "The storm with mist the sky's a-coverin', the snowy whirlwinds a-twistin', now like a beast she'll be a-howlin', now she'll be a-cryin' like a child."

The old master also thought they ought not to go, but he had already urged them to stay, and they had not listened to him. There was no point in asking further. "Maybe it's from old age that I'm so timid, and they'll get there," he thought. "And at least we'll go to bed in good time. No fuss."

<!-- vol. 29, p. 24 --> Petrukha, for his part, did not even think of danger: he knew the road and the whole countryside so well; and besides, the little verse about "the snowy whirlwinds a-twistin'" heartened him, because it exactly expressed what was going on in the yard. ## V

Vasily Andreich went up to the sleigh, making out with difficulty in the darkness where it was, climbed in, and took the reins.

"Go ahead in front!" he shouted.

Petrukha, kneeling in his low, wide sledge, started his horse. Mukhorty, who had long been neighing, sensing the mare ahead of him, plunged after her, and they drove out into the street. Again they drove through the settlement and by the same road, past the same yard with the frozen washing hung out, which could no longer be seen now; past the same shed, which was now drifted over almost to the roof and from which snow poured endlessly; past the same gloomily rustling, whistling, and bending willows; and again they drove into that snowy sea, raging above and below. The wind was so strong that when it was from the side and the travelers sailed against it, it tilted the sleigh over and knocked the horse aside. Petrukha drove ahead at the rolling trot of his good mare and called out cheerfully. Mukhorty strained after her.

Having driven like this for about ten minutes, Petrukha turned round and shouted something. Neither Vasily Andreich nor Nikita heard because of the wind, but they guessed that they had come to the turn. And indeed Petrukha turned to the right, and the wind, which had been from the side, was again against them, and on the right, through the snow, something black came into view. This was the little bush at the turn.

"Well, God be with you!"

"Thanks, Petrukha!"

"The storm the sky with mist a-coverin'," shouted Petrukha, and vanished.

"See what a poet he is," said Vasily Andreich, and shook the reins.

"Yes, a good lad, a real peasant," said Nikita.

They drove on.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 25 --> Nikita, wrapped up and with his head drawn into his shoulders, so that his small beard covered his neck, sat silently, trying not to lose the warmth he had taken in at tea in the hut. Nikita silently climbed out of the sleigh and, holding his coat, which the wind now pressed against him, now turned back and pulled off him, went clambering about in the snow; he went off to one side, he went off to the other. ## VI

Vasily Andreich in his two fur coats was quite warm, especially after his struggling in the drift; but a chill ran down his back when he understood that they really did have to spend the night here. To calm himself he sat down in the sleigh and began getting out his cigarettes and matches.

Nikita meanwhile was unharnessing the horse. He undid the bellyband and the back strap, took off the reins, removed the tug, took out the shaft-bow, and, never ceasing to talk to the horse, encouraged it.

## VII

Nikita, ever since he had sat down behind the back of the sleigh, covered with the coarse cloth, had sat motionless. He, like all people who live with nature and know want, was patient, and could wait calmly for hours, even days, without feeling either restlessness or irritation. He heard his master calling him, but did not answer, because he did not want to stir and answer. Although he was still warm from the tea he had drunk and from having moved about a great deal, clambering through the drifts, he knew that this warmth would not last long, and that he would no longer have the strength to warm himself by moving, because he felt as tired as a horse feels when it stops and cannot, in spite of any whip, go any farther, and its master sees that it must be fed so that it can work again. One of his feet, in the torn boot, had grown cold, and he could no longer feel the big toe on it. And besides that, his whole body was growing colder and colder. The thought that he might, and even in all probability must, die that night came to him, but this thought seemed to him neither particularly unpleasant nor particularly frightening. The thought seemed to him not particularly unpleasant because his whole life had not been a continual holiday, but, on the contrary, had been a never-ending service, of which he was beginning to grow tired. And the thought was not particularly frightening because, besides the masters like Vasily Andreich whom he served here, he always felt himself in this life dependent on the chief Master, the one who had sent him into this life, and he knew that in dying too he would remain in the power of this same Master, and that this Master would not wrong him. "A pity to leave what you've lived in and are used to? Well, but what's to be done; you have to get used to the new too."

"Sins?" he thought, and remembered his drunkenness, the money drunk away, the wrongs to his wife, the swearing, the not going to church, the not keeping <!-- vol. 29, p. 37 --> the fasts, and everything for which the priest had scolded him at confession. "Of course, sins. But then, did I bring them on myself? It seems God made me like this. Well, and sins! Where can you go?"

So he thought at first about what might happen to him that night, and after that he did not return to these thoughts and gave himself up to the memories that came into his head of themselves. Now he remembered Marfa's visit, and the workmen's drinking, and his refusals of vodka; now the present journey, and Taras's hut, and the talk about divisions; now his boy, and Mukhorty, who would be getting warm now under the horse-cloth; now his master, who was now making the sleigh creak as he turned over in it. "He too, I'd think, poor dear, isn't glad he came," he thought. "With a life like his you don't want to die. Not like the likes of us." And all these memories began to interweave and mingle in his head, and he fell asleep.

But when Vasily Andreich, mounting the horse, rocked the sleigh, and the back against which Nikita was leaning with his back jerked away altogether and the runner struck him in the back, he woke up and willy-nilly was forced to change his position. Straightening his legs with difficulty and shaking the snow off them, he got up, and at once an agonizing cold pierced his whole body. Understanding what was happening, he wanted Vasily Andreich to leave him the sackcloth, which the horse did not need now, so as to cover himself with it, and he shouted to him about it.

But Vasily Andreich did not stop and disappeared in the snow dust.

Left alone, Nikita considered for a minute what he should do. To go and look for a dwelling he felt he had no strength. To sit down in his old place was no longer possible—it was all covered with snow. And in the sleigh, he felt, he would not get warm, because he had nothing to cover himself with, and his own kaftan and sheepskin coat no longer warmed him at all. He was as cold as if he were in nothing but his shirt. He felt afraid. "Father, Heavenly Father!" he said, and the consciousness that he was not alone, but that someone heard him and would not abandon him, calmed him. He sighed deeply and, without taking the coarse cloth off his head, climbed into the sleigh and lay down in it in his master's place.

## VIII

Meanwhile Vasily Andreich, with his feet and with the ends of the reins, was driving the horse toward where, for some reason, he had supposed the forest and the watchman's hut to be. ## IX

Having stumbled his way to the sleigh, Vasily Andreich took hold of it and stood like that, motionless, for a long time, trying to calm down and get his breath. Nikita was not in his former place, but in the sleigh lay something already drifted over with snow, and Vasily Andreich guessed that it was Nikita. Vasily Andreich's fear had now passed completely, and if he feared anything, it was only that terrible state of fear which he had experienced on the horse, and especially when <!-- vol. 29, p. 41 --> he was left alone in the drift. He had at all costs not to let this fear come near him, and in order not to let it, he had to do something, to busy himself with something. And so the first thing he did was to stand with his back to the wind and open his coat. Then, as soon as he had got his breath a little, he shook the snow out of his boots and out of his left glove—the right one was hopelessly lost and must be somewhere already two quarter-arshins under the snow; then once more he girded himself tight and low with his sash, as he used to pull himself in when he went out of the shop to buy the grain the peasants brought on their carts, and made ready for action. The first task that presented itself to him was to free the horse's leg. Vasily Andreich did so and, having freed the rein, tied Mukhorty again to the iron clamp at the front of the sleigh, in his old place, and was going round behind the horse to set right the breeching, the saddle-pad, and the sackcloth on it, when at that moment he saw that something stirred in the sleigh, and from under the snow with which it was covered Nikita's head rose up. Evidently with great effort, Nikita, who was already freezing, raised himself and sat up, waving his hand before his nose in a strange way, as though driving away flies. He was waving his hand and saying something, calling him, as it seemed to Vasily Andreich. Vasily Andreich left the sackcloth without setting it right and went up to the sleigh.

"What is it?" he asked. "What are you saying?"

"I'm dy-dy-dying, that's what," Nikita brought out with difficulty, in a broken voice. "My earnings, give them to the boy or to the wife; it's all the same."

"Why, what, are you frozen?" asked Vasily Andreich.

"I feel—it's my death... forgive me, for Christ's sake..." said Nikita in a weeping voice, still waving his hands before his face as though brushing away flies.

Vasily Andreich stood silent and motionless for half a minute; then suddenly, with the same decisiveness with which he struck hands on a profitable purchase, he stepped back a pace, turned up the sleeves of his coat, and with both hands began raking the snow off Nikita and out of the sleigh. Having raked out the snow, Vasily Andreich hastily undid his sash, opened out his coat, and, pushing Nikita down, lay on top of him, covering him not only with his coat but with the whole of his warm, heated body. Tucking with his hands the skirts of his coat between the bast of the sleigh and Nikita, and gripping its hem with his knees, Vasily Andreich lay like that face down, his head pressed against the bast <!-- vol. 29, p. 42 --> of the front of the sleigh, and now he no longer heard either the movements of the horse or the whistling of the storm, but only listened to Nikita's breathing. At first Nikita lay motionless a long time; then he sighed loudly and stirred.

"There, you see, and you say you're dying. Lie still, get warm; this is how we..." Vasily Andreich began.

But, to his great surprise, he could not go on speaking, because tears came into his eyes and his lower jaw began to jump rapidly. He stopped speaking and only swallowed what was rising in his throat. "I've had a fright, it seems; I've gone quite weak," he thought of himself. But this weakness of his was not only not unpleasant to him, but gave him some special joy he had never yet experienced.

"This is how we," he said to himself, feeling some special, solemn tenderness. Rather a long time he lay like that in silence, wiping his eyes on the fur of his coat and tucking under his knees the right skirt of his coat, which the wind kept turning back.

But he wanted so passionately to tell someone about his joyful state.

"Mikita!" he said.

"It's good, warm," came the answer from below.

"So it is, brother; I was nearly lost. And you would have frozen, and I would have..."

But here his cheekbones began to tremble again, and his eyes again filled with tears, and he could not go on speaking.

"Well, never mind," he thought. "I know what I know about myself."

And he fell silent. So he lay a long time.

He was warm underneath from Nikita, and warm on top from his coat; only his hands, with which he held the skirts of his coat at Nikita's sides, and his legs, off which the wind kept turning back the coat, were beginning to freeze. Especially his right hand without the glove was freezing. But he did not think either of his legs or of his hands, but thought only of how to warm the peasant lying under him.

Several times he glanced at the horse and saw that its back was uncovered and the sackcloth and breeching were lying on the snow, that he ought to get up and cover the horse, but he could not bring himself to leave Nikita for a minute and break the joyful state he was in. He felt no fear at all now.

"Never fear, he won't slip away," he said to himself about warming the peasant, with the same boastfulness with which he spoke of his buying and selling.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 43 --> So Vasily Andreich lay one hour, and a second, and a third, but he did not see how the time passed. At first impressions of the snowstorm, of the shafts and the horse under the shaft-bow, shaking before his eyes, rushed through his imagination, and he remembered Nikita lying under him; then memories of the holiday began to mingle with these, of his wife, the district police officer, the candle box, and again of Nikita lying under this box; then there began to appear to him peasants selling and buying, and white walls, and houses roofed with iron, under which Nikita lay; then all this mingled, one thing went into another, and, like the colors of the rainbow uniting into one white light, all the different impressions came together into one nothing, and he fell asleep. He slept a long time without dreams, but before dawn dreams appeared again. It seemed to him that he was standing, as it were, by the candle box, and Tikhon's wife was asking him for a five-kopeck candle for the holiday, and he wants to take a candle and give it to her, but his hands will not lift; they are clamped in his pockets. He wants to go round the box, and his feet will not move, and his galoshes, new, polished, have grown fast to the stone floor, and you can't lift them or get out of them. And suddenly the candle box becomes not a candle box but a bed, and Vasily Andreich sees himself lying on his belly on the candle box, that is, on his bed, in his own house. And he is lying on the bed and cannot get up, and he has to get up, because Ivan Matveich, the district police officer, will come for him at any moment, and with Ivan Matveich he has to go either to bargain for the grove or to set right the breeching on Mukhorty. And he asks his wife: "Well, Mikolavna, hasn't he come?" "No," she says, "he hasn't come." And he hears someone driving up to the porch. It must be him. No, past. "Mikolavna, oh, Mikolavna, what, still not here?" "Not here." And he lies on the bed and still cannot get up, and keeps waiting, and this waiting is both eerie and joyful. And suddenly the joy is fulfilled: the one he was waiting for comes, and it is no longer Ivan Matveich, the district police officer, but someone else, but the very one he is waiting for. He has come and is calling him, and this one, the one who is calling him, is the very one who called out to him and told him to lie down on Nikita. And Vasily Andreich is glad that this someone has come for him. "I'm coming!" he cries joyfully, and this cry wakes him. And he wakes, but wakes not at all the same as he was when he fell asleep. He wants to get up—and cannot; wants to move his hand—cannot; his leg—cannot either. He wants to turn his head—and cannot do that either. And he is surprised; but is not at all grieved by it. He understands that this is death, and is not at all grieved <!-- vol. 29, p. 44 --> by this either. And he remembers that Nikita is lying under him and that he has got warm and is alive, and it seems to him that he is Nikita and Nikita is he, and that his life is not in himself but in Nikita. He strains his hearing and hears breathing, even Nikita's faint snoring. "Nikita is alive, and that means I am alive too," he says to himself in triumph.

And he remembers the money, the shop, the house, the buying, the selling, and the Mironovs' millions; it is hard for him to understand why this man, who was called Vasily Brekhunov, busied himself with all that he busied himself with. "Well, of course, he didn't know what it was all about," he thinks about Vasily Brekhunov. "He didn't know, but now I know. Now there's no mistake. *Now I know.*" And again he hears the call of the one who had already called out to him. "I'm coming, I'm coming!" his whole being says joyfully, tenderly. And he feels that he is free, and nothing holds him any longer.

And Vasily Andreich saw and heard and felt nothing more in this world.

All around, the snow smoked just the same. The same whirlwinds of snow went round, covering the fur coat of the dead Vasily Andreich, and all of trembling Mukhorty, and the sleigh, now barely visible, and, deep inside it, lying under his now dead master, Nikita, who had got warm.

## X

Toward morning Nikita woke. He was woken by the cold, which had again begun to get through to his back. He had dreamed that he was driving from the mill with a cartload of his master's flour and, crossing a stream, had gone off past the bridge and got the cart stuck. And he sees that he has crawled under the cart and is lifting it, straightening his back. But here is a strange thing! The cart does not move and has stuck to his back, and he can neither lift the cart nor get out from under it. It has crushed the whole small of his back. And cold too! Evidently he has to crawl out. "That'll do," he says to someone, to the one who is pressing his back with the cart. "Take out the sacks!" But the cart presses him colder and colder, and suddenly something peculiar knocks, and he wakes up completely and remembers everything. The cold cart is his dead, frozen master lying on him. And the knock was Mukhorty, who had struck the sleigh twice with his hoof.

"Andreich, oh, Andreich!" Nikita calls cautiously to his master, already sensing the truth, straining his back.

But Andreich does not respond, and his belly and legs are hard and cold and heavy, like weights.

<!-- vol. 29, p. 45 --> "He must have passed away. The Kingdom of Heaven be his!" thinks Nikita.

He turns his head, digs through the snow in front of him with his hand, and opens his eyes. It is light; the wind whistles in the shafts just the same, and the snow falls just the same, with this difference only, that it no longer lashes against the bast of the sleigh but soundlessly covers the sleigh and horse higher and higher, and neither the movement nor the breathing of the horse can be heard any more. "He must have frozen too," thinks Nikita about Mukhorty. And indeed, those blows of the hoof against the sleigh that had woken Nikita were the dying efforts of the now completely frozen Mukhorty to stay on his feet.

"Lord, Father, it seems you're calling me too," Nikita says to himself. "Thy holy will be done. But it's eerie. Well, a man can't die twice, and he can't escape dying once. Only let it be quicker..." And again he hides his hand, closing his eyes, and sinks into oblivion, fully sure that now he is certainly and completely dying.

It was only at dinnertime the next day that peasants dug out Vasily Andreich and Nikita with shovels, 30 sazhens from the road and half a verst from the village.

The snow had drifted higher than the sleigh, but the shafts and the kerchief on them could still be seen. Mukhorty, belly-deep in snow, with the breeching and sackcloth slipped off his back, stood all white, his dead head pressed against his stiffened throat; his nostrils were frozen into icicles, his eyes frosted over and frozen as though with tears. He had grown so thin in one night that only skin and bones were left on him. Vasily Andreich had frozen stiff like a frozen carcass, and as his legs had been spread apart, so, splayed out, he was rolled off Nikita. His bulging hawk's eyes had frozen over, and his open mouth under the clipped mustache was packed with snow. But Nikita was alive, though frostbitten all over. When Nikita was roused, he was sure that now he had already died and that what was happening to him now was happening no longer in this world but in the other. But when he heard the shouting peasants who were digging him out and rolling the stiffened Vasily Andreich off him, he was at first surprised that in the other world peasants shout in just the same way and the body is just the same; but when he understood that he was still here, in this world, he was grieved by it rather than glad, especially when he felt that the toes on both his feet were frostbitten.

Nikita lay in the hospital for two months. Three toes were taken off, but the rest healed, so that he could work, and he went on living for another twenty years—first as a workman, and then, <!-- vol. 29, p. 46 --> in his old age, as a watchman. He died only this year, at home, as he had wished, under the icons and with a lighted wax candle in his hands. Before his death he asked forgiveness of his old woman and forgave her for the cooper; he also said goodbye to his boy and his grandchildren, and died truly rejoicing that by his death he was relieving his son and daughter-in-law of the burden of an extra mouth to feed, and that he himself was now really passing from this life, of which he had grown weary, into that other life which with every year and hour had become more and more understandable and alluring to him. Is it better or worse for him there, where he woke after this real death? Was he disappointed, or did he find there just what he expected?—we shall all soon know.
