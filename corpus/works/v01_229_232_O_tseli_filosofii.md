## *VIII. ON THE AIM OF PHILOSOPHY*

a)

A person strives, that is, a person is active.—Where is this activity directed? How can this activ[ity] be made free?—this is the aim of philosophy in the true meaning of the word. In other words, philosophy is the science of life.

In order to define the science itself more exactly, one must define the striving that gives u[s] a conception of it.—

The striving that is found in everything that exists and in the human being is consciousness of life and the striving to preserve and increase it.—

By the increase of life I mean a person’s striving to have more impressions, or what we call happiness, well-being.—To satisfy this striving for happiness a person should not try to seek happiness in the external world, that is, seek it in accidental pleasant impressions of the external world, but in forming oneself so that every impression acts upon me as I wish.

—In the opposite case a person will resemble a madman who wants to pull a house over himself inst[ead] [of] going into it.—And so the aim of philosophy is to show how a person should form himself.—But a person is not alone; he lives in society, consequen[tly] philosophy must define a person’s relations to other people.—If everyone strove toward his own good, seeking it outside himself, the interests of private individuals could meet, and hence disorder.

But if every person strives toward his own improvement, order can in no way be disturbed, for everyone will do for another what he wishes another to do for him.—

<!-- vol. 1, p. 230 -->

For the knowledge of philosophy, that is, the knowledge of how to direct the natural striving toward well-being implanted in every person, one must develop and comprehend that faculty by which a person can limit natural striving, that is, the will, and then all the faculties of a person for attaining the good.—(Psychology.)

The method for the knowledge of speculative philosophy consists in the study of psychology and the laws of nature, in the development of the intellectual faculties (Mathemat.), in exercises for ease in expressing thoughts, namely [?] in definitions.—

The method for the study of practical philosophy consists in the analysis of all questions encountered in private life, in the exact fulfillment: of the rules of morality, in following the laws of nature.—

b) Definition of definition.

The definition of a concept is: the replacement of the defined concept by the simpler concepts of which it consists. This operation is called analysis.—By means of the analysis of any concept whatever one can go from the most complex to the most abstract, that is, to one that cannot be defined.—A concept of this kind is called consciousness. What then is consciousness? Consciousness is the concept of oneself—in other words—I.

c) On reasonings concerning the future life.

Everything that exists cannot cease to exist without a cause sufficient for this, and everything that did not exist cannot begin to exis[t] without a cause. The soul exists; consequently it cannot cease to exis[t] without a cause; but what constitutes the essence of the soul?

Consciousness of I...—What is consciousness? The representation of I as existing in various degrees of activity or move[ment].

What faculty represents I as ex[isting] in diff[erent] deg[rees] of move[ment]? Memory. We see that memory depends entirely upon the body, so that damage in one part of a person destroys this faculty; consequently death must completely destroy it. And so [there remains] only consciousness of I, [with] which a person appears in this [world]. As for hypotheses concerning a more exact determi[nation] of the future life, these hypotheses will always remain delirium.—

<!-- vol. 1, p. 231 -->

d) Method.

The aim of philosophy says that the sole striving of a person should be toward the formation of himself.—In order to form oneself, one must first know what I is—and what true formation is; for this one must have a method, namely: 1) to subject all bodily needs to the will; 2) to subject the faculty of reproduction to the will and develop it according to the following rules: remember every thought that you find good for a whole week and then write it down, and every evening repeat all thought[s].

—Exercises: study Mathematics.—3) subject the understanding, or faculty of inference, to the will and develop it according to the following rules. 1) Define every concept, that is, in place of one narrow concept insert two broader ones signifying the same thing, then define both these concepts, finally reach concepts of such a kind that they cannot have definitions, but which we are conscious of because they are nothing other than necess[ary] signs of the I itself.

—2) However new thoughts may be acquired, always follow the course of thoughts and note how many and what kinds of ways of thinking there are.—Exercises—Mathematics and disputation.

At the same time, do not lose sight of the true aim of philosophy and occupy oneself with the knowledge only of those truths that are necessary for this aim, not letting pass a single thought of whose truth [you are] convinced, so that you can convey it in words. 4) the faculty of will; its development will consist in the habit of rul[ing] over all the faculties and in activ[ity].—

Under the concept of the method of philosophy in general one must also understand the method of expressing thoughts. The rules for this will be the following. 1) Do not write a proposition on paper until you have found it fully within yourself in relation to what precedes and what follows.

Exercises: definitions and verses.

e)

Having found two fundamental parts or principles in something, I conclude that in everything there are two fundamental principles, which I understand because they are completely opposed; but if they were united, the result would be zero. The concept of negation arises from the fact that each of these principles acts in infinitely different ways—that is,

it has an infin[ite] num[ber] of degrees; consequen[tly] I can represent an infin[itely] small degree of movement, which is negation; in essence, however, the infinitely small movement of both principles can exist only in union (unlimited inactiv[ity]).

Having not yet found the tru[th] rationally, we can have a presentiment of it, and therefore I wish to define the rules of life.—They may be divided into two parts: bodily and intellect[ual], while the mover of both is the will.—1) eat, drink, sleep,[^1]—only one must not lose sight of the aim in every action, must not depart from generally accepted rules, avoiding occasions to do evil.—
