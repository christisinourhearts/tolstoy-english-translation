## \* IV. NOTES OF A HUSBAND

Here I am alone again, and alone there where I was both young and a child, where I was stupid and vile, and good and unhappy, and where I was happy, where I lived, truly lived once in my life for the space of twenty days. And these [20] days alone burn before me like the sun and still burn my selfish, vile heart with the fire of memories. Now God knows what I am, and what I am doing, and why everything around me is still the same, and time still runs around me in the same way, not carrying me with it, not even moving me. It runs, *and I* stand—and I do not stand, but vilely, lazily, aimlessly lie about in the midst of the same external life, without strength, without hopes, without desires, with one terrible knowledge—with knowledge of myself, of my weakness, worn-outness, and incurable coldness. I am not dear to myself in the least, from any side; I am not precious to myself, and I am not hateful to myself; I am worse than all that: I am uninteresting to myself, I bore myself, I know beforehand everything I will do, and everything I do will be vulgar, old, joyless. I have tried to break out too from this old, dusty, stale, rotting, enchanted circle of *self*, in which to me[^1] *my* own exclusive color, image, and smell. Only I could do this and do it in this way. Everything is the same, everything is the same. If I shot myself or hanged myself, which I sometimes think about as soberly as about whether to go into town, even that I would do not as the soldier did who hanged himself last year in the castle,[^2]

No, there is no escaping from oneself and one's past!

Freedom is not for man. Every second that I live, that I live against my will, fetters the future. And there is already less life left ahead than behind. The whole future is no longer mine. So bow down, submit, and bear the chains you forged for yourself. Yes, easy to say; but if only two moments of life remained to me, I would use even them to struggle painfully with this past, and would try to break out into light and freedom and at least once breathe the pure air freely and independently and look upon the unclouded, unconstricted, unslandered, but great, clear, and lovely world of God. —

It is true, I do not yet have the right to complain and weep; I had[^3]

My life is lived out, if those thirty-three years in which I existed were life; there is nothing for me to do; I am forever chained in the actual world. There remains only the moral world, in which I can be free. I want, taking advantage of those moments of exaltation in which I am free, to tell the *story of my life* and those events, the simplest and most ordinary events, that brought me to my present position. —

## I.

The first view of life and the first ideals.

Since I can remember myself, what were my first ideals? What did I desire? What was I proud of? Wealth and power were my ideals; I desired them and was proud of them. I remember I was three years old; my father was giving orders in the study while all of us—mother, aunt, I, and sister—sat at tea in the drawing room.
