## **UNPUBLISHED, UNFINISHED, AND INCOMPLETE**

## **V.** ***[ON THE CHARACTER OF THOUGHT IN YOUTH AND OLD AGE.]***

When we are young and do not know all the countless sides of life, we think a great deal without meeting the obstacles and objections of life itself, which we do not know; we draw complicated conclusions, we go far along the path of thought; we are clever and know it—and this is even true. But with the years (if we have not been lazy in spirit), we come to know many new sides of life, many different points of view, countless objections along the path of thought, and at every step we come up against what is incomprehensible, insoluble, immeasurable. We think little and contemplate much.

They say that people grow stupid with age.

The thoughts of youth are expressed in the logical sequence of words; the thoughts of old age are higher and therefore outside the realm of words. Only in youth are plans possible for remaking people—through education, through a new economic or civil organization of society; in old age a person feels over himself that inexorable law of nature which rules the plant and the animal, and contemplates it. The whole labor of life has brought the old man to this. Has he become more stupid or more intelligent than he was in youth? More stupid because he has now come closer to the animal and the plant, but wiser because he is becoming a conscious animal and plant.

He who has ears, let him hear! Christ the enthusiast says this formula whenever he rises to that height of thought where one must not think, not argue—that is, not criticize and object—but with the whole <ins>every</ins> soul seek and catch the truth, however unclearly it may be expressed, when truth is so high that it fits awkwardly into the frame of human words.—
