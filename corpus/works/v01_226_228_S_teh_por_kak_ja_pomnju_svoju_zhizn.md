## *VII. [UNTITLED FRAGMENT]

### [FIRST VARIANT]

Since I can remember my life, I have always found in myself some force of truth, some striving that was not satisfied; everywhere there were only contradictions, only insignificance.

The more I lived, the more unbearable it became for me.

[1 illegible] it was impossible to stop. I began making rules for myself on certain subjects, found them useful, but their unsatisfactoriness soon became apparent. I made others—the same thing.—Finally, examining the rules, I found relations among them and understood that they could all [be] brought under one principle or derived from one principle. But how was this principle to be found? Could it really be found from particular cases? I tried, but in vain.[^1]

Having made no further progress by this road, I took another: I began examining the subjects for which I had made rules, that is, everything in general. I found in myself activity, the cause of which is I and not-I.[^2] I knew [in] myself the causes; one unclearly, through consciousness, the other clearly, through the senses. Now I began examining [2 illegible] without benefit. There remains one last method—to examine both causes.

I am not limited in combinations with not-I, but am limited by combination itself.—The union of the conscious I with the sensory (to comprehend the sensory).

I am of two parts.

I am only the spiritual.

<!-- vol. 1, p. 227 -->

### [SECOND VARIANT]

Since I can remember myself, I have always found in myself some striving that was not satisfied, but whose satisfaction I understood, though unclearly.

I was conscious that I was limited in everything—and at the same time I understood unlimitedness, even found it in myself.—

To reconcile this contradiction, I made rules by which the manifestation of the unlimited in the limited was determined; but in making these rules I first had to experience evil in order to know how to avoid it.

Finding no satisfaction by this method, I understood that the striving I found in myself arose from the union of the limited with the unlimited—and since I am the unlimited, it was necessary to know how I—the unlimited—must be reconciled with the limited; and in order to know this, it was necessary to know what the unlimited is and what the limited is.[^3]

<!-- vol. 1, p. 228 -->

I rejected everything, that is, all the concepts I had previously accepted as true, and began to seek a principle that would be clear to me immediately, that is, one that would not arise from another concept.—Consequently, the more mediated a concept was, the more obscure it was to me; for example, the concept *tree* was more obscure to me than the concept of the sensation of the smell or sight of that tree.

I had two concepts that required no proofs and that could not be replaced by anything else equally unconditional. I expressed these concepts thus: I am limited and I am active; having these positive concepts in various degrees, I can represent to myself an infinitely small degree of both these concepts, which we call negative, that is, unlimit[edness] and inactiv[ity].

Combining these concepts, I obtain four compound concepts: 1) limit[ed] activity—activity that is very comprehensible and that I find in myself; 2) unlimit[ed] activity; this concept cannot be, for one is opposed to the other: activ[ity] cannot be unlimited; 3) limit[ed] inactiv[ity]—a concept that cannot be, that is, is nothing; 4) unlimit[ed] inactivity.

## Notes

[^1]: Here in the margin: I begin the history of my knowledge from the point up to which I proceeded in the same way as others.—From the very beginning I cast aside all prejudices, finding in them nothing satis[fying].
[^2]: Skepticism—is I.
[^3]: In the margins beside the text of this fragment the author gave something like an outline of it, in this form: I set out the history of my knowledge from the point up to which its course was too simple and the same for everyone. The striving I found in myself was a striving toward well-being or happiness, which I understood only in manifestation; in manifestation, however, it did not satis[fy]. Limitation is not a concept but consciousness: I understand limitation and unlimitedness, but I am limitation and unlimitedness.—Having found many of the existing rules unsatisfactory, I rejected them entirely and accepted it as correct only when I myself made it with those.[?]—Activity is not a concept but consciousness.—The cause of this is activity is a contradiction, because if one of these two principles did not exist, I cannot conceive activity either.—From this conclusion it follows that if there were no limitation, there would be no activity either; conseq[uently] the first consciousness is consciousness of limitation.
