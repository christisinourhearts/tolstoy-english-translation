## **UNPUBLISHED, UNFINISHED** **AND INCOMPLETE**

## \* NOTES ON THE CAUCASUS. A JOURNEY TO MAMAKAY-YURT.

(1852)

In the year 1852, in the month of June, I was staying at the waters of Old Yurt. — The Caucasus was so little known to me that, if I assume in my readers the view I had then, I come decidedly to a dead end and see a complete impossibility of giving an account of what struck me. — I hope that my readers either have no idea at all of the Caucasus, or an idea that is at least somewhat correct; otherwise we shall never understand one another. Once, in childhood or early youth, I read Marlinsky, and of course with delight; I also read, with no less pleasure, Lermontov's Caucasian works. These were all the sources I had for knowing the Caucasus, and I am afraid that most readers may be in the same position as I was. But that was so long ago that I remembered only the ~~poetic~~ feeling I experienced while reading and the poetic images that arose: warlike Circassians, blue-eyed Circassian women, mountains, cliffs, snows, swift torrents, plane trees... the *burka*, dagger, and saber occupied no small place among them. — These images, embellished by memory, arranged themselves in my imagination with extraordinary poetry. Long ago I forgot Marlinsky's and Lermontov's poems, but in my memory other poems were formed from those images, a thousand times more captivating than the first. I did not try to convey them in words, because I knew that it was impossible; but secretly I delighted in them. Have you ever happened to read verses in a language you only half know, especially verses that you know are good? Without entering into the meaning of every phrase, you keep reading, and from certain words that you understand there arises in your head an entirely different meaning, admittedly unclear, misty, and not susceptible of expression in words, but all the more beautiful and poetic for that. The Caucasus was for a long time such a poem for me in <!-- vol. 3, p. 216 --> an unfamiliar language; and when I made out its real meaning [?], in many cases I regretted the invented poem, and in many I became convinced that reality was better than what I had imagined. I shall try to convey the meaning of both poems. — ~~Imagination is always far from the word; but~~ The word is far from being able to convey what is imagined, but to express reality is still more difficult. A faithful rendering of reality is the stumbling block of the word. Perhaps the reader's imagination will make up for the deficiency of the author's expression. — Without this cooperation, how commonplace and colorless all descriptions would be. To place the reader's imagination at the point from which we can understand one another, I shall begin with the fact that there are no Circassians—there are Chechens, Kumyks, Abadzekhs, and so on, but there are no C[ircassians]. There are no plane trees; there is the beech, a familiar R[ussian] tree; there are no blue-eyed Circ[assian women] (even if by the word C[ircassians] one means the collective name of the A[siatic] peoples), and who knows how many other things there are not. You will have to give up many other resonant words and poetic images as well if you read my stories.[^1] I should like new images to arise for you, as they did for me, in place of those that have perished—images closer to reality and no less poetic.

The waters[^2] of Old Yurt, as regards their surroundings, bear very little resemblance to the waters of Baden-Baden or Ems, or even Pyatigorsk. — They are situated beyond the Terek, in Greater Chechnya, near one of the largest and richest peaceful *auls*—Old Yurt. — In general it is difficult to determine whether the territory occupied by Chechnya[^3] beyond the Terek line is peaceful or non-peaceful. Only peaceful Tatars and soldiers live there, in *auls* and fortresses, but outside the fortresses you have as much chance of meeting peaceful as non-peaceful inhabitants. — Therefore, outside the fortresses the place is neither peaceful nor non-peaceful, that is, it is dangerous. For the season, two companies of infantry and two guns are assigned to the waters to protect the camp, and wounded officers, soldiers, and ladies gather in the camp. This year there were three of the latter: the caretaker's wife, with curls, in a pink dress; Ensign Khrinev's wife, the foremost beauty and the most terrible coquette; and the apothecary's wife, a German woman. —

These ladies were, as far as I heard, very worthy and respectable ladies, but the one thing I could not forgive them was that they lived in Chechnya—on the Caucasus—a wild, poetic, and warlike country, in exactly the same way as they would have <!-- vol. 3, p. 217 --> lived in the city of Saratov[^4] or Oryol. Jasmine pomade is a beautiful thing, and prunella boots too, and a parasol too; but somehow they do not go with my ideas of the Caucasus. — Some officers were also dissatisfied with the ladies, but for entirely different reasons. They said that it was absolutely impossible to have the singers sing their best songs; one had no freedom at all. Lieutenant Chikin, in order to express his displeasure, even walked past the dugout of the chief aristocratic lady with the curls without his lower clothing. I forgot to say that the society of the ladies was divided into aristocratic, middle, and bad. You never get away from that anywhere.
