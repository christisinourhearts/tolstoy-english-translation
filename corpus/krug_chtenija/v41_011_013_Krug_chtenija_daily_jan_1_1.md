<!-- vol. 41, p. 11 -->

1 January

It is better to know a little that is truly good and necessary than a great deal that is mediocre and unnecessary.

1

> What enormous wealth there can be in a small, carefully chosen library. The company of the wisest and worthiest people, chosen from all the civilized countries of the world over the course of thousands of years, has given us here, in the best order, the results of its study and wisdom. The people themselves are hidden and inaccessible; perhaps they would be impatient if we disturbed their solitude and interrupted their work; perhaps social conditions would make communication with them impossible. Yet the thought they did not reveal even to their best friends is written here in clear words for us, strangers from another age. Yes, we owe to good books the greatest spiritual benefits of our lives.

> *Emerson.*

2

> We belong to the ruminant breed, and it is not enough merely to stuff ourselves with a multitude of books: unless we chew over and thoroughly digest everything we have swallowed, books will give us neither strength nor nourishment.

> *Locke.*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 12 -->

3

> Beware that reading many writers and every kind of book does not produce confusion and uncertainty in your head. You should nourish your mind only with writers of unquestionable worth if you wish to gain anything useful. Too many books distract the mind. Therefore read only books acknowledged to be unquestionably good. If you ever feel the desire to turn for a time to another kind of writing, never forget to return again to the former ones.

> *Seneca.*

4

> Read the best books first, or you may never get a chance to read them at all.

> *Thoreau.*

5

> One should read only when the source of one's own thoughts has run dry, something that often happens even to the cleverest person. But to frighten away one's own still-unformed thought for the sake of a book is to commit a crime against the spirit.

> *Schopenhauer.*

6

> In literature the same thing happens as in life. Wherever you turn, you run into the incorrigible rabble of humanity—its name is legion—swarming everywhere and fouling everything, like summer flies. Hence the multiplication of bad books, this extraordinary crop of literary weeds that choke the good grain. Such books steal from the public the time, money, and attention that by rights ought to go only to select works.
>
> Bad books are not only useless but positively harmful. After all, nine-tenths of current literature is printed only to coax a couple of extra thalers out of the pockets of the trusting public; for the same purpose authors, publishers, and printers deliberately make books thicker.
>
> An even more harmful, brazen, and shameless fraud is committed by writers paid by the line: charging a groschen for each line of their concoctions, <!-- vol. 41, p. 13 --> these day laborers pervert the reader's taste and destroy true enlightenment.
>
> To oppose this evil, one must unlearn the habit of reading; in other words, one should not read at all the books that occupy public attention or make a stir. Put simply, one must spit out all those publications whose first year of existence will also be their last.
>
> It must be added, however, that whoever writes for fools will always find a wide circle of readers; meanwhile, humanity ought to use its brief and sparingly allotted existence to become acquainted with the first-rate masters of all ages and peoples, with the richly gifted creators who rise like towers above the multitude of bad writers. Only writers of this kind are capable of *educating and instructing*.
>
> You can never read too few bad books, and you can never manage to read too many good ones. Bad books are a moral poison that dulls the mind.
>
> Because the crowd stubbornly insists on reading not the best books of all times but only the newest works of contemporary literature, today's scribblers revolve in the narrow circle of the same repeated ideas, all repeating the same thing, and our age never climbs out of its own filth.

> *Schopenhauer.*

*The difference between material and mental poisons is that most material poisons are unpleasant to the taste, while mental poisons, in the form of newspapers and bad books, are unfortunately often attractive.*
