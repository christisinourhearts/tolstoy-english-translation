<!-- vol. 41, p. 53 -->

23 January

Of all sins, only one — anger at one’s brother — is directly opposed to the chief good of human life, the good of love, and therefore there is no sin more certain to deprive a person of life’s highest good.

<!-- vol. 41, p. 54 -->

1

The Roman sage Seneca said that, in order to restrain oneself from anger, the best remedy is, when one feels anger rising, to freeze and do nothing: not walk, not move, not speak. If the body and tongue are given free rein, anger will flare up more and more.

It is also good, Seneca says, in order to unlearn anger, to observe other people when they are angry. When you look at other people and see what they are like in anger, when you see that they become like drunkards, like beasts, with red, angry, ugly faces, with an unpleasant hoarse voice, when you hear their foul language, remember not to become just as repulsive yourself.

2

Often people yield to anger and do not restrain it because they think there is a kind of bravado in anger. “I didn’t let him get away with it; I really tore into him,” and so on. But this is false. To avoid yielding to anger, one must remember that there is and can be nothing good in anger, that anger is a sign of weakness, not strength.

When a person who is angry fights or strikes someone weak, a child or a woman, even when he strikes a dog or a horse, he shows not strength but weakness.

3

However harmful anger is to other people, it is most harmful to the person who is angry.

It is always more harmful than the offense that caused it.

4

> One can understand why a self-seeking and miserly person wrongs others: he wants to take possession of their property in order to enrich himself; he harms people for his own gain. But an angry person harms others without any benefit to himself; more than that: in harming others, he harms himself.
>
> *According to Socrates*

<!-- vol. 41, p. 55 -->

5

> He whose malice knows no bounds — he who is entwined by it like dodder — will soon bring himself to the place where only his worst enemy would wish to thrust him.
>
> *Buddhist Wisdom. Dhammapada.*

6

> Your enemy will repay you with evil, a hater will repay you painfully, but incomparably more evil will be brought upon you by the anger in your heart.
>
> Neither father nor mother nor relatives nor those close to you will do you as much good as your heart when it forgives and forgets an offense.
>
> *Buddhist Wisdom. Dhammapada.*

7

*Never consider your anger at people just, and never call or consider any person lost or worthless.*

8

A person is angry only because he does not know what caused the thing he is angry about. For if he knew, he would be angry not at the consequence but at the cause. But the external cause of every phenomenon is so remote that it cannot be found; while the internal cause is always you yourself.

9

Why are we so glad to accuse, and why do we accuse so maliciously and unjustly? Because accusing others removes responsibility from us. It seems to us that things are bad for us not because we are bad, but because others are to blame.

When people argue maliciously with one another, a child does not sort out who is right and who is guilty, but sadly runs away from such people, condemning both, and he is always more right than either of the disputants.
