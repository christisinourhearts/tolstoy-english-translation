91. To N. A. Nekrasov. 1854. December 19. Eski-Orda.

Most Gracious Sir

Nikolai Alexeyevich,

Either my letters or yours, or both, are not arriving — otherwise I cannot explain your six months of silence; and meanwhile there is very much I should be interested to learn from you. I have also not received *Sovremennik* since August. 1 —

Have *Notes of a Billiard-Marker* been printed, and when will *Boyhood*[^1][^2] be printed, and why am I not receiving *Sovremennik*? Please inform me of this, and by registered letter so that it will be more certain.[^3]

My address is still the same: Kishinev, Headquarters of the Southern Army. I should especially like now to set my mind at rest about these two things — that is, read them in print and forget them — so that I may occupy myself with finishing new pieces which I hope to place in our journal and for which I have an enormous amount of material. Material of contemporary, <!-- vol. 59, p. 287 --> military content, collected and prepared not for your Journal but for the *Soldiers' Sheet*, of the attempt to found which with the Southern Army you may perhaps have heard in Petersburg. To my project the Sovereign Emperor most graciously deigned to permit us to print our articles in the *Invalid*![^4] Awaiting your speedy reply, with perfect respect I have the honor to be,

your obedient servant,

Count L. Tolstoy.

19 December 1854.

## Notes

[^1]: The story “Notes of a Billiard-Marker,” i.e. *Notes of a Billiard-Marker*, was printed in *Sovremennik*, 1855, no. 1.
[^2]: Tolstoy sent the manuscript of *Boyhood* to Nekrasov from Bucharest on 27 April with a letter unknown to us. Having received the manuscript and letter, Nekrasov wrote to Tolstoy, but this letter reached Tolstoy only on 24 August, as recorded in the diary. This letter is also unknown to us. Tolstoy wrote to Nekrasov again on 23 June 1854 (from Bucharest), but this letter too is unknown. On 10 July 1854 Nekrasov wrote Tolstoy about *Boyhood*: “If I say that I cannot find an expression sufficient to praise your latest piece, that seems to me the truest thing I can say; and it is somehow awkward to say more to you in a letter. The pen, like the tongue, has the property of shyness — I understood this just now, because I cannot manage, even incidentally, to say some of all that I think. I shall choose only this: that the talent of the author of *Boyhood* is original and sympathetic in the highest degree, and that such things as the description of the summer road and storm, or the sitting in the jail cell, and much, much else, will give this story a long life in our literature. I shall print *Boyhood* in book IX or X of *Sovremennik*. I do not know whether you will receive it — but it was sent to you in Bucharest.” (Ibid., pp. 189–190.)
[^3]: In reply Nekrasov wrote on 17 January 1855: “Your *Boyhood* came out in October 1854. (The censorship plucked it pretty thoroughly, striking out much of the first manifestations of love in the adolescent and something where the narrator speaks of his father) and it produced what is called an effect, that is, some talk in Petersburg. As for the literary circle, all decent people unanimously found this piece full of poetry, original, and artistically accomplished. Since I am writing to you about your *Boyhood* for the third time already, you will excuse me for the angularity of these phrases, and besides I am forever in a hurry — even now I have been prevented from finishing the letter. My friends Turgenev and Annenkov are as delighted with this work as I am. (This was added an hour later.)”
[^4]: In reply to Prince M. D. Gorchakov's request concerning the journal (see note 3 to letter no. 84), the Minister of War wrote on 7 November: “His Majesty, doing full justice to the well-intentioned aim with which it was proposed to publish the said journal, was pleased to consider it inconvenient to authorize its publication, since all articles concerning the military actions of our troops, before being placed in journals and newspapers, are first printed in the newspaper *Russky Invalid* and only then borrowed from it by other periodicals.” At the same time Nicholas I permitted the initiators of the journal to send their articles to *Russky Invalid* (a military-political newspaper), something they had the right to do even without this permission.
