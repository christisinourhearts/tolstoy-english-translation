1851. April 30. Moscow.

5. On this 30th day of April 1851, I, Count Lev Nikolaev son of Tolstoy, collegiate registrar and landowner of Krapivna district, have forever set free my serf household girl Maria, daughter of Ivan, who came to me by division with my brothers, Count Nikolai, artillery lieutenant, and Sergei and Dmitry, provincial secretaries, and my sister Maria Nikolaevna Tolstaya, a major's wife, and who is entered in the 9th revision for Krapivna district at my village of Yasnaya Polyana; neither I nor my heirs henceforth have any claim upon her or any right to interfere with her. Her identifying features are: about eighteen years old, gray eyes, dark-blond hair, nose and mouth of medium size, round-faced. She is free to live and choose whatever way of life she wishes, and for this purpose this deed of emancipation has been issued to her, Ivanova, under my signature and in the presence of the witnesses who also signed it. Collegiate secretary Count Lev Nikolaev son of Tolstoy; witness to this deed of emancipation was Major and Cavalier Count Alexei Nikolaev son of Tolstoy.[^1]

### Editorial notes

1. On 12 June 1851 Maria Ivanova, a serf girl freed by collegiate registrar Count Lev Nikolaev Tolstoy, presented this deed of emancipation with a petition to the Tula Civil Chamber and asked that it be confirmed and returned to her against receipt after the Chamber had collected the information required for the case. On 27 June 1853 the matter was reported and it was resolved, in accordance with supplement 3 to volume IX, article 1059 of the 1842 edition, to confirm the deed of emancipation presented by the girl Maria Ivanova, to make a notation upon it that it had been exhibited, and, after recording it in the register of deeds, to issue it against receipt to the girl Maria Ivanova. On 8 February 1854 it was signed by Assessor Milyaev, Secretary Afanasyev, Dmitrevsky for the clerk, and bore the seal of the Chamber. The deed was written on a sheet bearing a ninety-kopeck silver stamp duty.

2. The original deed of emancipation was received by the girl Maria Ivanova, who signed for it.

3. Printed from the *Register of Deeds of the Tula Chamber of Civil Court for Recording Deeds of Emancipation in 1854* (folios 5 and 6), held in the Tula Branch of the Moscow Regional Archival Administration.

4. <!-- vol. 59, p. 339 --> The first paragraph of the serf deed published here in type is a copy, certified in the legal manner for deeds, of the original deed of emancipation signed by Tolstoy on 30 April 1851 and handed by him to the Yasnaya Polyana peasant woman Maria Ivanova. As is clear from the deed, on 12 June of that same year the freed woman presented the original deed of emancipation with a petition to the Tula Civil Chamber for confirmation. After the text of the deed had been entered in the register and confirmed according to the procedure indicated in the deed, the original was returned to Maria Ivanova on 8 February 1854.

5. While living in Moscow in 1851 (from the beginning of January to the beginning of April), Tolstoy made the following entry in his diary on 27 March: “Marya came for a passport. I feel that I restrained myself from... only out of ~~the fact that~~ shame and because she has pimples on her face. — Therefore mark sensuality.” This entry gives some grounds for thinking that the “Marya” who came to Tolstoy in Moscow for a passport and the “Maria Ivanova” to whom, about a month later, he gave a deed of emancipation were one and the same person.

6. In the last days of April of that same year, 1851, Tolstoy, with his elder brother Nikolai, left Yasnaya Polyana by way of Moscow and Kazan for the Caucasus. On the road from Kazan, in a letter to T. A. Yergolskaya of 8 May 1851 (see letter no. 41), he wrote that they had spent two days in Moscow. On 1 May 1851 in Moscow he and his brother had their daguerreotypes taken in Mazer's studio. (See note 10 to letter no. 41.) Consequently, on the previous day, 30 April, while in Moscow, Tolstoy could have signed Maria Ivanova's deed of emancipation; she had apparently been living in Moscow during the latter half of the winter of 1850–51. How long she lived in Moscow, why she was there, exactly where she lived, and whether Tolstoy had intimate relations with her — we have no exact information on any of these questions.

7. The published deed does not show by whom the signatures under the deed of emancipation—either L. N. Tolstoy's own or that of the witness Count A. N. Tolstoy—were certified. If this is not an error by the copyist who entered the text in the register of deeds, it is unclear how the Civil Chamber could in June 1851 have accepted from Maria Ivanova a deed presented by her bearing uncertified signatures. In that case one must suppose either that, in addition to the deed of emancipation, Tolstoy also gave Maria Ivanova his own petition to the Civil Chamber, under which the signatures had been certified, or that the deed was presented to the Chamber in some other manner.

## Notes

[^1]: Count Alexei Nikolaevich Tolstoy (born 1823) — Lev Nikolaevich Tolstoy's fourth cousin, grandson of Count Stepan Fyodorovich Tolstoy (1756–1809). See the genealogical table in volume 46. Count Alexei Nikolaevich's first cousin Countess Alexandra Grigoryevna Saltykova was married to the Decembrist Pavel Ivanovich Kaloshin (on the Kaloshins see note 10 to letter no. 31).
