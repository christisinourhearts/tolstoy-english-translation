\* 105. To T. A. Yergolskaya. 1855. August 4. Sevastopol.

Dear Aunt!

Today, the 4th, there was a great battle. I was there and took very little part in it.[^1] I am safe and sound. But morally I have never been worse; the affair went badly. A dreadful day: our best generals and officers almost all wounded or killed. I am sending this letter with a courier who is leaving this very moment. I believe we shall undertake nothing for a long time now. — Farewell, dear Aunt, I kiss your hands a thousand times. How much I would have given to be with you now.

Yours, Léon.

On the envelope:

To Her High Nobility Tatyana Alexandrovna Yergolskaya.

In Tula.

Dear Auntie!

Today, the 4th, there was a great battle. I was there, but took little part.[^2] I am alive and well, but in my state of mind I have never felt worse; the battle was lost. A terrible day: nearly all our best generals and officers are wounded or killed. I am sending this letter with a courier who is leaving now. I think we shall now <!-- vol. 59, p. 326 --> undertake nothing for a long time. — Farewell, dear Auntie, I kiss your hands a thousand times. How much I would give to be with you now.

Yours, Lev.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the ATB. Published here for the first time. Dated from its contents, the postal postmarks: “Moscow, 1855, August 10” and “Received 1855, August 12,” and T. A. Yergolskaya’s reply of 23 August 1855, which we reproduce in full:

2. “I cannot express how happy I was on receiving your letter of 4 August, my dear Léon. I wept for joy at the sight of your handwriting and on learning that you were safe after that terrible battle. First of all, I sent for the priest, and he served a thanksgiving prayer service. I could not have expected that you would be in this battle; after all, in your last letter to Valerian you wrote that you were stationed near Bakhchisarai, and I became calm, knowing that it was far from Sevastopol; and suddenly you returned there again. How can one be calm when one knows that the city is being bombarded, that bombs fly day and night, and day and night I think of you and pray that the Almighty preserve your precious life. And my prayer will be heard, because I send it up from the depths of my heart. — This last letter of 4 August I received only on the 13th and with a Moscow postmark; evidently the courier to whom you handed it forgot to deliver it to the Tula post office, since it is marked Moscow. But through your loving care, my good Léon, I did receive your letter before I read in the newspapers about this bloody affair. Ah! how many losses! Thank God that Prince Gorchakov remained alive. Surely he was in great danger when a general was killed beside him. So many generals and officers killed, so much grief, so many tears; one shudders at the thought of it! My God, when will there be an end to this slaughter! Without hesitation I would give several years of my life for you to return unharmed. But when we shall live to see this happiness, God alone knows; everything is in His supreme will. I sent your letter to Marie, Valerian, and Nikolenka, who is with them: they will be glad of it, glad to know that you are safe. All our neighbors take a lively interest in you, and in general everyone wishes you success in the service. God grant that their wishes come true. Mlle Vergani and the Arseniev family are especially interested in you. Poor little Valeria has been ill ever since she caught cold at the ball at the Vauxhall and still cannot recover. She is a very pretty and very interesting girl. It is a pity she is often ill. And now farewell, dear, adored Léon. I call down God’s blessing upon you, and may He preserve you. I kiss you tenderly. Yours with all my heart. Aunt E.” (Original in French; published here for the first time; autograph in the ATB.)

3. Tolstoy’s letter to Count V. P. Tolstoy has not survived. The Vauxhall was the name of a building in Moscow, in Petrovsky Park, where performances, concerts, and balls were held.

## Notes

[^1]: On 4 August 1855, at the Chernaya River, the famous attack was made by Russian troops under the command of Prince M. D. Gorchakov. By 7 in the morning, when the battle appeared to be over (the Allies held the left bank of the Chernaya River, the Russians the right), Prince Gorchakov decided to renew the battle and lost it. On our side 8,000 men were lost; of these, 3 generals, 69 officers, and 2,273 lower ranks were killed; 4 generals, 160 officers, and 3,995 lower ranks were wounded. Among those killed was Adjutant General Baron P. A. Vrevsky, struck down by a cannonball a few paces from the commander-in-chief.
