\* 79. To T. A. Yergolskaya. 1853. Second half of August. Pyatigorsk.

Dear Aunt!

It happens fairly often to me that for several weeks at a time I receive no letters. At present I am in just such a position; I am expecting letters from every quarter, and letters that are to decide my fate, that is, decide whether I may return to Russia or not, and you can judge my impatience. But your silence pains me still more. I do not know why our correspondence, which gave me so much pleasure, has for some time become slower and less regular. If my letters depended on my affection for you, on the contrary they ought to have become more frequent than before, since never have I felt so strongly how much I love you and desire to be near you as now, since I have some hope of seeing my dearest plans realized. —

What shall I tell you about my service? It is too disagreeable a subject for me to take pleasure in speaking to you about it. It would be too long and too tedious to explain all the disagreeable delays that are happening to me; I shall tell you only that I have had quite enough of them and that all I want is to be able to leave the service as soon as possible. —

My health is good, and that consoles me when I see Marie's illness, which until now has yielded very little. Last year I had exactly the same illness as she, and it was the waters that restored me completely. I therefore hope they will do her the same good. —

My literary occupations are going badly, or, to put it better, are not going at all. This state of uncertainty, fear, and hope in which I find myself disturbs too much the evenness of mind indispensable for working fruitfully and with pleasure. —

Nicolas is on the point of leaving; Valerian and Marie will not be long in following him, which is to be desired, for if they remain it will only be because of the bad state of Marie's health. I shall remain completely alone, which will be all the more painful to me after the family life I have led these two months. The consolations your excellent letters give me will be more necessary than ever. They remind me so well of the rare happiness I have <!-- vol. 59, p. 245 --> in having in you a true friend to whom I can always confide my sorrows and my joys.

I kiss your hands, dear Aunt.

Léon.

On page 4:

To Her High Nobility Tatyana Alexandrovna Yergolskaya. —

Dear Aunt!

It happens fairly often to me that for several weeks in a row I receive no letters. I am now in such a position; from every side I am waiting for letters that must decide my fate, that is, decide whether I can return to Russia or not, and you can judge my impatience. But your silence grieves me still more. I do not know why there has lately been a break in our correspondence, which I enjoyed so much; it has become less regular. If my letters depended on my attachment to you, I would write much more often, because never have I been drawn to you so strongly as now, since the hope appeared that what I desire so ardently may come true. —

What shall I tell you about my service? I do not want to speak of what is unpleasant. It is both tedious and long to describe all the unpleasant delays; I shall tell you only that I am tired of all this and want to leave the service as soon as possible. —

My health is good, and this reassures me about Mashenka, whose illness is not yielding to treatment. Last year I was ill with the same thing, and the waters restored me completely. I hope they will help her as well. —

My literary occupations are progressing badly or, better said, are not progressing at all. The state of uncertainty, fears, and hope in which I find myself disturbs the evenness and calm of spirit necessary for working successfully and willingly.

Nikolenka is leaving; Valerian and Mashenka will soon be getting ready to follow him, which I desire; if they stay, that will mean Mashenka is worse. And I shall remain alone, which will be especially painful to me after two months of family life. More than ever I shall await consolation from your wonderful letters, which constantly remind me what a rare happiness it is to have such a friend, to whom I can confide both sorrows and joys.

I kiss your dear hands, dear Aunt.

Lev.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the ATB. Published here for the first time. The letter is dated by its contents: “Nikolenka is on the point of leaving. Valerian and Mashenka will soon be getting ready to follow him.” Count N. N. Tolstoy left Pyatigorsk for Tula Province on 1 September, and Count Valerian Petrovich and Maria Nikolaevna Tolstoy left at the beginning of October.
