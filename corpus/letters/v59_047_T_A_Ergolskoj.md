\* 47. To T. A. Yergolskaya. 1851. June 24. Stary Yurt.

24 June.

The soldier who carried my letter of the 22nd to the post[^1] has just brought me yours,[^2] dear aunt, in which you say that I meant to reproach you for your indifference. — Dear aunt! How can you believe that I, for whom your tender affection and the firm confidence I have in your love are a point of support for me in all the difficult moments of life, could have the idea of reproaching you for indifference? No, you are unjust! You know, good aunt, that if I have faults, frankness is not what I lack. Well, I shall tell you with that frankness that your letter made me shed tears. Why have these sad thoughts? — You say that you no longer hope to see Nicolas again. — Why believe that? Let us hope in God. In four or five months we shall all be together again at *Yasnoye*, and those sweet conversations will begin again. — You are too necessary to our happiness for God not to preserve you. —

I have made the firm decision to remain in service in the Caucasus. I have not yet decided whether it will be in military or civil service under Prince Vorontsov;[^3] my journey to Tiflis[^4] will decide the matter. Tell Valerian and Marie[^5] that I give them my piano and ask them not to thank me, because there is no merit in giving away something one does not need. Please send it to them. — Please have Arsenyev[^6] told that the news of his recovery gave me much pleasure and that I embrace him tenderly despite his pimpled face. —

*I send my regards to my faithful and good servants Gasha[^7] and Prov.[^8]* — Goodbye, dear aunt, I kiss your hands a thousand times. —

Leon.

On the fourth page:

To Her High Nobility Tatyana Alexandrovna Yergolskaya. Tula.

24 June.

The soldier who carried my letter of the 22nd to the post[^9] has just brought me your[^10] letter, dear auntie, in which you say that I wanted to reproach you for indifference. — Dear auntie, how could you think that I, for whom your tender affection and firm confidence in your love are a support in all the difficult moments of my life, could have meant to reproach you for indifference? No, you are unjust! You know, good auntie, that with all my faults there is no secretiveness in me. And I confess to you frankly that I wept over your letter. Why do you have such sad thoughts? — You say that you no longer hope to see Nikolenka again. — But why? God willing, in four or five months we shall all gather again at *Yasnoye*, and our peaceful conversations will begin again. — You are so necessary to our common happiness that God will preserve you. —

I have firmly decided to remain in service in the Caucasus. I do not yet know whether in military service or civil service under Prince Vorontsov;[^11] that will be decided by my journey to Tiflis.[^12] Tell Valerian and Mashenka[^13] that I give them my piano and ask them not to thank me for it, since there is no merit in giving away what one does not need. Please send it to them. — Have Arsenyev[^14] told that I am glad he is getting better and that I kiss him tenderly despite his spotted face. —

*I send my regards to my faithful and good servants Gasha[^15] and Prov.[^16].* — Goodbye, dear auntie, I kiss your hands a thousand times. —

Lev.
