*23. To T. A. Yergolskaya. Late December 1849. Tula.*

Dear Aunt,

On the evening of the very day you left, when I returned to Yasnoye I learned of your departure, [^1] which caused me a great deal of pain; all the more because Gasha [^2] tells me that you were very angry with me, <!-- vol. 59, p. 52 --> and, what grieved me still more, very sad.

—I could have excused myself: I could have told you that the dinner at which I had promised to be present kept me there the day before, and that the next day, having got up late, I stayed to dine with Arseniev, [^3] always thinking I would still find you if you had not left in the morning.

Although these are valid reasons, I prefer to appeal to your kindness, to admit frankly that I was inconsistent and bad to have caused you grief, and to ask your pardon.

I would have followed you the same day if I did not have a few fairly serious matters keeping me here. Tell Marie and Valerian that I kiss them very tenderly and that I would like nothing better than to be godfather to their future child; but since I cannot and do not wish to be present at Marie's delivery, I ask only that I be informed as soon as she has given birth, and within twelve hours I will be there.—How is she?

Goodbye, dear Aunt, I kiss your hands and ask you not to be angry with me any longer. Have you any news of Aunt Pauline?

I am sending the calendar to Aunt Lise; I congratulate her and kiss her hands.—

Thank you for your gift, which is of great use to me—the dressing gown.—

On the envelope:

To Her High Born Tatyana Alexandrovna Yergolskaya. At Pokrovskoye.

### Editorial notes

1. Dear auntie. On the evening of the very day you left, [^4] I returned to *Yasnoye* and was deeply upset to learn that you had gone, all the more because Gasha [^5] says that you were very angry with me and, what saddened me still more, were very sad.—I could have said in excuse that the day before I was kept by a dinner at which I had promised to be present, and that the next day I got up late and stayed to dine with Arseniev, [^6] still thinking I would find you if you had not left in the morning.

2. But although these reasons are sufficient, I still prefer to appeal directly to your kindness, frankly admitting that in causing you distress I acted inconsistently and badly, and asking you to forgive me.

3. I would have gone after you if I were not being kept here by a few serious matters. Tell Mashenka and Valerian that I kiss them tenderly and that I would gladly be godfather to their future child; but since I cannot and do not wish to be present at Mashenka's delivery, let them inform me when she has given birth, and I will be there in twelve hours. How is she?—

4. <!-- vol. 59, p. 53 --> Goodbye, dear auntie, I kiss your hands and ask you to stop being angry with me. Have you any news of Aunt Pauline? [^7]

5. I am sending the calendar to Aunt Lise, [^8] I congratulate her and kiss her hands.—

6. Thank you for the gift—the dressing gown. I need it very much.

7. Printed from the autograph held in the ATB. Published here for the first time. Dated from the content: Countess M. N. Tolstaya's delivery was approaching. On 8 January 1850 she gave birth to a daughter, Varvara.

## Notes

[^1]: T. A. Yergolskaya left Yasnaya Polyana for the Tolstoys at Pokrovskoye in order to be present at the delivery of Countess Maria Nikolaevna Tolstaya.
[^2]: Agafya Mikhailovna (1812–1896) had been maid to Tolstoy's grandmother, Countess Pelageya Nikolaevna, and then served the Tolstoy family in various capacities and lived her entire life on the Yasnaya Polyana estate. In the last years of her life she received a pension from Tolstoy. Although she had been beautiful in her youth, she remained unmarried. Agafya Mikhailovna was intelligent in an original way and was a gifted storyteller. She had a fondness for animals, and for a time she was in charge of the sheep and later of the hunting dogs. Tolstoy portrayed her in *Childhood* and *Boyhood* as the maid Gasha. She is depicted in *A Landowner's Diary* and in *Anna Karenina*. On her, see Ilya Tolstoy, *My Reminiscences*; T. A. Kuzminskaya, *My Life at Home and at Yasnaya Polyana*; T. L. Sukhotina-Tolstaya, *Friends and Guests of Yasnaya Polyana*. Moscow, 1923.
[^3]: Vladimir Mikhailovich Arseniev (1810–1853), a lieutenant of the Guards and a Tula landowner, owned the Sudakovo estate, seven versts from Yasnaya Polyana, and was married to Evgenia Lvovna Shcherbatova (1808–1856). After V. M. Arseniev's death, Tolstoy was appointed guardian of the surviving orphans: Valeria (1836–1909), Olga (1838–1867), later Princess Engalycheva, Evgenia (1845–1909), later Liprandi, and Nikolai (1846–1907).
