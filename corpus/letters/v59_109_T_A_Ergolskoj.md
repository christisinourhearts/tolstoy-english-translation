\* 109. To T. A. Yergolskaya. 1855. September 4. Sevastopol.

Dear and excellent Aunt!

On the 27th there was a great and glorious affair at Sevastopol.[^1] I had the good or bad fortune to arrive in the city on the very day of the assault; so that I was present at the affair and even took some part in it as a volunteer.[^2] Do not be frightened, I was hardly in any danger. The 28th, my birthday, was for the second time in my life a memorable and sad day for me: the first time, 18 years ago, it was the death of Aunt Alexandrine,[^3] and now the loss of Sevastopol. I wept when I saw the city in flames and the French flags on our bastions, and in general it was in many respects a very sad day. Valentin Kaloshin, whom I had grown very fond of here, is missing, and no one knows what has become of him.[^4] I am not writing to his parents because I still hope that he has been taken prisoner. The note I sent to the enemy camp has not yet received an answer. I cannot explain to myself, dear Aunt, the silence of all those to whom I never cease writing: you, Valerian, Marie, Nicolas, Serge. During these last days the idea of leaving the army comes to me more and more. I see that it would be easy for me; but in order to take this step, I should like to have your approval. And this, besides the desire to give you pleasure by putting your mind at rest about me, is the principal object of this short and ill-assorted letter.[^5] Farewell, dear Aunt, good and excellent Aunt, a thousand and a thousand times I kiss your hands, and I swear to you that I never cease thinking of you.

Your Léon Tolstoi.

4 September 1855.

<!-- vol. 59, p. 335 -->

Dear and wonderful Auntie!

On the 27th a great and important action took place at Sevastopol. I had the good and bad fortune to arrive in the city on the very day of the assault; so I was present at it and even took some part, as a volunteer. Do not be frightened: I was exposed to almost no danger. The 28th, my birthday, was for the second time in my life a memorable and sad day for me: the first time, 18 years ago, it was the death of Aunt Alexandra Ilyinichna; now — the loss of Sevastopol. I wept when I saw the city enveloped in flames and the French flags on our bastions; and in general, in many respects, it was a very sad day. Valentin Koloshin, whom I became very fond of here, is missing. I have not written to his parents because I still hope that he was taken prisoner. No answer has yet come to the inquiry I sent to the enemy camp. I cannot explain to myself, dear Auntie, the silence of everyone to whom I constantly write: you, Valerian, Masha, Nikolenka, Seryozha. During these last days the thought of giving up the army altogether comes into my head more and more often and insistently. I see that it would be easy for me to do this, but in order to decide on this step I should like to have your approval. And this, besides the desire to give you pleasure by putting your mind at rest about me, is the chief purpose of this short and *incongruous* letter.[^6] Farewell, dear Auntie, kind and priceless Auntie! A thousand and a thousand times I kiss your hands — believe me that I never cease thinking of you.

Yours, Lev Tolstoy.

4 September 1855.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the ATB. First published by N. G. Molostvov and P. A. Sergeenko in their book *L. Tolstoy. A Biographical Sketch*, St Petersburg, 1911, issue 3, p. 142.

2. Postal postmarks: “Received 1855 Sept. 21 Bakhchisarai” and “Tula Oct. 1 1855.” On the envelope, in an unknown hand, the word Tula is crossed out and “Chern” is written, together with “Letter of Count Lev Nik. Tolstoy from Sevastopol, from the war.”

## Notes

[^1]: On 27 August 1855 the Allies made their final assault on Sevastopol, after which the Russian troops abandoned all the fortifications and withdrew to the North Side, to which the last units crossed on 28 August, sinking the Black Sea Fleet.
[^2]: Tolstoy left Sevastopol for Simferopol on 25 August and returned on the 27th. Colonel P. N. Glebov wrote of his participation in the battle: “...Count Tolstoy, an artillery lieutenant; he commands two mountain guns, but himself wanders about wherever he pleases.”
[^3]: Tolstoy makes two strange mistakes here. Tolstoy's aunt, Countess Alexandra Ilyinichna Osten-Sacken, née Countess Tolstaya, died on 30 August 1841, that is, not 18 years before but 14, and not on Tolstoy's birthday.
[^4]: Valentin Pavlovich Koloshin (born 183?, died 27 August 1855), younger brother of Dmitry and Sergei Koloshin, on whom see notes 10 and 15 to letter no. 31. He served together with Tolstoy in Sevastopol, and Lev Nikolaevich was very friendly with him.
[^5]: This letter of Tolstoy's was, one must suppose, read by Turgenev, who wrote to Tolstoy on 9 October from Pokrovskoye, the estate of Countess M. N. Tolstaya.
[^6]: This letter of Tolstoy's was, one must suppose, read by Turgenev, who wrote to Tolstoy on 9 October from Pokrovskoye, the estate of Countess M. N. Tolstaya.
