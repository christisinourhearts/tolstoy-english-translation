\* 95. To Count N. N. Tolstoy. 1855. February 3. Position on the Belbek River.

3 February.

After my wild letter in verse and prose[^1] I do not know how to begin (like Mount Athos, as Nikolai Sergeyevich says)[^2] to write to you; all the more because in this letter I must make a sad confession. I have lost at cards all the money — 1,500 silver rubles — that was sent to me. Please, neither in letters nor in my absence, do not accuse or reproach me. I myself do not cease and shall not cease reproaching myself for this terrible stupidity until I redeem it by my own labor. From my letters you surely already know what I needed this money for and how the proposed journal failed. How I lost it is not worth describing — stupidly, groundlessly, childishly.[^3] —

In the first days of November I left Kishinev for Sevastopol, where I had asked to go as one of the artillery officers who were lacking, and I have remained in the Crimea to this day, although at first I wrote to you through Kishinev so as not to frighten the aunts. But now, first, Sevastopol has ceased to be so frightening and, second, Alyoshka gave me away with a letter whose rough draft fell into my hands for a certain use and in which he writes that we live in the Crimea and three lands have come to us by sea, namely: Turk, Frenchman, and Englishman, and that there is a strong battle with them. This is well put, though not entirely accurate, because for three months now there has been no battle at all except the continuing siege. And even that proceeds <!-- vol. 59, p. 300 --> sluggishly. Every day about 15 casualties, continual rifle fire in the advanced trenches, bombs thrown from both sides, mine explosions, deserters and prisoners — more, however, from their side than ours. Occasionally night sorties in which they stab one another with bayonets and bite; that is all. Sevastopol cannot be taken; the enemy is more convinced of this than we are, and so these useless cruelties have become hateful both to the enemy, who materially is hardly in a better position than in the year '12, and to us. “When, brothers, will this damned Sevastopol finally go away!” the soldiers say. —

From the beginning of my arrival I was attached to a battery in Sevastopol itself and spent almost a month on the bastions; then I spent a month near Simferopol (the battery was resting),[^4] played pianos, danced, hunted goats and deer, and saw pro—[^5]

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the ATB. Published here for the first time.

2. Of this letter Tolstoy wrote in his diary under 2 February: “I must write Nikolenka a letter confessing the gambling loss.”

## Notes

[^1]: This letter has not survived.
[^2]: On Nikolai Sergeyevich Voeikov see note 5 to letter no. 7.
[^3]: Of this gambling loss Tolstoy wrote in his diary under 28 January: “Played shtoss for two days and two nights. The result is understandable — the loss of everything — the Yasnaya Polyana house. It seems there is nothing to write — I am so disgusting to myself that I should like to forget my existence.”
[^4]: Tolstoy is unquestionably mistaken here. Having arrived from Kishinev in Sevastopol on 7 November 1854, Tolstoy, as his diary shows, already left on 15 November for the position at the village of Eski-Orda, 6 versts from Simferopol (under 23 November he wrote: “On the 15th I left Sevastopol for the position”). He remained there until the middle of January. By an order of 5 January 1855 for the artillery of the Southern Army, Tolstoy was attached to Light Battery No. 3 of the 11th Artillery Brigade. He was officially sent to this battery, which was camped on the Belbek River 20 versts from Sevastopol, on 14 January, but probably left somewhat later: see letter no. 94, still marked Simferopol.
[^5]: The end of the letter has not survived.
