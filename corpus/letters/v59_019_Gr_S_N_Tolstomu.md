*19. To Count S. N. Tolstoy. 1 May 1849. Petersburg.*

Read this letter alone.

Seryozha.

I think you are already saying that I am the most worthless fellow; and you are telling the truth.—God knows what I have done!—I went to Petersburg for no reason at all, did nothing useful there, only spent a vast amount of money and got into debt. Stupid.—Unbearably stupid.—You would not believe how it torments me.—Above all the debts, which I must pay and as quickly as possible, because if I do not pay them soon, then besides money I will lose my reputation too.—For God's sake, do this: without telling the aunts and Andrei why or what for, sell Vorotynka [^1] to Uvarov [^2] or Seleznev. [^3] It is my last resource, but one I cannot do without, both in order to live and in order to pay the Board of Guardians. Until the new income I absolutely need 3,500 silver rubles—1,200 to the Board of Guardians; 1,600 to pay debts; 700 for living expenses.—I know you will gasp, but what is to be done? One does foolish things once in a lifetime. I had to pay for my freedom and philosophy, and now I have paid for them.

There was no one to flog me. That is the chief misfortune.

Please take the trouble to get me out of the false and nasty position I am in now—without a kopeck and owing money all around.—As quickly as possible I need 800 silver rubles here and, in Moscow, as I wrote Andrei, 300 and another 150. For the forest and horses they will surely receive 1,400; all of it must be sent out—450 to Moscow and the rest here.—

Vorotynka can be sold soon; I think there are no obstacles. Obolensky [^4] says that in the Shcherbachevka re-mortgage the entire delay is in the powers of attorney: there is neither yours, nor mine, nor Mashenka's; [^5] I sent mine to Mitenka [^6] long ago. What is it you do not understand about our being quits over the debts? [^7] It means this: why should I have taken the horses from you for nothing? That was not in the division, and even if it had been, it would have been unjust; that is why I wrote to you that with the sale of the horses we will be quits for the 1,500 silver rubles that you owed me <!-- vol. 59, p. 45 --> under the division... You gave me horses worth, surely, if not more, 1,500 silver rubles. And the reason I wrote this to you and did not say it earlier is that it would have been awkward for both you and me to talk about it. I do not know why, but it would have been awkward...

You surely know that all our troops are going on campaign, and that part of them (2 corps) have crossed the border and, they say, are already in Vienna. I had begun taking the examination for Candidate and passed 2—well; [^8] but now I have changed my intention and want to enter the Horse Guards Regiment as a junker. I am ashamed to write this to you because I know that you love me, and all my foolishness and groundlessness distress you. I even got up several times and blushed over this letter, as you too will do while reading it; but what can be done? The past cannot be changed, and the future depends on me.—

God willing, I will reform and someday become a decent man; most of all I place my hopes in service as a junker. It will accustom me to practical life, and *nolens volens* [^9] I will have to serve until I reach officer's rank.—With luck, that is, if the Guards see action, I may be promoted even before the two-year term; the Guards are going on campaign at the end of May. I can do nothing now because, first, I have no money, of which I do not need much (everything is government issue), and second, 2) the metrical certificates are at Yasnaya; have them sent as quickly as possible. Please do not be angry with me. I already feel my worthlessness too much now, and carry out my requests quickly. Goodbye; do not show this letter to Auntie, I do not want to upset her.

## Notes

[^1]: Vorotynka, or more correctly Malaya Vorotynka, an estate in Bogoroditsk district of Tula province with 22 souls, fell to Tolstoy in the division of 1847.
[^2]: We cannot say who Uvarov was.
[^3]: Dmitry Stepanovich Seleznev (1807–188?) was a Tula landowner who bought up land. In 1879 he owned 5,400 desyatinas of hereditary land in Tula province and 3,200 desyatinas purchased in various provinces.
[^4]: Prince Dmitry Alexandrovich Obolensky. On him, see note 7 to letter no. 12.
[^5]: Countess Maria Nikolaevna Tolstaya.
[^6]: Count Dmitry Nikolaevich Tolstoy.
[^7]: On the debt to Count S. N. Tolstoy, see note 3 to letter no. 14.
[^8]: The manifesto on assisting the Austrian government in suppressing the Hungarian uprising was issued on 28 April 1849. At the same time a detachment under F. S. Panyutin (four infantry regiments and one artillery brigade, not two corps as Tolstoy writes) was moved from Krakow toward Vienna. The rumor reported by Tolstoy that the detachment was “already in Vienna” was incorrect. The detachment stopped at Hradisch (between Olmütz and Göding).
[^9]: In the article “Upbringing and Education,” written in 1862, Tolstoy recalled: “In 48 *Lev Nikolaevich misremembered: it was unquestionably in April 1849* I took the Candidate examination at Petersburg University and literally knew nothing and literally began preparing a week before the examinations. I did not sleep at night and received Candidate marks in civil and criminal law, preparing for each subject no more than a week.” Löwenfeld, from Tolstoy's words, wrote that Lev Nikolaevich “patiently passed two examinations in criminal law” (F. Löwenfeld, *Count L. N. Tolstoy: His Life, Works and Worldview*, translated from German, 2nd ed., Moscow, 1904, p. 39). In the letter of 11 May Tolstoy writes that he took one examination. Probably there was an examination in two subjects on one day. Professor of criminal and police laws at Petersburg University from 1835 to 1856 was Yakov Ivanovich Barshev (1807–1892); professor of Russian civil laws from 1843 to 1855 was Konstantin Alexeyevich Nevolin (1806–1855).
