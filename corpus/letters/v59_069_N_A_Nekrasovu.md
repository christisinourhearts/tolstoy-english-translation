69. To N. A. Nekrasov (unsent). 1852. November 18. Starogladkovskaya stanitsa.

1852.

Dear Sir!

With extreme displeasure I read in no. IX of *Sovremennik* a story under the title *The Story of My Childhood* and recognized in it the novel *Childhood* which I sent you.[^1] As the first condition of publication I stipulated that you should first value the manuscript and send me what it was worth, in your opinion. This condition was not fulfilled. As the second condition—that nothing in it be changed. This condition was fulfilled still less: you changed everything, beginning with the title. Having read this wretched, mutilated story with the saddest feeling, I tried to discover the reasons that prompted the editors to treat it so mercilessly. Either the editors set themselves the task of mutilating this novel as badly as possible, or, without supervision, entrusted its proofreading to a completely illiterate Contributor. — The title *Childhood* and a few words of preface explained the idea of the work; the title *The Story of My Childhood*, on the other hand, contradicts the idea of the work. What does anyone care about the story of my childhood... 2 The portrait of my mama instead of the little icon of my angel on the first page is such a change that it would make every decent reader throw down the book without reading further.[^2] There is neither possibility nor need to enumerate all changes of this kind; but without speaking of countless senseless trimmings of phrases, misprints, incorrectly rearranged punctuation marks, bad spelling, unsuccessful changes of words—*to breathe* instead of *dvoshat* (about dogs),[^3] “fell to the ground in tears” instead of “collapsed” (a beast falls that way),[^4] which prove ignorance of the language—I shall point out one change incomprehensible to me. Why was the whole story of Natalya Savishna's love omitted, a story that depicted her, the way of life of old times, and gave importance and humanity to this person? “She even suppressed her love for the waiter Foka.” Here is the senseless phrase replacing that passage.[^5] The word *delire* in Mimi's note was translated as “ardor.”[^6] The cast-iron plate which the watchman strikes <!-- vol. 59, p. 212 --> was replaced by a copper one. Incomprehensible.[^7] — I shall say only that, reading my work in print, I experienced the unpleasant feeling a father experiences at the sight of his beloved son hideously and unevenly shorn by a self-taught barber. “Where did these bald patches and cowlicks come from, when before he was a pretty boy?” But my child was not very beautiful to begin with, and now they have cropped and mutilated him still more. — I console myself only with the fact that I have the possibility of printing the whole novel separately under my own surname and completely disowning the story *The Story of My Childhood*, which in all justice belongs not to me but to an unknown contributor to your editorial office.

I have the honor to be, Dear Sir, Your most obedient servant, L. N.

### Editorial notes

1. Printed from the autograph held in the ATB. First printed by M. A. Tsyavlovsky in the journal *Pechat i revolyutsiya*, Moscow, 1928, book 6, pp. 91—92.

2. Not having received *Sovremennik* with *Childhood* in due time (see N. A. Nekrasov's letter of October 30, 1852, in the note to letter no. 66), Tolstoy read it only at the end of October. In the diary under October 31 he wrote: “Read my story, mutilated to the extreme.” Under November 8: “Wrote a letter to the editor, which calmed me, but which was not sent.” This version has not survived. Under November 17 again: “Once more wrote letters to Dyakov and the Editor, which again I shall not send. To the Editor too harsh.”

3. Since this letter was not sent to Nekrasov, unfortunately we do not have the latter's answer to the series of accusations made against him by the beginning author. Nor has the manuscript of *Childhood* that was sent by Tolstoy and from which, probably, the story was typeset for *Sovremennik* come down to us. The absence of the manuscript prevents us from judging how greatly the text sent by Tolstoy was distorted in the journal. According to him, the *Sovremennik* text contained “countless senseless trimmings of phrases, misprints, incorrectly rearranged punctuation marks, bad spelling, unsuccessful changes of words.” Four years later (in the first days of October 1856) Tolstoy's book *Childhood and Boyhood*, published by the author himself, appeared. Comparing the text of *Childhood* in this edition with the text in *Sovremennik*, we must regard Tolstoy's judgment of the journal text as too severe. Thus, for example, the 1856 edition does not reveal in *Sovremennik* “countless senseless trimmings of phrases,” and there are not so many misprints in *Sovremennik* either. The variant readings between the text of the 1856 edition and the *Sovremennik* text are given by us in volume 1.

## Notes

[^1]: Tolstoy is dissatisfied with the subtitle “Story” that was given in the journal, but, apart from the fact that Tolstoy himself calls *Childhood* a “story” in his diary and letters, the designation “novel” is also absent from the 1856 edition.
[^2]: Nekrasov was not responsible for the genuinely foolish substitution of “the portrait of my mama” for “the little icon of my angel”: this substitution was made at the demand of the censorship.
[^3]: In chapter XIV (the fourth paragraph) *Sovremennik* printed: “Several borzoi dogs—some breathed heavily while lying in the sun...” Tolstoy indicates that his manuscript had not “breathed” but “*dvoshali*,” but “breathed” also stands in the 1856 edition: evidently Tolstoy himself abandoned the provincialism “*dvoshali*.”
[^4]: The words “fell to the ground in tears” occur in chapter XII (the third paragraph from the end). In the 1856 edition it reads, as Tolstoy demanded, “collapsed.”
[^5]: It is possible that the replacement in chapter XIII of the second half of the first paragraph by the phrase “she even suppressed in her heart her love for the young waiter Foka” was caused by censorship requirements.
[^6]: Tolstoy's remark that the word *delire* in Mimi's note was translated as “ardor” indicates that in the manuscript sent by Tolstoy the text of Mimi's note was in French. Nekrasov, however, gave only the Russian translation. Only the Russian text is given in the 1856 edition too, but there it reads “in delirium,” as Tolstoy demanded.
[^7]: The “cast-iron” plate (chapter XII, fifth paragraph), rather than the “copper” one, was unlucky. “Copper” remained in the 1856 edition (and in all subsequent ones), and only in the present edition have we introduced this conjectural reading on the basis of the Tolstoy letter printed here. See volume 1, p. 34.
