<!-- vol. 46, p. 11 -->

21 March.

Chapter X sets out the fundamental rules and [^1] the most dangerous errors concerning Criminal procedure.—

At the beginning of this chapter she asks herself a question. Where do punishments come from, and where does the right to punish come from? To the first question she answers: “punishments arise from the necessity of preserving the laws.”—To the second she likewise answers very ingeniously. She says: “the right to punish belongs to the laws alone, [^2] and only the Monarch can make laws, as the representative of the whole State.”—Throughout this *Instruction* two heterogeneous elements are constantly presented to us which Catherine constantly wished to reconcile: namely, an awareness of the necessity of constitutional government and self-love, i.e., the desire to be [^3] the unlimited ruler of Russia. For example, in saying that under Monarchical government only the Monarch can possess legislative power, she takes the existence of this power as an axiom, without [^4] mentioning [^5] its origin.—A subordinate government cannot impose punishments, because it is a part of the whole, while the Monarch has this right because he is the representative of all citizens, says Catherine.

<!-- vol. 46, p. 12 --> But is the representation [^6] of the people by the Sovereign in unlimited Monarchies an expression of the aggregate of the private, free wills of the Citizens? No, the expression of the general will in unlimited Monarchies is the following: I endure the lesser evil, because if I did not endure it I would expose myself to a greater evil.—

The second question consists in what means are needed for keeping [^7] an accused person under guard and for discovering a crime? In resolving the first part of this question she says that keeping an accused person under guard is a punishment preceding [^8] conviction. Catherine [^9] felt the falsity of this idea and the injustice of this custom and tried to justify it by saying that every accused person necessarily [^10] must be guilty. [^11] But why does the man who [^12] is a hundred times guiltier than the accused, yet has not been accused because he has no enemies, not suffer [^13] equal punishment? In my opinion, keeping an accused person under guard can never be justified; for it is the highest injustice: to subject the innocent [^14] and the criminal to the same punishments, while distinguishing between rich and poor, because the rich can easily find sureties for themselves, while the poor rarely can.—In this same chapter there occurs a completely Republican thought. Namely, she says that judgments in cases ought to be public, so that citizens [^15] may be conscious of their [^16] security under the protection of the laws.—Can the security of citizens under the protection of the laws exist where not only judicial decisions but the laws themselves are changed at the whim of the autocrat?—

Then noteworthy is her proposal that already accused criminals should be allowed <!-- vol. 46, p. 13 --> to exonerate themselves.—This provision, [^17] although it has the lofty moral aim [^18] of giving a criminal every possible means of exonerating himself, cannot be accepted in positive legislation, because it would lead to great abuses.—A criminal, by presenting false evidence, would merely try to postpone the date of his sentence. *Fides testis eo minoris est ponderis, quo gravius est maleficium, et res id circumstantes minorem prae se ferunt verisimilitudinem* [^19]—she says. The answer to Question III, whether torture violates justice and whether it is contrary to the aim of the laws, would be ordinary in our century; but [^20] amid the crude ideas of that time it was the work of Catherine's great mind and elevated feelings.—Torture in general was used either to force an accused person to confess his crime, or to learn from him about his accomplices, or to extract from him his former crimes, or to explain contradictions he had given under interrogation. Of the first application of torture she says that torture, having as its aim to distinguish the guilty from the innocent, on the contrary effaces every distinction that existed between them (in the eyes of the judges). Of the second she says that one who has already accused himself will without difficulty accuse others as well, and that it is just not to punish one man for the crimes of others. Of the third she says that torture of this kind would be punishment for the fact that a man could have been still worse.—Of the fourth kind of torture she says that in an interrogation both the guilty and the innocent become confused in their words. All the arguments are extraordinarily ingenious.—Question IV consists in this: [^21] Ought punishments to correspond to crimes? Among other sound observations on this subject she says: *Quamquam leges non possunt punire consilium seu propositum, dici tamen nequit, actionem, per quam crimen incipit existere, quae que* <!-- vol. 46, p. 14 --> *revelat voluntatem criminis patrandi, non mereri poenam, quamvis minorem constituta ad puniendum crimen iam re ipsa perpetratum.* [^22]—
