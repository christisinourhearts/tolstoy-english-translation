<!-- vol. 46, p. 14 -->

22 March.

In my opinion, intention, since it is an action of the soul that has not expressed itself outwardly, can never be contrary to juridical law, for it is not subject to it. All actions of the soul are subject to nothing except the will, and the will is an unlimited faculty. Although it is said that deeds proving a criminal intention are subject to punishment, even these deeds should not be punished, for such deeds in themselves cause no harm.—And an intention, since it is subject to the influence of a person's will, can change from an evil intention into a good one before it is carried out. The strongest manifestation of a person's conscience usually occurs before the commission of some evil.—Further Catherine says that when criminals gather together, they usually strive to equalize among themselves the danger of committing the crime, and in order that this danger should not be equal, the perpetrators of the crime are assigned a greater punishment than its accomplices. Just.—But her observation that if the perpetrator of a crime receives some definite payment for it, the punishment should be equal for all, cannot be applied in [^1] positive legislation, although it is morally just. In fact, considered from the point of view of morality, people who by payment induce a person to commit a crime are more criminal than those who merely form the design of the crime. And the person [^2] who agrees for payment to commit a crime is less criminal than one who, from the mere desire to commit a crime, resolves upon it.

<!-- vol. 46, p. 15 --> Considered from the positive point of view, however, we shall find that [^3] every punishment must be proportionate to the benefit brought by the crime.—I do not approve [^4] her opinion [^5] that [^6] it should be determined by law that a criminal who informs on his accomplices should not be punished, despite all the advantages such a provision might bring, because, first, a person who has betrayed his word is not virtuous, and government should support virtue; and second, because under no circumstances should there be injustice in the laws [^7] (not to punish a criminal [^8] is injustice), and in my opinion positive law, in order to be perfect, must be identical with moral law.—
