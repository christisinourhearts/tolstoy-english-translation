1850, 8 December.

[Moscow].—For 5 days I wrote a diary, and for 5 months I did not take it in hand.—I will try to remember what I did during this time and why I have apparently fallen so far behind in my occupations.—A great change has taken place in me during this time; the quiet life in the country, my former foolishness, and the necessity of attending to my own affairs have borne fruit.—I have stopped building castles in Spain and making plans for whose execution no human strength would suffice.—The chief and most favorable thing in this change of convictions is that I no longer hope to arrive at anything by my reason alone and no longer despise forms accepted by everyone. Formerly everything ordinary seemed unworthy of me; now, on the contrary, I recognize almost no [^1] conviction as good and just until I see its application and execution in practice, and its application by many. Strange—how could I have neglected what constitutes the chief advantage of a human being—the faculty [^2] of understanding the convictions of others and seeing in others their execution in practice. How could I have let my reason run its course without any verification, without any application?—In one word, and the simplest one, I have got over my wildness and grown older.—My self-love contributed greatly to this change.—Having embarked on a dissolute life, I [^3] noticed that people who stood below me in everything were much above me in this sphere; it hurt me, and I became convinced that this was not my calling. Perhaps two shocks also contributed to this. The first [^4] was the loss to Ogarev, which brought my [^5] affairs into complete disorder, so that it even seemed there was no hope of putting them right; [^6] and after this the fire, which involuntarily forced me to act. Winning back the loss gave these <!-- vol. 46, p. 39 --> actions a more cheerful color.—One thing seems to me: I have already become too cold. Only occasionally, especially when I lie down to sleep, moments come over me when feeling asks to come out; likewise in moments of drunkenness; but I have given myself my word not to get drunk. I will not continue my Notes now, because I am occupied with affairs in Moscow; but if there is free time, I will write a story of Gypsy life.—I noticed [^7] another important change in myself: I have become more self-confident, i.e. I have stopped becoming embarrassed; I suppose this is because I have one aim in view (interest), and in striving toward it I have been able to appraise myself and have acquired a consciousness of my own worth, which makes relations among people so much easier.—

Rules for gambling in Moscow until 1 January.

1) The money I have in my pocket I may risk over one or several evenings. 2) Play only with people whose fortune is greater than mine. 3) Play on my own, but do not back another player's stake.—4) Regard the sum I have set myself to lose as winnings when there is three [^9] times that amount over it [^8], i.e. if I have set myself to lose 100 rubles, and if I win 300, then count 100 as winnings and do not let it be played back; but if I am lucky enough to keep winning, then likewise count as winnings the same sum that I had intended to lose only when I have won three times as much, and so on to infinity. With regard to gambling sessions, keep the following calculation: if I have won one winning amount, designate it for loss; if I have won double, then use this sum twice, and so on. If after a win there is a loss, then subtract the sum lost and divide the remainder of the last winnings over two sessions; divide the next winnings over three. [^10] Begin play by dividing the sum I have set aside into some equal [^11] parts. I have now divided 300 silver rubles into three.

Notes. Count as a session, of course, when you yourself stop and lose or win the amount set.—Before every <!-- vol. 46, p. 40 --> session recall everything written and do not lose sight of it; therefore do not sit down from one session straight into another without making the calculation at leisure. I may change these rules after acquiring more experience, but until I write new ones I must follow these. After thinking it over, I may make exceptions to these rules when I am ahead by 9 thousand silver rubles and 29 silver rubles.—

Rules relating to the process of play itself.—Always deal the cards myself; (in *palki*) [^12] calculate [^13] the coup: (20—and 40) in 120 [^14] 20—10—80.—After three lost coups, if they reach 3 times the average number—settle accounts. When winning, raise as much as possible. Always keep an approximate calculation of the result of the game in mind.—Rules for society. Choose difficult situations; try always to command the conversation; speak loudly, calmly, and distinctly; try to begin and end the conversation myself.—Seek the society of people who stand higher in society than I do.—With people of this kind, before seeing them, prepare myself as to what relations I am to have with them. Do not be embarrassed to speak in the presence of outsiders. Do not constantly switch the conversation from French to Russian and from Russian to French. [^15] Remember that one must force oneself, especially at first, when [^16] one is in company in which one feels constrained.—At a ball invite the most important ladies to dance.—If I become embarrassed, do not lose myself, but continue. Be as cold as possible and show no impression whatever.
